Thesis in short

A good life does not come from chasing positive experiences and avoiding pain, but from choosing a few good values worth suffering for. Pain, problems, and failure are unavoidable, so the work is to take total responsibility for how you interpret and respond to what happens, to doubt your own beliefs, to fail and keep going, to say no to everything that is not what matters, and to keep your own death in view so what truly matters stays in perspective. When you give better fucks, about better values, you get better problems, and a better life.

Overall summary

Manson argues against the standard self-help pitch that life should be happier, healthier, and more positive all the time. That pitch, he says, only reminds you of what you lack, and chasing positive feeling reinforces the sense that you lack it. The alternative is not indifference. It is choosing carefully what to care about, because care is limited and you are always choosing, whether you admit it or not. The book moves through nine chapters, each built around stories: Bukowski and the feedback loop from hell, the Buddha and Disappointment Panda, an entitled acquaintance and Manson's own teenage expulsion, a Japanese soldier who fought on for 29 years after the war ended, William James and Malala, a button experiment and a self-help stalker, Picasso and a child learning to walk, Russian frankness and Romeo and Juliet, and the death of Manson's friend Josh at nineteen. The argument is practical, not systematic: pick better values, take responsibility, doubt yourself, fail forward, say no, commit, and remember you will die. The tone is blunt, profane, and anecdotal throughout. Manson says the book is not a guide to greatness, which he calls an illusion, but a guide to suffering better.

Central thesis

Happiness is not a solvable equation, and it is not the absence of problems. Life is an endless series of problems, and the solution to one problem is merely the creation of the next. The secret is in the solving, not in not having problems. So the interesting question is not what you want out of life, since everybody wants to be happy, fall in love, look good, and make money, but what pain you are willing to struggle for. Who you are is defined by what you are willing to struggle for, and the joy is in the climb itself. Values decide what problems you get. Bad values, pleasure, material success, always being right, staying positive, dominance, being liked by everybody, create poor problems because they are not reality-based, not socially constructive, and not within your control. Good values, honesty, vulnerability, standing up for yourself and others, self-respect, curiosity, charity, humility, creativity, are reality-based, socially constructive, and immediate and controllable. Self-improvement is really about prioritizing better values. Around that core, Manson adds three disciplines. First, responsibility is not fault: fault is past tense, responsibility is present tense, and you are always responsible for how you interpret and respond, even when you did not cause what happened. Second, certainty is the enemy of growth: beliefs are hypotheses, actions are experiments, and growth means going from wrong to slightly less wrong, so ask what if I am wrong, what would it mean, and would being wrong create a better or worse problem. Third, death is the compass: without death everything would feel inconsequential, and confronting mortality strips away fragile, superficial values and forces the question of legacy, what the world will be different and better for when you are gone.

Chapter 1. Don't Try, pp. 8-25

Manson opens with Charles Bukowski, alcoholic, womanizer, chronic gambler, lout, deadbeat, and on his worst days a poet, the last person anyone would look to for life advice, and therefore the perfect place to start. Bukowski wanted to be a writer and collected decades of rejection; he worked as a letter-filer at the post office, drank, gambled at the racetrack, and hammered poetry on a beat-up typewriter. At fifty, an editor at a small independent publishing house took a chance on him, and he wrote his first novel, Post Office, in three weeks. He went on to publish six novels and hundreds of poems and sell over two million copies, and his tombstone reads Don't try. His success came not from determination to be a winner but from knowing he was a loser, accepting it, and writing honestly about it, especially the worst parts of himself. The point: self-improvement and success often occur together, but they are not the same thing. Modern culture fixates on unrealistically positive expectations, be happier, healthier, the best, smarter, faster, richer, sexier, more popular, and conventional life advice fixates on what you lack, which only reminds you of what you are not. The Feedback Loop from Hell: anxiety about confronting someone makes you anxious about being anxious, which makes you doubly anxious; anger about being angry; guilt about feeling guilty. Social media and consumer culture have made this epidemic: everybody else's feed shows them having a grand old time while you feel terrible, and then you feel bad about feeling bad. Not giving a fuck short-circuits the loop. The crisis is no longer material but existential: we have so much stuff and so many opportunities that we do not know what to give a fuck about anymore. The desire for more positive experience is itself a negative experience, and the acceptance of one's negative experience is itself a positive experience. Alan Watts's backwards law: pursuing something only reinforces that you lack it. The more you want to be rich, the poorer you feel; the more you want to be happy, the lonelier you become. Pursuing the negative generates the positive: gym pain brings health, business failures bring understanding, openness about insecurities brings confidence, honest confrontation brings trust. Everything worthwhile is won through surmounting the associated negative experience, and avoiding suffering is itself a form of suffering. Manson's own moments of not caring, quitting his finance job after six weeks to start an internet business, selling his possessions and moving to South America, define his life. You have a limited amount of fucks to give, and giving a fuck about everything without choice means you get fucked. The subtle art is learning to focus and prioritize thoughts, picking what matters based on finely honed personal values, which takes a lifetime of practice and regular failure, and is perhaps the only struggle in one's life. Giving too many fucks makes you feel entitled to comfort and happiness at all times, so every adversity becomes an injustice. Three subtleties: first, not giving a fuck does not mean being indifferent; it means being comfortable with being different. Indifferent people are couch potatoes and internet trolls hiding behind sarcasm. There is no such thing as not giving a fuck, since biology makes us always care about something; the question is what we choose to care about. Second, to not give a fuck about adversity, you must first give a fuck about something more important than adversity. He illustrates this with an elderly lady screaming at a cashier over a thirty-cent coupon, because coupons are all she has left; people who hand out fucks like ice cream have nothing more fuck-worthy to dedicate them to; when a person has no problems, the mind invents some. Third, whether you realize it or not, you are always choosing what to give a fuck about. Children give too many fucks, and maturity is learning to give a fuck only about what is truly fuckworthy. The book's stated point: to help you think more clearly about what you choose to find important and unimportant, and to lead to practical enlightenment, becoming comfortable with the idea that some suffering is always inevitable, so you become invincible in a low-level spiritual way, since the only way to overcome pain is first to learn to bear it. It is not a guide to greatness, but a guide to suffering better, turning pain into a tool, trauma into power, and problems into slightly better problems.

Chapter 2. Happiness Is a Problem, pp. 26-41

About 2,500 years ago in the Himalayan foothills, a king decided his newborn son would never know a moment of suffering: high walls, every need met, servants catering to every whim. The prince grew up spoiled, ignorant of human cruelty, and angry: every experience felt empty and valueless, nothing ever enough. Sneaking out one night, he saw sickness, old age, homelessness, pain, and death for the first time, came home in existential crisis, and blamed his father for the very things his father had tried to do for him. He ran away, gave up royalty, starved and tortured himself, begged for scraps, then realized suffering without purpose has no value either. Sitting under a tree for forty-nine days, he realized life itself is a form of suffering: the rich suffer because of their riches, the poor because of poverty, people with families because of their families, people who pursue pleasures because of their pleasures. He became the Buddha. The premise that happiness is algorithmic, if I achieve X, then I can be happy, is the problem; happiness is not a solvable equation, and dissatisfaction is a necessary component of consistent happiness. Enter Disappointment Panda, Manson's invented superhero in a cheesy eye mask and too-small shirt, whose superpower is telling people harsh truths they need to hear but do not want to accept, door to door like a Bible salesman. Suffering is biologically useful: nature's preferred agent for inspiring change; we evolved to live with dissatisfaction and insecurity because the mildly dissatisfied creature does the most work to innovate and survive; pain and misery are a feature of human evolution, not a bug. Physical pain is the nervous system's feedback mechanism, and research shows the brain registers little difference between physical and psychological pain; emotional pain signals something out of equilibrium. Problems never go away, they just improve: Warren Buffett has money problems and a drunk homeless man has money problems, but Buffett's are better ones. Life is an endless series of problems, and the solution to one problem is merely the creation of the next; the secret is in the solving, not in not having problems. Two bad responses: denial, deluding and distracting, which feels good short-term and leads to insecurity and repression, and victim mentality, blaming others, which leads to anger, helplessness, and despair. Both are highs, and highs are addictive: almost anything can become addictive depending on motivation. Emotions evolved to help us live and reproduce a little better; they are feedback mechanisms, signposts, suggestions, not commandments. Just because something feels good does not mean it is good, and vice versa; repressing negative emotions denies the feedback that helps solve problems, while overidentifying with them is what three-year-olds and dogs do. Emotions never last, and the hedonic treadmill means whatever makes us happy today will not tomorrow; problems are recursive: the person you marry is the person you fight with, the house you buy is the house you repair. Choose Your Struggle: the interesting question is not what you want out of life, everybody wants to be happy, fall in love, look perfect, make money, but what pain you want in your life, what you are willing to struggle for, because happiness requires struggle and grows from problems. Manson's rock star fantasy illustrates it: for over half his lifetime he fantasized about being a musician on stage, but he was in love with the result, not the process. He did not like practicing, hauling gear, or broken strings; he liked imagining the summit. Who you are is defined by what you are willing to struggle for, and the joy is in the climb itself.

Chapter 3. You Are Not Special, pp. 42-59

Jimmy, a guy with endless business ventures, consulting firms, a medical app, charity events, keynote speeches, a more efficient gas pump, always rolling, always name-dropping, always positive, a real go-getter, was a total deadbeat: all talk, stoned most of the time, spending as much in bars and restaurants as on his business ideas, a professional leech living off his family's money, occasionally making money through sketchy means like selling someone else's idea or finagling loans. The worst part: he believed his own bullshit, and his delusion was so bulletproof it was hard to get mad at him. The self-esteem movement of the 1960s and 1970s, teaching parents, therapists, and schools to raise children's self-esteem through grade inflation, participation awards, and exercises like listing reasons why they were special, produced a generation of Jimmys, because merely feeling good about yourself means nothing without a good reason, and adversity and failure are necessary for developing strong-minded adults. The true measure of self-worth is how a person feels about the negative aspects of herself: Jimmy feels great 99.9 percent of the time despite his life falling apart, so his self-esteem is a sham. Entitlement, feeling you deserve good things without earning them, makes people need to feel good all the time even at others' expense, and once the pattern of construing everything as self-aggrandizing sets in, any reasoned challenge is seen as a threat. Entitlement is a failed strategy, just another high. Manson then tells his own story: at thirteen, pulled out of biology class by assistant principal Mr. Price, who searched his backpack for drugs; he had cut a secret compartment into the bottom to hide marijuana, lied when asked, and was caught anyway, handcuffed in a police car, expelled, homeschooled, his Marilyn Manson and Metallica shirts thrown out. Then his parents divorced, with no broken dishes or screaming, just a logistics question and answer session, after years in a house where horrible things that needed to be said never were. Losing friends, community, legal rights, and family in about nine months, he concluded he had problems he could never solve, which made him feel uniquely special or uniquely defective, entitled. His entitlement played out in relationships: chasing women for validation, becoming a player for a decade, craving to be wanted and loved. There are two forms of entitlement, I am awesome and the rest of you suck, so I deserve special treatment, and I suck and the rest of you are awesome, so I deserve special treatment, opposite on the outside, the same selfish core, and people flip between them. There is no such thing as a personal problem: if you have a problem, millions of others have had it, have it now, and will have it in the future, which does not minimize it, just means you are not special, and realizing that is the first step toward solving it. The Tyranny of Exceptionalism: the internet floods us with the truly extraordinary, the 99.999th percentile, the best of the best and worst of the worst, because extremes get eyeballs, while most of life rides in the humdrum middle; conditioned to believe exceptionalism is normal, we feel insecure and compensate through entitlement. Being average has become the new standard of failure, and it is better to be at the extreme low end than the middle, because at least there you are still special, which is why many choose to prove they are the most miserable or victimized. The rare people who become truly exceptional do so not because they believe they are exceptional, but because they are obsessed with improvement, stemming from the belief that they are not that great at all. Accepting the bland, mundane truths, your actions do not matter much in the grand scheme, most of your life will be boring and not noteworthy, and that is okay, lifts the pressure to be amazing and frees you to appreciate simple friendship, creating something, helping a person in need, reading a good book, laughing with someone you care about.

Chapter 4. The Value of Suffering, pp. 60-81

In December 1944, Second Lieutenant Hiroo Onoda of the Japanese Imperial Army was deployed to Lubang island in the Philippines with orders to slow the United States' progress, stand and fight at all costs, and never surrender, essentially a suicide mission. When the Americans took the island in February 1945 and Japan surrendered that August, Onoda and three men hid in the jungle and kept fighting a guerrilla war. Leaflets announcing the war was over were burned as fakes; a 1952 air-drop of letters and pictures from families, with a personal note from the emperor, was refused as a trick; by 1959 one companion had surrendered and another been killed, and a decade later Kozuka was killed in a shootout while burning rice fields, still waging war a quarter-century after World War II ended. In 1972, Norio Suzuki, a young adventurer who had dropped out of school and hitchhiked across Asia, the Middle East, and Africa, sleeping on park benches and in jail cells, returned to Japan, found it stifling, and set out to find three things: Lieutenant Onoda, a panda bear, and the Abominable Snowman, in that order. He found Onoda in four days by screaming his name and telling him the emperor was worried about him. Onoda had spent more than half his life in the jungle for a phantom war, yet later said he regretted nothing and was proud; Suzuki found Onoda and the panda, then died in the Himalayas searching for the Yeti. Both men chose how they wished to suffer, Onoda for loyalty to a dead empire, Suzuki for adventure, and because their suffering meant something, they could endure it. The question is not how to stop suffering but why you are suffering, for what purpose. Onoda returned to Japan in 1974, became a celebrity, and was horrified by a consumerist, superficial culture that had lost honor and sacrifice; his suffering had meant nothing, so it suddenly became real, thirty wasted years, and in 1980 he moved to Brazil, where he remained until he died. The Self-Awareness Onion has three layers: first, simple understanding of one's emotions, which many people cannot even do; second, asking why you feel them, difficult, often requiring a therapist; third, personal values, why do I consider this success or failure, by what standard am I judging myself? This third layer is the most important, because values determine the nature of problems, and problems determine the quality of lives. Most people answer why questions badly, blaming others and avoiding responsibility. Manson's own example: it bugs him that his brother does not return texts; digging down, the value is brothers are supposed to have a good relationship and the metric is frequency of contact, a poor metric; mutual respect or trust would measure brotherhood better. Problems may be inevitable, but the meaning of each problem is not; we control what our problems mean by how we choose to think about them and measure them. Rock Star Problems: in 1983, guitarist Dave Mustaine was kicked out of Metallica days before recording their first album, handed a bus ticket home; by the time the bus reached Los Angeles he vowed to form a band so successful Metallica would regret it. Megadeth sold over 25 million albums, yet in a 2003 interview he tearfully admitted he still considered himself a failure, because his metric was success relative to Metallica, over 180 million albums. Pete Best, fired by the Beatles in 1962 three days before their first record and replaced by Ringo Starr, fell into depression and attempted suicide in 1968, yet said in 1994 that he was happier than he would have been with the Beatles. Getting kicked out led him to his wife, children, a stable marriage, and a simple life, and he reprioritized and measured his life differently. Shitty values that create poor problems: pleasure, a false god, the easiest to obtain and easiest to lose, a by-product, not a cause, of happiness; material success, once basic needs are met, the correlation with happiness approaches zero; always being right, brains are inefficient machines full of biases, basing self-worth on being right prevents learning; and staying positive, denying negative emotions leads to deeper, prolonged negative emotions and emotional dysfunction, constant positivity is avoidance. The greatest moments are often not pleasant, successful, known, or positive: completing a marathon beats eating a chocolate cake. Defining Good and Bad Values: good values are reality-based, socially constructive, and immediate and controllable, honesty, innovation, vulnerability, standing up for oneself and others, self-respect, curiosity, charity, humility, creativity; bad values are superstitious, socially destructive, and not immediate or controllable, dominance through manipulation, indiscriminate sex, feeling good all the time, always being the center of attention, being liked by everybody, being rich for the sake of being rich. Values are about prioritization, and self-improvement is really about prioritizing better values, choosing better things to give a fuck about, because when you give better fucks, you get better problems, and when you get better problems, you get a better life.

Chapter 5. You Are Always Choosing, pp. 82-101

A gun to your head: run 26.2 miles in under five hours or your family dies, that would suck. Choose to run a marathon, train for months, finish with family cheering, one of the proudest moments of your life. Same miles, same person, same pain, but chosen freely and prepared for, glorious; forced, terrifying. Often the only difference between a problem being painful or powerful is a sense that we chose it and are responsible for it. William James's story: born into a wealthy prominent family with life-threatening health issues from birth, an eye problem that temporarily blinded him, a stomach condition, trouble hearing, back spasms so bad he could not sit or stand. He spent his time at home painting, was ridiculed by his father for laziness, while his brother Henry became a world-renowned novelist. His father got him into Harvard Medical School, where he felt like a fraud; he dropped out, joined an anthropological expedition to the Amazon in the 1860s, contracted smallpox on the first day and nearly died, returned emaciated to New England at nearly thirty, unemployed, a failure, and fell into deep depression, planning suicide. Reading Charles Peirce one night, he decided to conduct an experiment: spend one year believing he was 100 percent responsible for everything that occurred in his life, no matter what, and do everything in his power to change circumstances; if nothing improved, he would take his own life. He became the father of American psychology, his work translated into many languages, taught at Harvard, married with five children, and called the experiment his rebirth. The realization: we are responsible for everything in our lives, no matter the external circumstances. We do not always control what happens, but we always control how we interpret it and how we respond, and choosing not to interpret or respond is still a choice. The Responsibility/Fault Fallacy: with great power comes great responsibility is actually Uncle Ben's line from Spider-Man, and the better version is with great responsibility comes great power. Accepting responsibility for problems is the first step to solving them. Responsibility and fault are not the same: if I hit you with my car, I am both at fault and responsible; but a newborn baby left on your doorstep is not your fault, yet the baby is now your responsibility, and every choice you make about it carries problems you are responsible for. A judge did not commit the crime but is still responsible for it. Fault is past tense; responsibility is present tense. Many people may be to blame for your unhappiness, but nobody is ever responsible for your unhappiness but you, because you always choose how you see things, react, and value them. His first girlfriend cheated on him with her teacher and left him for him; he held her responsible for his misery and got nowhere. She was to blame, but never responsible for how he felt. When he took responsibility, exercising, spending time with neglected friends, meeting new people, a study-abroad trip, volunteer work, he slowly improved, and later recognized his own role: he had not been a great boyfriend, and people do not cheat unless they are unhappy for some reason. Responding to Tragedy: even horrible events do not change the equation. Robbed, you are not at fault but immediately responsible for a life-and-death situation. In 2008 the Taliban took the Swat Valley, banning girls from school; eleven-year-old Malala Yousafzai spoke out, kept attending school, and in 2012, at fourteen, was shot in the face on the bus home after identifying herself to the gunman; she survived, kept speaking out, became a best-selling author, and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2014. It would have been easy to lie down and say she had no choice; that itself would have been her choice. A blog commenter whose son had died in a car accident accused Manson of not knowing true pain and said he was an asshole for suggesting the man was responsible for his pain; Manson did not argue. He chose a better problem, practicing patience and understanding readers better, and replied simply that he was sorry for his loss. Genetics and the Hand We Are Dealt: a 2013 BBC documentary followed teenagers with OCD, Imogen, seventeen, who had to tap every surface or be flooded with thoughts of her family dying; Josh, who had to do everything with both sides of his body or suffer panic attacks; Jack, a germophobe who wore gloves, boiled his water, and refused food he had not prepared. Psychiatrists taught them to accept their compulsive values as irrational and choose more important values, Imogen taking control of her thoughts, Josh a normal social life, Jack leaving the house without trauma, and by the end, Imogen no longer needed to tap every surface, Josh could go twenty-five to thirty minutes without equalizing, and Jack could eat in restaurants and drink from glasses without washing them. Jack's line: he did not choose his condition, but he gets to choose how to live with it. Life is like poker: you are dealt cards, some better than others, but the real game is the choices you make with them; people who consistently make the best choices come out ahead, just as in life. Victimhood Chic: the responsibility/fault fallacy lets people pass off responsibility through blame, which gives a temporary high and moral righteousness; social media made it easier than ever, and public blame and shame has become popular, even cool. Victimhood chic is in style on the right and the left, among rich and poor, and every demographic group feels unfairly victimized simultaneously. Ryan Holiday calls it outrage porn: the media finds something mildly offensive, broadcasts it, generates outrage, then broadcasts that outrage back, distracting everyone from real problems, and the more people proclaim themselves victims over tiny infractions, the harder it is to see the real victims. Tim Kreider's line in the New York Times: outrage feels good but devours us from the inside out. We should pick battles carefully, empathize with the so-called enemy, and prioritize honesty, transparency, and doubt over being right, feeling good, or getting revenge. There Is No How: people ask how to change, and his answer, in a Yoda impersonation, is do, or do not; there is no how. You are already choosing what to give a fuck about every moment, so change is as simple as choosing to give a fuck about something else, simple, but really hard, with side effects of uncertainty and feeling like a failure.

Chapter 6. You're Wrong About Everything (But So Am I), pp. 102-121

Five hundred years ago cartographers believed California was an island, doctors believed bleeding a person could cure disease, scientists believed fire was made of phlogiston, and astronomers believed the sun revolved around the earth, and in five hundred years people will laugh at our certainties too. Growth is iterative: we do not go from wrong to right, but from wrong to slightly less wrong; values are hypotheses, actions are experiments, and emotions and thought patterns are data. Many people become so obsessed with being right about their life that they never actually live it, the lonely woman who never goes out, the man who never asks for the promotion, because testing their beliefs would force them to confront them; it is easier to sit in a painful certainty than to test it. Certainty is the enemy of growth; nothing is certain until it has already happened, and even then it is debatable. Architects of Our Own Beliefs: in the button experiment, a person in a room with buttons is told that doing some undefined something will make a light flash for a point; people mash buttons until the light comes on, then invent elaborate sequences, standing on one foot, pressing buttons in a specific order, to reproduce it, but the points are random; the mind is a meaning machine that invents patterns, and once it creates meaning it is biased to hold on to it. Most of what we know is the product of the brain's inaccuracies and biases, so most beliefs are wrong, or, more exactly, all beliefs are wrong, some just less wrong than others. The Dangers of Pure Certainty: Erin, a self-improvement junkie he met at a seminar in 2008, slept with once, who then invited him to move in, threatened suicide when he tried to break it off, created fake accounts and a website impersonating him, and after a near-death car accident became convinced she could cure death and that they were destined to save the world together, embodying every self-help lesson to a T, yet with values so fucked that none of it matters, because her certainty refuses to relinquish itself. Roy Baumeister's research found the opposite of the expected result: the worst criminals often felt pretty damn good about themselves, and feeling justified lets people hurt others, racists certain about genetic superiority, religious fanatics certain of heaven as martyrs, men who rape out of certainty they are entitled to women's bodies. Evil people never believe they are evil; they believe everyone else is. The Stanford Prison Experiment showed normal people escalating to physical abuse when given moral righteousness. The backwards law applies again: the more you try to be certain, the more uncertain and insecure you feel; embracing uncertainty makes you comfortable with what you do not know. Manson's Law of Avoidance: the more something threatens your identity, the more you will avoid it. An artist friend talked for years about launching his work online but never did, because becoming an artist nobody likes was scarier than remaining an artist nobody has heard of; a party-guy friend never gave up drinking and chasing girls, because giving up the lifestyle would threaten his identity as the Party Guy. We protect the values that define us, even when they hurt us. Kill Yourself: Buddhism argues the idea of who you are is an arbitrary mental construction and you should let go of it; letting go frees you to act, fail, and grow. The woman who admits she is not good at relationships is suddenly free to end a bad marriage. Do not find yourself; never know who you are, because that is what keeps you striving and discovering, and forces humility. The belief that your problems are uniquely special is narcissism, pure and simple. How to Be a Little Less Certain of Yourself: three questions, what if I am wrong; what would it mean if I were wrong; would being wrong create a better or a worse problem than my current problem, for both myself and others? The friend's brother who admonishes his sister about her engagement is the example: if he is wrong, the only explanation is his own insecurities and fucked-up values. If it feels like it is you versus the world, chances are it is really just you versus yourself.

Chapter 7. Failure Is the Way Forward, pp. 122-135

Manson graduated college in 2007, just in time for the financial collapse and Great Recession; a roommate vanished owing three months' rent, he lived on a friend's couch for six months, counted coins for McDonald's, sent out twenty resumes without a reply, and considers himself fortunate, because he entered the adult world already a failure, at rock bottom, so starting a blog and an internet business had nothing to lose. His values were freedom and autonomy, not money, so not pursuing his own projects would have been the real failure. The Failure/Success Paradox: Picasso, as an old man in a cafe in Spain, doodled on a napkin, and when a woman asked to buy it, said twenty thousand dollars. It took him over sixty years to draw this. Improvement at anything is based on thousands of tiny failures, and if someone is better than you at something, likely because she has failed at it more than you have; a child learning to walk falls hundreds of times and never thinks walking just is not for him. Avoiding failure is learned later, from an education system that punishes poor performance and overbearing parents. Shitty values are outcome-based and outside your control, make everyone I meet like me, so failure is defined by others' actions; better values are process-oriented, express myself honestly to others, and never finished. Conventional goals like buying a lake house or losing fifteen pounds are limited in the happiness they produce and make poor guides for a life's trajectory; Picasso's underlying value was simple, humble, and endless: honest expression. Pain Is Part of the Process: in the 1950s, Polish psychologist Kazimierz Dabrowski studied World War II survivors and found a sizable percentage believed their wartime suffering, though painful and traumatic, made them better, more responsible, happier people, more confident, grateful, unfazed by trivialities. He argued fear, anxiety, and sadness are not necessarily undesirable but the necessary pain of psychological growth; just as physical pain builds stronger bone and muscle, emotional pain builds resilience, a stronger sense of self, compassion, and happiness. The most radical changes in perspective happen at the tail end of our worst moments, hitting bottom, which he calls weathering the shitstorm, and pain is part of the process; chasing highs to cover it up never generates the motivation to actually change. The Do Something Principle: his high school math teacher Mr. Packwood said that if you are stuck on a problem, do not sit and think about it, just start working on it, and the right ideas will show up. When he quit his day job after six weeks in 2008 to pursue an online business and spent weeks playing computer games in terror, that advice became his mantra. Action is not just the effect of motivation; it is also the cause of it. The chain runs Inspiration, Motivation, Action, and then loops, but you can enter it at action. Tim Ferriss's story of a novelist who wrote over seventy novels by forcing himself to write two hundred crappy words a day illustrates it: the act of writing inspired him, and thousands of words followed. When acting itself becomes the metric for success, even failure pushes you forward.

Chapter 8. The Importance of Saying No, pp. 136-157

In 2009 Manson sold or stored all his possessions, left his apartment, and set off for Latin America, planning years as a digital nomad, a dating advice blog earning a modest living, lower cost of living abroad, fifty-five countries over five years, but beneath the adventure was a screwed-up value: commitment issues and shame left over from his teenage years, overcompensating for inadequacy by being able to meet, befriend, and love anybody. Now he lives in New York with a house, furniture, an electric bill, and a wife, and likes it that way, because the biggest lesson of all his adventuring was that absolute freedom, by itself, means nothing; freedom grants the opportunity for greater meaning, but meaning comes only through a rejection of alternatives, a narrowing of freedom, a choice of commitment to one place, one belief, or one person. In 2011 he traveled to Saint Petersburg, Russia: food, weather, apartment, prices, and people all awful, yet one of his favorite trips, because of Russian frankness: no fake niceties, no verbal webs of politeness; if something is stupid, you say it is stupid. His Russian teacher's theory: under communism, with little economic opportunity and fear everywhere, the most valuable currency was trust, and to build trust you had to be honest; in the free West, with abundant economic opportunity, it became more valuable to present yourself a certain way, even falsely, than to actually be that way, so people smile and lie and agree when they do not. Rejection Makes Your Life Better: to value something, you must reject what is not that something. To value X, reject non-X; rejection is inherent and necessary to maintaining values and identity, and avoiding rejection at all costs is a deep form of entitlement. Boundaries: Romeo and Juliet, a boy and a girl from families that hate each other, married the next day, a potion, a misunderstanding, two suicides, is synonymous with romance today, yet Shakespeare likely wrote it as a satire, a neon sign saying keep out. For most of human history romantic love was seen as unnecessary and dangerous; today we get brain boners for batshit crazy love, as if it were cocaine. It stimulates the same parts of the brain, gets you high, and creates as many problems as it solves. Unhealthy love is two people trying to escape their problems through each other; healthy love is two people acknowledging and addressing their own problems with each other's support. The difference comes down to how well each person accepts responsibility and how willing each is to both reject and be rejected by their partner. Poor boundaries examples: you cannot go out with your friends without me; I cannot believe you made me feel so stupid in front of my own sister; I would love to take that job in Milwaukee, but my mother would never forgive me. Entitled people either expect others to take responsibility for their problems or take on too much responsibility for others', the victim and the saver, drawn to each other, each using the other to achieve emotional highs, and passing on emotionally healthy partners because solid boundaries do not feel exciting enough. Acts of love are valid only if performed without conditions or expectations. The litmus test is if I refused, how would the relationship change? How to Build Trust: his wife spends an hour on makeup, hair, and clothes, asks how she looks, and when she looks bad he tells her. Honesty matters more than feeling good all the time, and she calls him out on his bullshit too, which makes him a better person. Without conflict there can be no trust; conflict shows who is there for you unconditionally and who is just there for the benefits. Cheating destroys trust, not sex, and rebuilding it requires the cheater to peel away the self-awareness onion and show his values have changed, plus a consistent track record of improved behavior; trust is like a china plate. Break it once and it can be put back together with care, break it again and it splits into more pieces, and eventually it shatters beyond restore. Freedom Through Commitment: consumer culture wants more, more, more, but more is not always better. The paradox of choice means more options make you less satisfied with whatever you choose. Commitment to one woman, one place, a handful of friendships, and writing full-time liberates: it hones attention, makes decision-making easier, removes fear of missing out, and lets you achieve more than breadth ever would, because breadth has diminishing returns. The first country inspires a massive perspective shift, the fifty-first adds little. The rejection of alternatives liberates us.

Chapter 9. … And Then You Die, pp. 158-175

The most transformational moment of his life came at nineteen: his friend Josh took him to a party on a lake north of Dallas; they drank beers in the pool, talked about bands and girls and moving to New York, and Josh's last words to him were seek the truth for yourself, and I will meet you there. Later that evening Josh jumped off a thirty-foot cliff into the lake; his legs cramped from dehydration and alcohol, it was dark, no one could see where his screams were coming from, and it took scuba divers three hours to find his body. Manson cried for twelve hours in his car driving back to Austin, fell into a deep depression, and dreamed about Josh for months. In one dream, sitting in a Jacuzzi, Josh laughed and said something like, why do you care that I am dead when you are still so afraid to live? He woke up crying. The realization: if there is no reason to do anything, there is also no reason to not do anything, and in the face of death's inevitability there is no reason to give in to fear, embarrassment, or shame. That summer he gave up weed, cigarettes, video games, and rock star fantasies, dropped out of music school, signed up for college courses, started going to the gym, lost weight, made new friends, got his first girlfriend, actually studied; the next summer he read fifty nonfiction books in fifty days; the following year he transferred to an excellent university on the other side of the country. Josh's death was the clearest before and after point in his life: pre-tragedy inhibited, unambitious, obsessed with what the world might think; post-tragedy responsible, curious, hardworking, and the primary lesson was that there is nothing to be afraid of, ever. Death scares us, so we avoid thinking about it, yet death is the light by which the shadow of all of life's meaning is measured. Without death, everything would feel inconsequential. Something Beyond Our Selves: Ernest Becker, an academic outcast, PhD in anthropology comparing Zen Buddhism and psychoanalysis, fired from four jobs in six years, once for denouncing psychiatry as fascism to a boss who was a psychiatrist, once for siding with students against the National Guard at San Francisco State, got colon cancer, wrote The Denial of Death from his deathbed, died in 1974, and the book won the Pulitzer Prize. Becker's two points: humans are the only animals that can conceptualize themselves abstractly and therefore become aware of their own death, causing death terror; and we have two selves, the physical self that will die and the conceptual self we construct to live forever, names on buildings, statues, books, children, which he called immortality projects, the source of all human civilization and of our values. We give a fuck about things because giving a fuck distracts us from the reality of our own death. Becker's deathbed realization was that immortality projects were the problem, not the solution. People should question their conceptual self and become comfortable with death, the bitter antidote. The Sunny Side of Death: at South Africa's Cape of Good Hope, Manson climbs to the cliff edge and inches toward it, ten feet, five feet, three feet, two feet, one foot, fighting his body's magnet pulling him back and his mind's visualizations of falling, until he sits on the edge itself, legs over, wind ruffling his hair, and laughs, reminding himself it is all right to die. The Stoics implored people to keep death in mind to appreciate life; Buddhism treats meditation as preparation for death; Mark Twain said the fear of death follows from the fear of life. Confronting mortality obliterates crappy, fragile, superficial values and forces the only truly important question: what is your legacy, how will the world be different and better when you are gone? Death is the only thing we can know with any certainty, so it must be the compass for all other values. The only way to be comfortable with death is to see yourself as something bigger than yourself, choosing values that stretch beyond serving yourself, caring about something greater, believing you are a contributing component in some much larger entity. Entitlement strips this away, sucks attention inward, and makes you feel you are the center of all problems. Culture confuses great attention with great success, but they are not the same. You are already great because, in the face of endless confusion and certain death, you continue to choose what to give a fuck about. He ends with Bukowski, we are all going to die, which alone should make us love each other, and with an Australian tourist at the Cape asking if he is okay, to which he answers, alive, very alive.

Key terms

  • Giving a fuck: caring about something; care is limited, and you are always choosing what to care about.
  • The Feedback Loop from Hell: feeling bad about feeling bad, anxiety about anxiety, guilt about guilt, which compounds the original feeling.
  • The backwards law: pursuing a positive experience reinforces that you lack it; pursuing the negative, pain, failure, insecurity, generates the positive.
  • Disappointment Panda: Manson's invented superhero who tells people harsh truths they need to hear but do not want to accept.
  • Entitlement: feeling you deserve good things without earning them; two forms, I am awesome so I deserve special treatment, and I suck so I deserve special treatment, same selfish core.
  • The Tyranny of Exceptionalism: the internet floods us with the extreme best and worst, so ordinary life feels like failure.
  • The Self-Awareness Onion: three layers, understanding your emotions, asking why you feel them, and examining the values underneath.
  • Good values: reality-based, socially constructive, immediate and controllable; honesty, vulnerability, curiosity, charity, humility, creativity.
  • Bad values: superstitious, socially destructive, not immediate or controllable; pleasure, material success, always being right, staying positive, dominance, being liked by everybody.
  • Responsibility vs. fault: fault is past tense, responsibility is present tense; you are responsible for how you interpret and respond, even when you did not cause what happened.
  • Victimhood chic: public blame and shame as a popular high, on the right and the left, which distracts from real problems and real victims.
  • Manson's Law of Avoidance: the more something threatens your identity, the more you will avoid it.
  • The Do Something Principle: action is not just the effect of motivation, it is also the cause of it; start working and the right ideas show up.
  • Immortality projects: Becker's term for the conceptual self we construct to live forever, names on buildings, statues, books, children, the source of civilization and of our values.
  • Practical enlightenment: becoming comfortable with the idea that some suffering is always inevitable, so you become invincible in a low-level spiritual way.

What the book does NOT prove, and what it leaves open

  • The book is an argument built from stories, not a systematic study. Its central claims, that better values produce better problems and a better life, that responsibility cures victimhood, that embracing uncertainty makes you less insecure, are presented through anecdotes, not tested evidence. The notes record them as Manson's claims, not verified findings.
  • Many factual claims are stated without sources in the text as read: the Buddha story as told, Onoda's jungle war, Mustaine and Pete Best's later feelings, Dabrowski's survivor study, Baumeister's criminal self-esteem research, the Stanford Prison Experiment, and the OCD documentary outcomes. Some are historically documented, others are retold secondhand; none were checked against primary sources here.
  • The book's model of emotions as feedback, of the brain registering little difference between physical and psychological pain, and of the hedonic treadmill is presented in general terms, not as a literature review. Treat it as a popular framework, not neuroscience.
  • The advice on responsibility for tragedy, including Malala, a grieving father, and a suicide plan in the William James story, is philosophical, not clinical. It is not medical or therapeutic advice, and the notes do not present it as such.
  • The chapter on saying no treats Romeo and Juliet as satire and romantic love as a cocaine-like high. That is Manson's reading, not a settled literary or scientific claim.
  • The excerpt from Everything Is F*cked is a preview, not a read of that book. Nothing in these notes is a claim about that book beyond what the excerpt itself contains.
  • Coverage limit: this was a full read of the 2016 HarperOne eBook in the Libby reader, sliders 1 to 199. It is not a claim to have read any other edition, the audiobook, or Manson's later work.

Questions worth carrying forward

  • What pain are you willing to struggle for, and does your current life reflect that choice or someone else's?
  • What are your three best values, and are they reality-based, socially constructive, and within your control, or are they pleasure, success, being right, or staying positive?
  • Where are you in the Feedback Loop from Hell right now, feeling bad about feeling bad, and what would breaking the loop look like?
  • What is one thing you are certain about that you have never tested, and what would it mean if you were wrong?
  • What are you avoiding because it threatens your identity, and what is the cost of staying unheard, unknown, or unchanged?
  • What is one failure you have been avoiding, and what is the smallest action, the Do Something step, you could take today?
  • What are you saying yes to that you should say no to, and what commitment would free you if you made it?
  • If you kept your own death in view, what would you stop caring about, and what legacy would you start building now?
