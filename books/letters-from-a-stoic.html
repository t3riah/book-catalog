Thesis in short

Philosophy is a daily cure, not a pastime. Guard time, your only true property; withdraw from crowds, business, and luxury; treat death, pain, poverty, and Fortune's gifts as indifferents; and hold that the wise man's only goods are within, virtue, reason, and a good conscience, so that he lives according to nature, serves others, and is ready to die at any hour.

Overall summary

The letters, written in 63 to 65 A.D. to Lucilius, teach Stoic philosophy as daily practice. Each letter starts from a concrete incident, a book, a journey, an illness, a death, a crowd at the arena, and turns it into a lesson: save time, read few master-thinkers, judge a friend before befriending him, despise death, live inwardly different but outwardly conforming, share knowledge by example, avoid crowds, withdraw to help more people, be self-sufficient yet desire friends, live to yourself, cherish old age, fear imagination more than reality, withdraw from the world, prefer brains to brawn, let philosophy guide life, cast away riches, practise poverty, retire from business, practise what you preach, seek renown from within, withdraw fully or not at all, find true joy in a good conscience, despise death, reform yourself, learn to die, let virtue abide, change your soul not your climate, speak only to the willing, conquer the conqueror by facing death, be deaf to siren songs, round out your life, study whole works not maxims, perfect yourself to love, retire, enlist under virtue, converse quietly, aspire nobly, speak composedly, find the god within, know values, despise fame, ignore pedigrees, avoid quibbling, see life as short, cure your blindness, flee vice, choose teachers by their lives, face faults, practise dying in asthma, hide less and live more, study through noise, face travel's trials, turn to being, distinguish joy from pleasure, pray rightly, meet death cheerfully, keep good company, grieve by choice, do the philosopher's task, and trace all things to their first cause, Creative Reason, God.

Gummere presents the letters as essays built on a concrete incident, notes that Seneca protests slavery, the arena, and luxury, and argues that his life proves his sincerity. Contents pp. iii-v; Introduction pp. vii-xiii; The Text pp. xii-xiii; Bibliography pp. xiv-xv.

Letters I-X, pp. 3-59

I, On Saving Time: time is the one thing we truly own; we die daily, so hold every hour in your grasp and do not depend on tomorrow. II, On Discursiveness in Reading: stay in one place and among a few master-thinkers; everywhere means nowhere; acquire one thought daily against poverty and death. III, On True and False Friendship: judge before befriending, trust after; regard him as loyal and you will make him loyal; combine action and repose. IV, On the Terrors of Death: hasten to enjoy an improved mind; death is the last evil, so no evil is great, it either does not come or passes; nature asks only for enough. V, On the Philosopher's Mean: be inwardly different but outwardly conform; avoid ostentatious asceticism; live better than the multitude, not contrary to it; hope and fear are chained together. VI, On Sharing Knowledge: true friendship is a union of like minds; the living voice and shared life teach more than the written word; the way is long if one follows precepts, but short and helpful if one follows patterns. VII, On Crowds: avoid the crowd; the midday arena show proves it corrupts, Seneca comes home more greedy, ambitious, voluptuous, cruel, and inhuman; be content with few, with one, with none at all. VIII, On the Philosopher's Seclusion: withdraw to help a greater number; warn against Fortune's gifts, which are snares; indulge the body only as far as good health requires; if you would enjoy real freedom, you must be the slave of Philosophy. IX, On Philosophy and Friendship: the wise man is self-sufficient yet desires friends for friendship's own sake, not for use; Stilbo, after losing city, wife, and children, said he has all his goods with him. X, On Living to Oneself: even solitude is dangerous to the fool, who plans folly alone; pray only for a sound mind and good health; pray to God for nothing you cannot pray for openly.

Letters XI-XX, pp. 61-141

XI, On the Blush of Modesty: the young friend's blush is a good sign; wisdom cannot remove natural bodily weaknesses; keep a man of high character ever before your eyes. XII, On Old Age: the crumbling country house and the aged slave Felicio show old age everywhere; cherish it, life is most delightful on the downward slope; every day completes a life; it is wrong to live under constraint, but no man is constrained to live under constraint. XIII, On Groundless Fears: there are more things likely to frighten us than to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality; do not be unhappy before the crisis; temper fear with hope; the fool is always getting ready to live. XIV, On the Reasons for Withdrawing from the World: cherish the body but be ready to deliver it when reason demands; the violence of the stronger terrifies by spectacle; take refuge in philosophy; the wise man regards the reason for his actions, not the results. XV, On Brawn and Brains: being well means studying philosophy; body-building strangles the soul; limit the flesh, give free play to the spirit; the fool's life is empty of gratitude and full of fears. XVI, On Philosophy, the Guide of Life: no man can live happily without wisdom; keep resolutions already made; whether Fate, God, or Chance rules, philosophy must be our defence, obey God cheerfully, Fortune defiantly; if you live according to nature, you will never be poor. XVII, On Philosophy and Riches: cast away everything and strive toward a sound mind; poverty is unburdened and carefree; stop postponing philosophy; wisdom offers wealth in ready money; the fault is in the mind, not in wealth. XVIII, On Festivals and Fasting: at the Saturnalia be neither wholly like nor wholly unlike the crowd; practise poverty for a few days to test yourself, as a soldier trains in peacetime; dare to scorn the sight of wealth; ungoverned anger begets madness. XIX, On Worldliness and Retirement: withdraw from business or tear yourself away; we have spent our lives on the high seas, let us die in harbour; prosperity is greedy and exposed to others' greed; choose table-companions before dishes. XX, On Practising What You Preach: test progress by stoutness of heart and decrease of desire; prove words by deeds; always desiring the same things, and always refusing the same things, is wisdom; set aside days to practise fancied poverty.

Letters XXI-XXX, pp. 141-221

XXI, On the Renown Which My Writings Will Bring You: studies give renown from within; Seneca promises Lucilius what Epicurus promised Idomeneus, fame through his letters; if you wish to make Pythocles rich, do not add to his store of money, but subtract from his desires. XXII, On the Futility of Half-Way Measures: withdraw from showy pursuits, loosening the knot rather than cutting it; seize the fleeting opportunity; there are few men whom slavery holds fast, but many more who hold fast to slavery; everyone goes out of life just as if he had but lately entered it. XXIII, On the True Joy Which Comes from Philosophy: do not find joy in useless things; real joy is a stern matter, born within, from a good conscience and contempt of chance; they live ill who are always beginning to live. XXIV, On Despising Death: do not summon trouble before it arrives; measure the fear, it is either insignificant or short-lived; Rutilius, Metellus, Socrates, Mucius, Cato, and Scipio prove death is little to be feared; we die every day; Epicurus rebukes those who crave death as much as those who fear it. XXV, On Reformation: correct one friend's faults, crush out another's; hardened men can still be cured if excess is resisted; do everything as if Epicurus were watching you; appoint a guardian over yourself until you respect yourself. XXVI, On Old Age and Death: age damaged Seneca's body, not his mind; death will deliver the final judgment in your case; learn to die and you unlearn slavery. XXVII, On the Good Which Abides: only virtue gives lasting joy; let your faults die before you die; do not delegate the work, Calvisius Sabinus bought slaves to memorize the poets for him; no man is able to borrow or buy a sound mind; real wealth is poverty adjusted to the law of Nature. XXVIII, On Travel as a Cure for Discontent: travel does not shake off gloom, you need a change of soul, not climate; why wonder that globe-trotting does not help, seeing that you always take yourself with you; the knowledge of sin is the beginning of salvation. XXIX, On the Critical Condition of Marcellinus: speak only to the willing listener; wisdom is an art with a definite aim, choosing those who will progress; Seneca will brave Marcellinus's taunts; I have never wished to cater to the crowd. XXX, On Conquering the Conqueror: Aufidius Bassus, shattered in health, is joyful in the very sight of death; death stands so far beyond all evil that it is beyond all fear of evils; old age grants no reprieve; always think on death in order that you may never fear it.

Letters XXXI-XL, pp. 223-271

XXXI, On Siren Songs: be deaf to those who love you most, for they pray for bad things with good intentions; only one good, trust in oneself; the good soul is a god dwelling as a guest in a human body, and one may leap to heaven from the very slums. XXXII, On Progress: do not let the crowd hinder progress; life is short, made shorter by unsteadiness; round out your life before death; get control over yourself. XXXIII, On the Futility of Learning Maxims: do not skim wisdom by epitomes, study the whole work; the Stoics' writings are strong throughout; make maxims, do not memorize them; predecessors are guides, not masters, truth lies open for all. XXXIV, On a Promising Pupil: Seneca claims Lucilius as his handiwork; the will is almost everything; the good man is one complete and finished, whom no constraint can render bad. XXXV, On the Friendship of Kindred Minds: perfect yourself to learn how to love; friendship always helps, love sometimes harms; test progress by whether you desire the same things today as yesterday. XXXVI, On the Value of Retirement: despise those who upbraid retirement; prosperity is turbulent; Fortune has no jurisdiction over character; learn contempt of death, death is nothing to us, since when we exist death is not present, and when death is present we do not exist. XXXVII, On Allegiance to Virtue: you have enlisted under oath to virtue; die erect and unyielding; you cannot escape necessities, but you can overcome them. XXXVIII, On Quiet Conversation: conversation creeps into the soul by degrees; philosophy is good advice, not given at the top of one's lungs; words should be scattered like seed, small, but unfolding to greatest growth. XXXIX, On Noble Aspirations: a cram cannot substitute for reading the philosophers; the noble soul is roused to honourable things like flame springing straight up; excess ruins, like over-rich soil flattening grain; vices become habits. XL, On the Proper Style for a Philosopher's Discourse: the philosopher's speech, like his life, should be composed, neither the torrent of Serapio nor the drip of the stammerer; be slow of speech.

Letters XLI-L, pp. 273-335

XLI, On the God Within Us: a holy spirit indwells us; no man can be good without God's help; in each good man a god dwells; reason demands the easiest thing, to live according to nature. XLII, On Values: a good man cannot become one so quickly, a true one springs up like the phoenix, once in five hundred years; vice's worst penalty is dissatisfaction with itself; loss is an opinion, not a loss; he that owns himself has lost nothing. XLIII, On the Relativity of Fame: greatness is relative, a ship large in a river is tiny on the ocean; live with your door wide open; a good conscience welcomes the crowd. XLIV, On Philosophy and Pedigrees: philosophy never looks into pedigrees; all men spring from the gods; the soul alone renders us noble; the happy life is freedom from care and unshaken confidence. XLV, On Sophistical Argumentation: read Seneca as one still seeking truth, he sold himself to no man; time is lost in word-quibbling; teach instead that the happy man is not whom the crowd calls happy; the truly happy man's possessions are all in his soul. XLVI, On a New Book by Lucilius: Seneca meant only to taste Lucilius's book but its charm coaxed him through it entirely; smooth, vigorous, chaste, lofty, and noble. XLVII, On Master and Slave: slaves are men, fellow-slaves, comrades; as many enemies as you have slaves, they are not enemies when we acquire them, we make them enemies; treat your inferiors as you would be treated by your betters; no servitude is more disgraceful than self-imposed. XLVIII, On Quibbling as Unworthy of the Philosopher: friendship creates a partnership of all interests; live for your neighbour if you would live for yourself; the mouse is a syllable, therefore a syllable eats cheese fallacy shows logic games are not merely useless but harmful; philosophy promised to make us equal to God. XLIX, On the Shortness of Life: time's flight is infinitely swift, clearer looking backward; all past time lies in the same abyss; life is but a point; teach how to face death; the language of truth is simple. L, On Our Blindness and Its Cure: like Harpaste, the blind clown who thinks the house is dark, we do not see our own greed; the evil is within us, so the cure is hard; learning virtue means unlearning vice; philosophy is at the same moment wholesome and pleasant.

Letters LI-LXV, pp. 337-459

LI, On Baiae and Morals: flee the provocations of vice, one winter in Campania relaxed Hannibal; our warfare allows no rest; freedom is serving no circumstance; pleasures are bandits who embrace us only to garrotte us. LII, On Choosing Our Teachers: Epicurus's three classes of men seeking truth, those who find it unaided, those who need a leader, Metrodorus, and those who must be forced; choose guides who teach by their lives; choose as a guide one whom you will admire more when you see him act than when you hear him speak. LIII, On the Faults of the Spirit: Seneca's seasickness anecdote, we forget even bodily failings, let alone deeper faults; the more serious in proportion as they are more hidden; do not study philosophy merely in spare time; the wise man's one advantage over a god is that he is freed from terrors by his own effort. LIV, On Asthma and Death: Seneca's asthma is practising how to die; death is non-existence, known before birth; what was before me will happen again after me; the wise man is never thrust out, for he does nothing unwillingly. LV, On Vatia's Villa: Vatia knew how to hide, not how to live; leisure is not idleness; place contributes little to tranquillity, the mind must make everything agreeable to itself; a friend retained in the spirit can never be absent. LVI, On Quiet and Study: the bathhouse racket over Seneca's lodgings proves external noise is harmless, all outdoors may be bedlam, provided that there is no disturbance within; sometimes quiet means disquiet; hidden vices do most harm. LVII, On the Trials of Travel: the Naples tunnel's gloom gave a mental thrill without fear; some emotions no courage can avoid, nature reminds courage of mortality; the soul, subtler than fire, escapes through the crushing body. LVIII, On Being: Latin lacks a word for Plato's being, so Seneca coins quod est and essentia; Plato's sixfold division of things which exist, genus and species, the ideas as everlasting patterns; Heraclitus, we go down twice into the same river, and yet into a different river; sense-objects are imaginary and transient, so turn the mind to the eternal; frugal living prolongs life to an end worth enjoying. LIX, On Pleasure and Joy: joy, gaudium, belongs only to the wise, an elation of spirit trusting in its own possessions; pleasure is a wayward emotion inspired by a false good; Sextius's simile of the army in hollow square, the wise man has all virtues deployed on every side; the foolish rejoice no more than lions that have caught prey. LX, On Harmful Prayers: parents' prayers for us were hostile; natural hunger costs little, ambitious cravings cost much; he really lives who is made use of by many, he really lives who makes use of himself. LXI, On Meeting Death Cheerfully: cease desiring what we have desired; live each day as a complete life; dying gladly means doing nothing unwillingly; I have lived, my dear friend Lucilius, long enough, I have had my fill, I await death. LXII, On Good Company: Seneca's time is free; he spends it with the best of all ages; Demetrius, half-naked, lacks nothing; the shortest cut to riches is to despise riches. LXIII, On Grief for Lost Friends: Flaccus is dead; weep but do not wail, Homer allowed Niobe only a day of weeping; end grief by choice, not merely by time; Seneca confesses he wept immoderately for Annaeus Serenus, never imagining Serenus could die before him; perhaps the dead have only been sent on ahead. LXIV, On the Philosopher's Task: Quintus Sextius the Elder fills Seneca with confidence, why keep me waiting, Fortune, enter the lists; we enter into the inheritance of many predecessors and must increase it; worship the discoverers, Cato, Laelius, Socrates, Plato, Zeno, Cleanthes. LXV, On the First Cause: the Stoics' two sources, cause and matter; Plato's five causes, material, agent, form, pattern, end in view; the first, general cause must be simple, Creative Reason, God; the body is a chain manacling freedom; I was born to a greater destiny than to be a mere chattel of my body; and what is death, it is either the end, or a process of change, I have no fear of ceasing to exist, it is the same as not having begun. Central thesis

Seneca's thesis, across all 124 letters, is that philosophy is not a subject but a cure, applied daily to time, fear, desire, and death. The wise man guards the one thing he truly owns, time; withdraws from the crowd, business, and luxury that corrupt him; treats everything Fortune gives or takes, wealth, health, reputation, life itself, as indifferent; and finds his only goods within, virtue, reason, and a good conscience. That is how he lives according to nature, serves others, and is ready to die at any hour, because death is either the end or a change, and neither is to be feared.

Key terms

Indifferents: death, pain, poverty, wealth, health, and Fortune's gifts, neither good nor evil in themselves. Virtue: the only good, living according to nature, reason in action. Directing mind: the ruling principle, reason, the god within. Gaudium: joy, an elation of spirit trusting in its own possessions, belonging only to the wise, as against pleasure, a wayward emotion inspired by a false good. Time: the one true property, we die daily, so hold every hour. Crowd: the multitude whose approval corrupts, to be avoided or used only with a few, one, or none. Fortune: the power that gives and takes externals, with no jurisdiction over character. First Cause: Creative Reason, God, the simple first cause behind matter and form.

What the book does NOT prove

It does not prove that the wise man can be fully self-sufficient, or that death, pain, and poverty are truly indifferent in the way Seneca claims; those are Stoic doctrines he assumes and repeats, not conclusions he argues from evidence, and his own letters confess grief, asthma, old age, and fear. It does not prove that withdrawing from crowds and business always helps, or that travel never helps; Seneca's own examples, the arena, Baiae, the Naples tunnel, show the opposite can happen, and his advice is counsel, not a measured result. Questions to carry forward

What hour today did you waste, and what would it look like to hold it in your grasp? What crowd, business, or luxury is corrupting you, and what would withdrawing from it cost you? What are you fearing in imagination that has not crushed you in reality? What would you do differently if you lived each day as a complete life, and were ready to die at any hour?

Thesis for Volume II: the only good is the honourable, virtue, perfect reason, and the happy life is peace of mind and lasting tranquillity, unimpaired by fortune, pain, poverty, exile, or death.

LXVI, On Various Aspects of Virtue, pp. 3-35: Claranus, an old schoolmate hale in spirit in a frail body, shows virtue needs nothing to set it off, it is its own great glory and hallows the body it dwells in. All goods are equal: virtue, right reason, a portion of the divine spirit in a human body, cannot be increased or decreased; joy and brave endurance of torture are equal goods, differing only in their material. Epicurus, on his last and most blessed day, in agony from bladder and ulcerated stomach, still called the day happy; Mucius, watching his hand drip blood over the enemy's fire, deserves greater praise than the uninjured brave man.

LXVII, On Ill-Health and Endurance of Suffering, pp. 35-45: spring discloses itself uncertainly; old age fastens Seneca to his bed, and he thanks it. Not the hardship itself is desirable, but the virtue that endures it patiently. Bravery's finest part is not shrinking from the stake; praying for a life of honour indirectly includes Regulus's chest, Cato's wound, Rutilius's exile, and Socrates's cup of poison. Attalus: prefer that Fortune keep me in her camp rather than in the lap of luxury.

LXVIII, On Wisdom and Retirement, pp. 45-53: retire and conceal yourself in repose, but conceal your retirement also, no placard reading Philosopher and Quietist; a locked room invites the thief. The wise man's field of public life is the universe. Seneca is curing his own sores. Use retirement for greater and more beautiful activities; I shall depart a better man.

LXIX, On Rest and Restlessness, pp. 53-55: do not change headquarters and scurry about; frequent flitting means an unsteady spirit. Remedies help most when not interrupted; give the eyes time to unlearn what they have seen. Vices tempt by rewards, but the life Seneca speaks of must be lived without being paid. Ponder and practise how to welcome death, or even invite it. You are throwing away none of your own time; for what you leave behind does not belong to you.

LXX, On the Proper Time to Slip the Cable, pp. 57-73: we have sailed past life as if on a voyage, lands and towns left astern. Death is the harbour, not a dangerous reef. For mere living is not a good, but living well; the wise man will live as long as he ought, not as long as he can. Socrates spent thirty days in prison rather than starve himself, to show submission to the laws; Scribonia to Drusus Libo: what pleasure do you find in doing another's work. The best thing eternal law ever ordained was that it allowed us one entrance into life, but many exits. The German gladiator who choked himself with the sponge-tipped stick, and another who broke his neck in the cart's wheel spokes, prove virtue is found in the gladiators' training-school as well as among leaders. He is truly great who not only has given himself the order to die, but has also found the means.

LXXI, On the Supreme Good, pp. 73-95: the archer must know his target: when a man does not know what harbour he is making for, no wind is the right wind. The Supreme Good is that which is honourable, and that which is honourable is the only good. Socrates recalled philosophy to rules of conduct: follow these rules in order that you may be happy; and let some men think you even a fool. Cato bore failure of election, spending the day in play, and his intended death, the night in reading, with an equally stout heart; long ago destiny saw to it that Cato should come to no harm. Nothing is free from change, earth, sky, the whole fabric of the universe. Regulus paying the penalty for keeping his word is not unhappy; virtue is straight and admits no bending, like a carpenter's rule. Virtue is the only good; at any rate there is no good without virtue; and virtue itself is situated in our nobler part, that is, the rational part; virtue is a true and never-swerving judgment. Conquer greed, ambition, and the fear of death, which conquered the conquerors of the world.

LXXII, On Business as the Enemy of Philosophy, pp. 97-103: memory needs unrolling like a book whose rolls stuck together by disuse. The study of philosophy is not to be postponed until you have leisure; everything else is to be neglected in order that we may attend to philosophy. Intermittent study goes back to the beginning. The wise man's joy is a woven fabric, rent by no chance happening; his happiness is within himself. The mind once healed is healed for good and all. Attalus's dog simile, snapping at tossed bread, swallowing whole, always expecting more, the image of greedy absorption of external goods.

LXXIII, On Philosophers and Kings, pp. 105-113: philosophers are not stubborn rebels scorning rulers, no class is so grateful to the ruler, who bestows the privilege of peace and leisure; the restless public man owes much and expects much, his cravings growing by being supplied. Peace and liberty are indivisible goods belonging in their entirety to all men as much as to each individual; philosophy teaches to honourably avow and pay the debt of benefits received, sometimes acknowledgment itself constitutes payment. Sextius: Jupiter had no more power than the good man; virtue is not greater which lasts longer. This is the way to the stars; this is the way, by observing thrift, self-restraint, and courage. God comes into men; divine seeds are scattered through mortal bodies.

LXXIV, On Virtue as a Refuge from Worldly Distractions, pp. 113-137: the happy life consists in believing the only good is the honourable; anyone deeming other things good puts himself in Fortune's power. No man can be happy who yields to fancies, nothing brings happiness unless it also brings calm. Fortune's dole simile: the most sensible man runs from the theatre when the dole is brought in, knowing one pays a high price for small favours. Man is not more fortunate than God, because God has no enjoyment of the things given to us, lust, banquets, wealth, degrading pleasures. Preferred things, producta, are chattels, not parts of ourselves, use them sparingly, as given for safe-keeping. Love reason; the love of reason will arm you against the greatest hardships. A good can be lost only by changing into what is bad, impossible by nature's law; virtue suffers no space in us to be unoccupied; it takes possession of the whole soul and removes all sense of loss. The Supreme Good is neither impaired nor increased; scale an honourable life from a hundred years to a single day and it is equally honourable. The sage may feel natural agitation, colour change, cold limbs, but retains the firm belief none of these is evil; folly is despised precisely because she does not gladly do even the things she prides herself on; what greater madness than to be tortured by the future and not save strength for actual suffering.

LXXV, On the Diseases of the Soul, pp. 137-147: Lucilius complains the letters are carelessly written; Seneca prefers them spontaneous and easy, like conversation, nothing strained or artificial; he would rather show than speak his feelings. Let us say what we feel, and feel what we say; let speech harmonize with life; that man has fulfilled his promise who is the same person both when you see him and when you hear him. The sick man wants a physician who cures, not one who tickles his ears. Knowledge alone does not make one happy, one must do. Diseases of the mind are hardened, chronic vices, greed, ambition, a persistent perversion of judgment; passions are sudden, vehement impulses which by repetition cause disease, like a catarrh becoming a cough, then consumption. Three classes of those making progress, proficientes: nearest perfection, beyond diseases but still feeling passions; those who have escaped the great ills and passions but not yet in assured possession of immunity, and can slip back; and a third, not to be despised, of men beyond the reach of the basest vices. We hasten towards virtue while hampered by vices; we worship that which is honourable only in so far as we have time to spare. Break off affairs that forestall us, and neither desire nor fear shall rout us; death is no evil and the gods are not powers of evil; it is a priceless good to be master of oneself.

LXXVI, On Learning Wisdom in Old Age, pp. 147-169: Seneca attends a philosopher's lectures at two o'clock for the fourth day; men of all ages are admitted to the classroom. As long as you live, keep learning how to live. Crowds jam the Neapolitan Theatre to judge flute-players, but the school where What is a good man is discussed has few. Wisdom does not fall by chance; money, honours, authority come unasked, virtue does not. Each thing is judged by its own good: vine by productiveness and wine flavour, stag by speed, beast of burden by sturdiness of back, dog by scent, speed, courage. In man the best quality is reason; perfect reason is man's peculiar good, strength he shares with lions, beauty with peacocks. A ship is good not for painted colours or ivory figure-head but for being steady, staunch, taut, obedient to helm; a sword for fine edge and piercing point, not gold belt and gemmed scabbard; a carpenter's rule, ask how straight, not how beautiful. Only virtue is good; wisdom never slips back into folly; the good man does what is honourable even if toilsome, harmful, perilous, and refuses baseness even for money, pleasure, power. The test: would you die for your country gladly, if you would, there is no other good. A dwarf is not tall, though he stand upon a mountain-top; a colossal statue will still be tall, though you place it in a well. Strip a man naked, strip off estate, titles, Fortune's deceptions, even his body; consider the soul, whether its greatness is borrowed or its own. If an evil has been pondered beforehand, the blow is gentle when it comes; to the fool trusting fortune each event arrives new, a large part of evil is its novelty. The wise man knows all things are in store for him, whatever happens, he says I knew it.

LXXVII, On Taking One's Own Life, pp. 169-181: the Alexandrian mail-boats arrive; Seneca delights in his laziness, in no hurry to know how his affairs stand. Life is not incomplete if honourable; at whatever point you leave off living nobly, life is a whole. Tullius Marcellinus, in hopeless but protracted disease, deliberates about dying; his Stoic friend advises him to die honourably: it is not an important matter to live; all your slaves live, and so do animals; but it is important to die honourably, sensibly, bravely. Marcellinus distributed little sums among his sorrowing slaves, fasted three days, lay in a hot tub and gradually passed away. Would you not think him an utter fool who wept because he was not alive a thousand years ago, and is he not just as much of a fool who weeps because he will not be alive a thousand years from now. It is all the same; you will not be, and you were not. Neither of these periods of time belongs to you. Time is a point; fate is fixed, unalterable, governed by mighty everlasting compulsion. The Spartan boy captured while still a stripling, crying I will not be a slave, dashed his brains out against the wall: so near at hand is freedom, and is anyone still a slave, life, if courage to die be lacking, is slavery. A man who clings to life is nothing but a wine-strainer, connoisseur of oyster and mullet, torn away unwillingly from the very things he exhausted. Gaius Caesar to the grey-bearded prisoner begging death: what, are you alive now. Life is a play, it matters not how long the action is spun out, but how good the acting is; stop whenever you choose, only see that the closing period is well turned.

LXXVIII, On the Healing Power of the Mind, pp. 181-199: Seneca's own catarrh and fevers, succumbing to extreme thinness, often tempted him to end his life, held back by his kind old father. He commanded himself to live, sometimes it is an act of bravery even to live. I owe my life to philosophy, and that is the least of my obligations. Nothing refreshes and aids a sick man so much as the affection of his friends; nothing so steals away the expectation and the fear of death. He imagined he would live through his friends, yielding his soul over to them; it is most miserable to have lost one's zest for dying and have no zest in living. The counsel: despise death, the cure for the whole life. Three serious elements in every disease: fear of death, bodily pain, interruption of pleasures. You will die, not because you are ill, but because you are alive. Pain: nature made it either endurable or short; severest pains sit in the slenderest parts, which soon go numb and lose sensation. Everything depends on opinion; ambition, luxury, greed, hark back to opinion. It is according to opinion that we suffer. A man is as wretched as he has convinced himself that he is. Cut away fear of future suffering and recollection of past suffering, the latter no longer concerns me, the former concerns me not yet. Perchance some day the memory of this sorrow will even bring delight. Fight with all your might, give way and be vanquished, strive and conquer. Athletes endure every torture for fame; we endure for a higher reward, virtue, steadfastness of soul, peace won for all time, fortune utterly vanquished in combat. Disease hampers the body, not the soul, plead, teach, listen, learn, investigate, meditate; there is a place for virtue even upon a bed of sickness; be your own spectator and seek your own applause. Pleasures of the mind are higher and less uncertain, no physician can refuse them to the sick man. Surfeit of self cannot seize a life surveying all things manifold, great, divine; only idle leisure makes men hate their lives; truth never palls, only untruths cloy; death untimely still leaves a taste of all the longest life can give. Posidonius: a single day among the learned lasts longer than the longest life of the ignorant. Hold fast: yield not to adversity; trust not to prosperity; keep Fortune's full scope before your eyes; that which has been long expected comes more gently.

LXXIX, On the Rewards of Scientific Discovery, pp. 201-211: Seneca awaits Lucilius's letter about his trip round Sicily, especially about Charybdis: is it lashed into a whirlpool by a wind from one direction only, or do all storms disturb its depths, is it true objects snatched down are carried many miles under water and emerge near Tauromenium. He will ask Lucilius to climb Aetna too. The Aetna poem is Lucilius's own craze for fine writing, not Seneca's commission, Ovid was not deterred by Virgil, nor Cornelius Severus by either; predecessors opened the way, not forestalled all that could be said. A topic already treated grows daily; the last writer has the best bargain, finding words to marshal anew, they are common property. Wisdom has the advantage that no man can be outdone by another except during the climb, at the top it is a draw, like the sun, moon, seas, the universe keeping the same limits; the wise are equal and on the same footing, differing only in peculiar gifts, while happiness is equal in them all. Virtue alone has no lowering; like the stars, her stature is fixed. Fame is the shadow of virtue, sometimes preceding, sometimes following, always greater the later it comes, once envy retreats. Democritus was thought mad, Socrates barely won fame, Cato unknown until lost, Rutilius's virtue shone only when wronged, his suffering was his triumph. Many men's progress toward virtue appears only after death; some are ruined, not rescued, by reputation. Epicurus, unknown in Athens itself, wrote late that he and Metrodorus were so blest it did no harm to be unknown in Greece, discovered only after he ceased to be, yet his renown shone forth. Virtue never lost to view; a day will reveal her though hidden by contemporaries' spite; many thousands of years and peoples will come after you, have regard to them; if fame is virtue's reward, even that cannot pass away. Pretence accomplishes nothing; truth is the same in every part; lies are thin stuff, transparent if examined carefully.

LXXX, On Worldly Deceptions, pp. 213-219: the games keep the bores away, free time; he follows predecessors but not as a slave, allows himself to discover, alter, reject; the stadium's cheer sets him thinking: how many train bodies, how few train minds; crowds at spurious games, solitude where good arts are taught; how feather-brained the athletes whose muscles we admire; if the body can be trained to endure blows and kicks of several opponents and the scorching sun in burning dust, drenched in blood, how much more the mind. The mind is toughened by blows of Fortune, rising after being trampled, it grows from within, nourishing itself, exercising itself. Virtue comes without equipment or expense; all that goes to make you a good man lies within yourself. What do you need in order to become good, to wish it. Freedom from slavery cannot be bought, the ledger entry Freedom is useless; neither buyers nor sellers possess it. Free yourself first from fear of death, the yoke about our necks, then from fear of poverty, the poor laugh more often and more heartily than the rich. The poor man's merriment is genuine, troubles shallow, anxiety passing like a fitful cloud; the so-called happy feign mirth while festering sadness eats their hearts, forced to act happiness amid sorrows. Human life is a drama where we play our parts badly, the actor who declaims Lo, I am he whom Argos hails as lord is but a slave on five measures of grain and five denarii; the proud declaimer against Menelaus receives a daily pittance and sleeps on rags. So too the dandies in litters above the crowd, their happiness is the actor's mask; tear it off and you will scorn them. Judge a man as you would a horse or slave for sale: strip him. Weigh yourself by putting away money, estates, honours, and looking into your own soul, at present you take others' word for what you are.

LXXXI, On Benefits, pp. 219-241: on ingratitude, better to get no return than to confer no benefits; sow again after a poor crop; worth trying many ungrateful ones to find one grateful. Keep conferring benefits despite ingratitude, the traveller wanders but returns to the path; sailors try the sea after shipwreck; the banker is not frightened away by the swindler. If a benefactor later harms you, does that square the account. A strict judge checks one act against the other but still credits any excess of benefits, the helpful act came first, so time counts; benefits and injuries depend on the spirit, how gladly help was offered, how reluctantly harm was done. The good man arranges the ledger to cheat himself, adding to the benefit, subtracting from the injury; the indulgent magistrate orders us to forget the injury and remember the accommodation; strict justice, thanks for benefit, retribution for injury, applies only when different men are involved, if the same man did both, the injury's force is nullified by the benefit; reckon a benefit at a higher rate than an injury; only the wise man knows exactly what value to put on things. None but the wise man knows how to confer a benefit, that man, I mean, who enjoys the giving more than the recipient enjoys the receiving. A gift's consequence matters more than its size, a thousand denarii at the right moment; the good man judges fairly but inclines toward the benefit, forgiving small injuries, remitting great ones where duty permits, easy in striking the balance, desiring to remain under obligation and to make return. The grateful man should be more joyful in repaying than in borrowing, anyone who receives a benefit more gladly than he repays it is mistaken. A man is an ingrate if he repays a favour without interest. Gratitude is good for ourselves: justice returns in large measure to itself; no man benefits his neighbour without benefiting himself, the reward of virtue lies in virtue itself, the wages of a good deed is to have done it. Gratitude itself is priceless, the consciousness of gratitude comes only to a soul divine and blessed; ingratitude is immediate unhappiness, he is unhappy forthwith. Attalus: evil herself drinks the largest portion of her own poison. The ungrateful man tortures himself, hates gifts he must repay, belittles them, enlarges injuries, nothing more wretched than a man who forgets benefits and clings to injuries; wisdom lends grace to every benefit, delighting by continued recollection. When injury merely equals benefit, kindly feeling is left over, like a defendant acquitted on equal votes. No man can be grateful unless he has learned to scorn what drives the herd to distraction, to repay a favour one must be willing to go into exile, pour forth blood, undergo poverty, let innocence be stained by shameful slanders. It is no slight price that a man must pay for being grateful. We hold nothing dearer than a benefit while seeking it, nothing cheaper after receiving it; what makes us forget benefits is greed for receiving others, we consider not what we have obtained but what we are to seek. Nothing is more honourable than a grateful heart; all cities and races will echo it; on this, good and bad agree, though at present we pay back injuries instead of benefits, and the chief reason a man is ungrateful is that he found it impossible to be grateful enough; conferring great benefits is dangerous, for the recipient, thinking repayment shameful, would have no one left alive to repay. There is no worse hatred than that which springs from shame at the desecration of a benefit.

LXXXII, On the Natural Fear of Death, pp. 241-259: Seneca has ceased being anxious about Lucilius; his voucher is a soul in love with the upright and good; the better part of him is on safe ground; Fortune can injure him, but he fears lest Lucilius injure himself; prefer trouble to luxury, in trouble means a hard, rough, toilsome life; the soul is made womanish by degrees by ease and laziness. Both extremes are to be deprecated, tension and sluggishness; the man on a perfumed couch is no less dead than the man dragged by the executioner's hook; leisure without study is death, a tomb for the living man; no hiding-place escapes fear of death or pain; gird yourself about with philosophy, an impregnable wall; Fortune can seize none except him that clings to her; knowledge of self and of Nature alone frees us. Strength of heart comes from constant study, practising with the soul, not the tongue, preparing to meet death. Do not expect encouragement from those who try to prove by hair-splitting logic that death is no evil, Seneca pokes fun at Greek absurdities: Zeno's syllogism, no evil is glorious; but death is glorious; therefore death is no evil, a cure, Zeno, I have been freed from fear, will you not utter sterner words instead of rousing a dying man to laughter. The counter-syllogism: nothing that is indifferent can be glorious; death is glorious; therefore death is not indifferent. The fallacy: mere death is not glorious, but a brave death is glorious. Indifferent things, sickness, pain, poverty, exile, death, are neither good nor evil; nothing glorious except as it deals with indifferent things. It is not poverty we praise but the man whom poverty cannot humble; not exile but the man who withdraws into exile in the spirit he would have sent another; not pain but the man whom pain has not coerced; not death but the man whose soul death takes away before it can confound it. Virtue visiting any indifferent thing makes it honourable and glorious; the death glorious in Cato's case is in Brutus's case, Cato used death honourably, Brutus basely; virtue adds glory. Indifferent things are like a sunny room, day fills it with light, night steals it; death is honourable when related to virtue; among middle things there are vast distinctions, death is not so indifferent as whether your hair is worn evenly; death belongs among things not evils but having a semblance of evil. Love of self, desire for existence and self-preservation, and abhorrence of dissolution make us shrink from death; the unknown future and fear of shades; death is indifferent yet not easily ignored, the soul must be hardened by long practice. The mind will never rise to virtue if it believes death is an evil; it will rise if it holds death a matter of indifference. No deed is honourable unless done with the whole heart, without rebellion. Yield not to evils, but, still braver, go where'er thy fortune shall allow, you cannot go braver if you believe these things are real evils; root out the idea, or apprehensions will check the impulse to action. Away with dialectical subtleties, when truth is at stake act frankly, when fear is to be combated act bravely; solve such questions rationally, to win conviction not force judgment. How would a general exhort troops about to die for wives and children, not with Zeno's syllogism, with the Fabii and the Lacedaemonians at Thermopylae; Leonidas: fellow-soldiers, let us to our breakfast, knowing that we shall sup in Hades. Great weapons for great monsters, the fierce serpent in Africa could not be wounded by arrows, slings, or siege engine; fear of death is not for the Three Hundred but all mankind; catchwords and petty syllogisms will not do it.

LXXXIII, On Drunkenness, pp. 259-275: give an account of each day, live as if in plain sight; think as if someone could look into your inmost soul, and there is one who can; nothing is shut off from God; watch myself continually, review each day; what makes us wicked is that no one looks back over his own life. The day's account, today unbroken, divided between rest and reading, brief exercise, his old age makes exercise cheap, as soon as he stirs, he is tired; his pace-maker the slave Pharius, pleasant fellow, both losing their teeth; he can scarcely follow Pharius's pace as he runs; very soon does a wide interval open between two persons who travel different ways, my slave is climbing up at the very moment when I am coming down, nay, his life is not coming down, it is falling outright; after tiring himself out he took a cold bath, he, former cold-water enthusiast who celebrated each new year by plunging into the canal and the Virgo aqueduct, has changed allegiance first to the Tiber, then to his favourite tank warmed only by the sun; stale bread, no table, no hand-washing, short nap, I am satisfied if I can just stop staying awake; the din of the Races sounds about him, he endures the uproar like waves or wind. His remaining thought from yesterday: why the wisest men offered trifling intricate proofs for problems of greatest importance, proofs true but resembling fallacies. Zeno's syllogism on drunkenness: no one entrusts a secret to a drunken man; but one will entrust a secret to a good man; therefore, the good man will not get drunk. Posidonius defends it by distinguishing the man actually drunk from the habitual drunkard; Seneca calls it a fallacy, the premise means actually drunk, not about to get drunk; a man actually drunk may be drunk for the first time without the habit, while the drunkard is often sober; using an equivocal word to smuggle in a fallacy is wrong when truth is the object; even granting Zeno meant the habitual drunkard, the conclusion is false, many not-always-sober soldiers are entrusted with secret messages by generals and centurions. Tillius Cimber was trusted no less than Cassius, though Cassius drank water all his life and Cimber was a sot and brawler, I carry a master, I cannot carry my liquor. L. Piso, Director of Public Safety, drunk from the time of his appointment, spent nights at banquets, slept till noon, yet applied himself most diligently to his official duties; Augustus trusted him with secret orders in Thrace, Tiberius with the city; Tiberius then made Cossus city prefect, a grave, moderate man but soaked in drink, once carried home from the Senate overcome by slumber. Cossus never let a secret slip, though the whole senate trusted him; abolish such harangues, drunkenness looses secrets like new wine bursting vats, raising the dregs; but we also take counsel with known drinkers on serious matters, so Zeno's premise is false; better to arraign drunkenness frankly and expose its vices, the middling good man avoids them, the perfect sage is content to slake his thirst, and if carried too far by good cheer stops short of drunkenness. Drunkenness is nothing but a condition of insanity purposely assumed, prolong it days and it is madness, no less now, merely shorter; Alexander stabbed his dearest friend Clitus at a banquet, then wished to die, and assuredly ought to have died; drunkenness kindles and discloses every vice, removes shame, drunkenness does not create vice, it merely brings it into view; lust, lechery, spite all given free play; we forget who we are, words halting, glance unsteady, step falters, head dizzy, the very ceiling whirls, stomach tortured by swelling wine. Public calamities of drunkenness, betrayed spirited nations, breached walls, conquered invincible men by the wine-cup; Alexander passed safely through marches, battles, winters, rivers, seas, intemperance in drinking laid him low, the famous death-dealing bowl of Hercules. What glory in carrying much liquor, when you outlast all banqueters, you are vanquished by the cask. Mark Antony ruined by drunkenness and love of Cleopatra, made an enemy of the state, made cruel by drink, gleefully inspecting Cicero's severed head and hand; cruelty follows wine-bibbing; continued bouts bestialize the soul. State why the wise man ought not get drunk by facts not words, demonstrate that pleasures are punishments once they exceed bounds; if logic could prove the wise man keeps his course while drunk, it could prove he will not die of poison or sleep on a sleeping-potion.

LXXXIV, On Gathering Ideas, pp. 277-285: journeys shake out his laziness, profitable for health and studies; reading indispensable, to keep him from being satisfied with himself alone, to judge others' discoveries and reflect on discoveries yet to be made; reading nourishes the mind and refreshes it when wearied with study; do not confine oneself to writing or reading alone, blend them, so reading's fruits are reduced to concrete form by the pen; imitate the bees, who flit about, cull flowers for honey, arrange and assort them in cells. Sift what we gathered from varied reading, keep separate, then blend the several flavours into one delicious compound that, even though it betrays its origin, is clearly a different thing from whence it came, as nature does in our bodies without labour: food we take is transformed into blood, energy, strength. Food unassimilated is a burden; so with reading, digest what is absorbed, or it stays in the memory, not the reasoning power; welcome such foods, make them our own, so something one may be formed out of many, as one number from lesser sums; the mind should hide away all the materials by which it has been aided, bring to light only what it has made of them; resemble the admired model as a child resembles his father, not as a picture resembles its original, for a picture is a lifeless thing; if the copy is true, the source may be unrecognisable; a chorus, many voices, one voice results, tenor, bass. I would have my mind of such a quality as this, many arts, precepts, patterns from many epochs, blended into one; accomplished by constant effort and doing nothing without reason's approval; reason says abandon riches, a danger or a burden, pleasures, they soften and weaken, office, swollen, idle, empty, no goal, afflicted with twofold envy, envied and envying; behold the homes of the great, thresholds uproarious with brawling callers. Pass by the steps to rich men's houses, porches hazardous from the huge throng, standing not merely on a precipice but on slippery ground; direct your course to wisdom, whose ways are surpassing peace and plenty; whatever seems conspicuous in human affairs, however petty, is approached by a difficult, toilsome path; the road to greatness is rough, but if you desire to scale the peak far above Fortune's range, you will look down on all men regard as most lofty, yet proceed to the top over level ground.

LXXXV, On Some Vain Syllogisms, pp. 285-309: Seneca had spared Lucilius the knotty problems, giving a taste of the school's proofs that virtue alone suffices for the happy life; now Lucilius bids him include the whole bulk of Stoic syllogisms and those devised by other schools to belittle them, the result will be a book, instead of a letter; Seneca declares again he takes no pleasure in such proofs, is ashamed to enter the lists armed with logic. The Stoic syllogism, he that possesses prudence is also self-restrained, is happy, therefore, the prudent man is happy, and prudence is sufficient to constitute the happy life; certain Peripatetics reply by interpreting unperturbed, unwavering, free from sadness as rarely perturbed and only moderately, the wise man is not overcome by grief but merely touched by it; they moderate passions, not abolish them; Seneca scoffs, what petty superiority to be merely braver than the most craven, happier than the most dejected; would Ladas boast his swiftness comparing himself with the halt and weak. Speed must be estimated by its own standard, not by comparison with the slowest; a man with a light fever is not well; good health means not moderate illness. In the good man, not gradual weeding-out of evils but complete absence; small evils grow and hamper, like a medium cataract dulling vision. If the wise man has any passions, reason is no match for them, carried along on a rushing stream; a throng of moderate passions affects him more than one powerful one, moderate craving for money, not-fully-aroused ambition, appeasable temper, mild inconstancy, non-violent lust; better one full-fledged vice than all vices mildly. It makes no difference how great the passion is; no matter what its size may be, it knows not how to obey. The passions, like wild beasts, do not obey reason however slight they are; tigers and lions never lose their wildness, vices are never genuinely tamed; easier to stop passions at the start than control them once they gather force; moderation in the passions is like being moderately insane or ill; virtue alone has moderation; chronic vices, greed, cruelty, wantonness, are beyond control, and passions lead into them; grant any privilege to sadness, fear, desire and they leave our jurisdiction, growing with their external causes. If the passions' beginnings are outside our control, it is folly to believe their endings are within it; easier to keep a thing out than to keep it under once let in. A wise man who fears would go to battle reluctantly and with flagging spirit, such inconsistency does not suit the wise; keep two principles separate: first, the only good is the honourable; second, virtue alone suffices for happiness, prove each separately; Xenocrates and Speusippus say a man can be happy by virtue alone yet deny the honourable is the only good; Epicurus says the virtuous man is happy, but virtue alone insufficient because pleasure resulting from virtue makes him happy, a futile distinction, since virtue never exists without pleasure and is inseparable from it; absurd to say a man will be happy by virtue alone yet not absolutely happy. The happy life contains a perfect good that cannot be excelled, if a man has it, life is completely happy; if the life of the gods contains nothing greater, the happy life is divine; if it wants nothing, every happy life is perfect, happy, and most happy, no degrees of happiness; the happy man prefers no other's life to his own, and the more prudent he is the more he strives after the best, how can one be happy who can still crave something else. The happy life is a unit, its essence, not extent, sets it on the noblest plane; complete equality between long and short lives; its distinctive quality is fullness, satiety is the limit of eating and drinking; less happy is not happy; the brave man is fearless, the fearless free from sadness, the free from sadness happy. A lesser vice is not a virtue; fear in slight degree is still wickedness, milder only in form; the brave man will not fear death, imprisonment, burning, he knows they only seem evils, mere bugbears; he is careful, not fearful; what is an evil, it is the yielding to those things which are called evils; it is the surrendering of one's liberty into their control, when really we ought to suffer all things in order to preserve the honourable. Bravery is not rashness or love of danger but knowledge distinguishing evil from non-evil; it takes the greatest care of itself and endures with greatest patience things with a false appearance of being evils. The brave man under torture feels pain, no virtue rids a man of feelings, but has no fear, looks down unconquered from a lofty height upon his sufferings, with the spirit of one comforting a sick friend. That which is evil does harm; that which does harm makes a man worse. But pain and poverty do not make a man worse; therefore they are not evils. The pilot's art is never made worse by the storm; the pilot promises not a prosperous voyage but serviceable performance of his task; the storm interferes not with the pilot's work but only with success, Neptune, you shall never sink this ship except on an even keel. The pilot's double role, passenger and pilot; the storm harms him as passenger, not as pilot; the pilot's art is another's good, like the physician's; but the wise man's good is a common good, belonging to his company and himself, so he is not harmed by poverty, pain, or life's storms; he is always in action, greatest when Fortune blocks him; poverty prevents him teaching how the State should be handled, but he teaches how poverty should be handled; no fortune or external circumstance can shut the wise man off from action. Phidias's statues in ivory or bronze, marble or meaner material, so the wise man develops virtue in wealth or poverty, at home or in exile, as commander or common soldier, in sound health or enfeebled; animal-tamers subdue the savage and dwell with them, so the wise man is skilled at taming evils: pain, want, disgrace, imprisonment, exile, universally to be feared.

LXXXVI, On Scipio's Villa, pp. 311-323: resting at Scipio Africanus's country-house, reverencing his spirit and altar, perhaps his tomb; his soul returned to the skies, not for commanding armies, Cambyses also did, a madman, but for moderation and duty, more admirable after withdrawal from his native land than defending her: either Scipio should remain in Rome, or Rome remain free; I go into exile, if it is true that I have grown beyond what is to your advantage. Magnanimity, voluntary exile to relieve the state of its burden. Scipio's villa, hewn stone, forest, defensive towers, a well that could supply an army, a small dark old-style bath, our ancestors did not think that one could have a hot bath except in darkness; Scipio bathed a body wearied with field work beneath a dingy roof; now we think ourselves poor if our walls lack costly mirrors, Alexandrian marble with Numidian mosaics, glass ceilings, Thasian marble pools, and silver taps. Freedmen's baths with silver spigots, statues and useless columns, crashing waterfalls, walking only on precious stones; modern baths deemed fit for moths unless sun all day through widest windows, bathing and tanning at once, looking over land and sea from the tub; early baths few, plain, invented for use not delight. Scipio condemned as a boor for bathing in muddy water to wash off sweat, not ointment; the ancients washed only arms and legs daily, bathing all over once a week, but they smelled of the camp, the farm, and heroism. Since the invention of spick-and-span baths, men are really fouler; Horace's Buccillus, reeking of perfume, now a Buccillus would smell of goat, and ointment must be renewed two or three times a day; Aegialus, the careful householder now owning the estate, taught Seneca an old tree can be transplanted, old men must learn this, since every one of them plants an olive-yard for his successor; the tree bore fruit after three or four unproductive years; Virgil's line about the slow-growing tree shading grandsons, Virgil sought not truth but appropriateness, to please the reader not teach the farmer. Virgil's sowing advice, beans in spring, clover, millet yearly care; June, nearly July, farmers harvesting beans and sowing millet the same day; the olive-yard transplanted two ways, large trees with trunks cut to a foot, ball and roots cut, smeared with manure, packed down to keep out cold and wind; trunk no more than three or four feet out of ground so a thick growth comes from the bottom. Aged vine transplanted; trees should be helped with tank-water; we are our own rain-makers; no more precepts, lest Aegialus trained a competitor in me and I train one in you.

LXXXVII, Some Arguments in Favour of the Simple Life, pp. 323-349: I was shipwrecked before I got aboard, the journey showed how much we possess that is superfluous, and how easily we can do away with things. Maximus and Seneca's two happy days, few slaves, mattress on the ground, two rugs, luncheon an hour, dried figs and writing tablets always, figs make a New Year feast every day; the soul greatest when it has laid aside extraneous things, fearing nothing, craving nothing; the farmer's cart, mules barely alive, barefoot driver, Seneca blushes at the rattle-trap, proof his progress is still insufficient; he has not yet the courage openly to acknowledge thriftiness. The rich man is poor if he owes all he has, reckon men's real worth by elimination of debts; what good are fat mules in uniform livery, decorated chariots, steeds decked with purple, golden harness, neither master nor mule is improved by such trappings. Cato the Censor rode a donkey carrying saddle-bags with his necessaries, O how I should love to see him meet to-day on the road one of our coxcombs, with his outriders and Numidians, and a great cloud of dust before him, a general who had triumphed, a censor, a Cato, content with a single nag, and less than a whole nag; the man who first called such things hindrances, impedimenta, had a prophetic inkling. Then a few Stoic syllogisms: that which is good makes men good. For example, that which is good in the art of music makes the musician. But chance events do not make a man good; therefore chance events are not goods. Peripatetic replies, that which is good makes men good challenged by music's instruments; Seneca's distinction: instruments assist performance, not the art itself; that which can fall to the lot of any man, no matter how base, is not a good, wealth falls to the pander and gladiator-trainer, so wealth is not a good; Peripatetics counter that goods also fall to the lowest in grammar, medicine, navigation, but those arts make no profession of greatness of soul. Virtue stands above all such things, appraised in coin of its own minting; money tumbles into certain men's hands like a shilling down a sewer, Chelidon, Cleopatra's eunuch, great wealth; Natalis, shameless tongue, heir of many and made many his heirs. A strong-box is worth just what it holds; owners of great estates are only accessories of their possessions; the wise man is great because he has a great soul; what falls to even the most despicable person is not a good. Inactivity is not a good, tree-frog and flea possess it; rest and freedom from trouble are not goods; what makes the wise man, that which produces a god, an element of godliness, heavenliness, grandeur; the good does not come to everyone, nor allow any random person to possess it. Products apportioned to countries, Tmolus saffron, India ivory, Sheba incense, Chalybes iron, so the Supreme Good has its own abode: the soul, pure and holy, room in it for God. Good does not result from evil. But riches result from greed; therefore, riches are not a good. Peripatetic reply: money comes from sacrilege and theft too, but those are evil only because they work more evil than good, gain accompanied by fear, anxiety, torture of mind and body. Good cannot spring from evil, sacrilege, theft, adultery counted among goods is monstrous; petty sacrilege is punished, grand sacrilege honoured with a triumph; if sacrilege were partly good it would be honourable and right conduct; the worst punishment for crime lies in the crime itself, crime is punished immediately, at the moment committed; good does not spring from evil any more than figs from olive-trees; the honourable and the good are identical. The gold-and-serpent jar, profit from sacrilege is not the origin of the money; the gold in the jar beside the serpent is not evil, the serpent is; so in sacrilege, the crime is the evil, the profit is not. But Seneca disagrees: gold can be taken without the serpent, but profit cannot be made without committing sacrilege, gain is blended with crime. Riches are not goods but positive evils per the objector, Seneca replies they are antecedent causes, not efficient causes, riches inflate the spirit, breed pride, invite envy, estrange the mind until reputation for wealth delights though harmful; goods are pure, do not corrupt the spirit, give confidence without swelling, while riches give audacity; Posidonius's syllogism: things bestowing no greatness, confidence, or freedom from care are not goods, riches and health do none of these, so not goods. Advantages vs. goods, an advantage contains more usefulness than annoyance; a good must be unmixed, no harmful element, a thing is not good if it contains more benefit than injury, but only if it contains nothing but benefit; advantages can be predicated of animals, imperfect men, fools; a good belongs to the wise man alone, without alloy. One knot left, a knot for a Hercules: good does not result from evil. But riches result from numerous cases of poverty; therefore, riches are not a good, the Peripatetics concoct and solve it; Antipater's refutation: poverty denotes not possession but non-possession, deprivation; poverty states not what a man has but what he has not, no fullness can result from a multitude of voids; many positive things, not many deficiencies, make up riches. Better to mitigate poverty and relieve wealth of arrogance than quibble about words; suppose a bill to abolish riches, syllogisms will not persuade the Roman people to demand poverty, the foundation of their empire, and fear wealth found among conquered victims; the diatribe on Rome's corruption; if we can, let us speak more boldly; if not, let us speak more frankly.

LXXXVIII, On Liberal and Vocational Studies, pp. 349-375: you have been wishing to know my views with regard to liberal studies. My answer is this: I respect no study, and deem no study good, which results in money-making, they are our apprenticeship, not our real work. But there is only one really liberal study, that which gives a man his liberty. It is the study of wisdom, that is lofty, brave, and great-souled. All other studies are puny and puerile. We ought not to be learning such things; we should have done with learning them. The scholar busies himself with language, history, poetry, but which of these paves the way to virtue. If they teach virtue they are philosophers; they clearly do not teach the same thing, Homer is made Stoic, Epicurean, Peripatetic, Academic by turns, disproving the thesis by the very arguments used to prove it. Whether Homer or Hesiod was older is as pointless as why Hecuba, younger than Helen, aged worse; what use to know the ages of Achilles and Patroclus, or where Ulysses strayed, instead of stopping ourselves straying. Our spirits are daily tossed by storms; beauty tempts our eyes, enemies assail us, savage monsters, treacherous allurements of the ear, shipwreck; show me how to love my country, wife, father, and how even after shipwreck to sail toward honourable ends; why ask whether Penelope was chaste or whether she suspected Ulysses before she knew him. Teach me what purity is and whether it sits in body or soul; teach my soul harmony with itself, not treble and bass; teach me not to utter a doleful note amid adversity; the mathematician should teach me how much is enough for a man to own, not how to lay out estates; counting adapts fingers to avarice, teach that there is no point in such calculations, that no one is happier for tiring out book-keepers with his possessions; what good is parceling land if I cannot share with my brother. What good is measuring an acre exactly if an ill-tempered neighbour scraping a bit of my land embittered me. The mathematician teaches me to lose none of my boundaries; I seek to learn to lose them all with a light heart. I am being driven from the farm which my father and grandfather owned, who owned the land before your grandfather. The land is public property belonging to mankind; the geometrician's skill is marvellous but measuring the circle is nothing, measure me the mind of man, tell me how great it is, or how puny. Knowing a straight line is useless without knowing what is straight in life; the astrologer boasting knowledge of Saturn and Mercury, what benefit to know this, rather than learn the stars are propitious wherever they are, driven by an unending round of destiny. If the stars cause whatever happens, foreseeing the immutable is useless; if they merely indicate, what good is foreseeing what you cannot escape. Behold the fleeting sun, the stars that follow in his train, or be misled by nights without a cloud, enough has been ordained that I shall be safe from misleading; what, does the morrow never play me false. Whatever happens without my knowledge plays me false. I do not know what is to be, but I do know what may come to be, I await the future in its entirety, ready for favourable events, prepared for evil. Painting, sculpture, marble-working are not liberal arts, helpers of luxury; wrestling, oil and mud, perfumers, cooks likewise excluded. What liberal in emetic-taking athletes, bodies fat, minds thin. The ancestors taught children to hurl a spear, wield a pike, guide a horse, handle weapons, nothing learned lying down; but neither new system nor old teaches or nourishes virtue. What good to guide a horse and control its speed, then find our own uncurbed passions bolting with us. To beat many in wrestling or boxing, then be beaten by anger. Liberal studies contribute much in other respects, nothing as regards virtue; low-grade arts contribute greatly to life's equipment but nothing to virtue; we educate children in liberal studies not because they can bestow virtue but because they prepare the soul for the reception of virtue, as the primary course, grammar, prepares the ground, so liberal arts merely set the soul going in virtue's direction. Posidonius divides the arts into four classes: common and low, workmen's hand-work, equipping life, no pretence of beauty or honour; arts of amusement, pleasing eye and ear, stage-machinists; arts of boys' education, somewhat similar to liberal arts, Greeks call cycle of studies; and liberal arts, only those are really liberal, or rather free, whose concern is virtue. Food aids the body but is not part of it; mathematics aids philosophy, as carpenter aids mathematician, but neither is part of the other; the wise man knows causes, the mathematician computes numbers; the philosopher demonstrates the sun is a large body, the astronomer computes how large, needing borrowed first principles; philosophy builds on its own soil, mathematics on alien soil. Only the unalterable knowledge of good and evil brings the soul to perfection; no other art investigates good and evil; review of virtues: bravery scorns what inspires fear, challenges and crushes terrors and all that would drive our freedom under the yoke, do liberal studies strengthen it. Loyalty is the holiest good in the human heart, burn me, slay me, kill me, I shall not betray my trust; and the more urgently torture shall seek to find my secret, the deeper in my heart will I bury it, can liberal arts produce such a spirit. Temperance controls desires, knows the best measure of appetites is not what you want to take but what you ought to take. Kindliness forbids over-bearing and grasping; it counts no evil as another's solely; loves its own good chiefly because it will some day be the good of another. Liberal studies no more teach simplicity, moderation, self-restraint, thrift, economy, kindliness than they teach such character; the objection, since you declare virtue cannot be attained without liberal studies, how deny they offer assistance, answered: virtue cannot be attained without food either, yet food has nothing to do with virtue; wood does not assist a ship, though a ship cannot be built except of wood; wisdom is even attainable without liberal studies, virtue must be learned, but not by means of these studies; wisdom is not in letters, wisdom communicates facts and not words; wisdom is large and spacious, needs free room, learn divine and human things, past and future, ephemeral and eternal, and Time. The soul's questions, where it comes from, its nature, when it begins, how long it exists, whether it changes habitation from animal shape to animal shape, is a slave but once, is corporeal or not, what becomes of it when it ceases to use us, how it will employ its freedom when it escapes this prison, whether it will forget its past and begin to know itself when released from the body and withdrawn to the skies; virtue will not surrender herself to narrow bounds, a great subject needs wide space in which to move; drive out all else, empty the breast to receive virtue; keep only as much of the arts as is essential, wanting to know more than is sufficient is a sort of intemperance. The pursuit of liberal arts makes men troublesome, wordy, tactless, self-satisfied bores who fail to learn essentials because they learned non-essentials; Didymus wrote 4,000 books, pity him if he had read as many superfluous volumes, investigating Homer's birthplace, Aeneas's mother, Anacreon rake or drunkard, Sappho a bad lot; come now, do not tell me that life is long; many of our own countrymen's works ought to be cut down with the axe; what a learned man you are, vs. what a good man you are; shall I unroll annals, count years between Orpheus and Homer, study Aristarchus's absurdities, wear my life away upon syllables, wallow in geometrician's dust, have I forgotten save your time. Apion's claim Homer added a prefatory poem to Iliad and Odyssey; measure the years of your life, they have no room for these things; philosophers too have descended to syllable divisions, envying scholars and mathematicians, they know more about careful speaking than about careful living; over-nice exactness is an enemy of truth, Protagoras, Nausiphanes, Parmenides; Zeno of Elea declares nothing exists; Pyrrhonean, Megarian, Eretrian, Academic schools introduce the new knowledge of non-knowledge; sweep these theories in with the superfluous troops of liberal studies, one class gives knowledge of no use, the other takes away hope of attaining knowledge; better to know useless things than nothing; if Protagoras, nothing in nature is not doubtful; Nausiphanes, only this is sure, everything is unsure; Parmenides, nothing except the One; Zeno, not even the One; what are we, then, the whole universe is then a vain or deceptive shadow. I cannot say whether I am more vexed at those who would have it that we know nothing, or those who would not leave us even this privilege.

LXXXIX, On the Parts of Philosophy, pp. 377-393: it is a useful fact that you wish to know, one which is essential to him who hastens after wisdom. The parts of philosophy; studying parts leads more easily to understanding the whole; he wishes philosophy might come before the eyes in all her unity, as the firmament, then she would ravish all mortals with love, and we should abandon things believed great in ignorance; since that cannot be, view her as men gaze upon the secrets of the firmament; the wise man's mind embraces the whole framework; divide philosophy into parts, not scraps, for over-analysis is faulty in precisely the same way as no analysis at all; whatever you cut so fine that it becomes dust is as good as blended into a mass again. Wisdom vs. philosophy, wisdom is the perfect good of the human mind; philosophy is the love of wisdom, the endeavour to attain it; the latter strives toward the goal the former has reached; definitions: knowledge of things divine and human, adding their causes superfluous, study of virtue, study of amending the mind, search for right reason; as avarice differs from wealth, so philosophy differs from wisdom, wisdom is philosophy's result and reward; philosophy does the going, and wisdom is the goal. Philosophy and virtue inseparable, philosophy cannot exist without virtue nor virtue without philosophy; the path to virtue leads by way of virtue herself. Three divisions: moral, keeps the soul in order; natural, investigates the universe; rational, works out meanings of words, combinations, proofs keeping falsehood from displacing truth; Peripatetics added a fourth, civil philosophy. Other divisions, some add economics, household management, others kinds of life; all fall under moral philosophy. Epicureans: philosophy twofold, natural and moral, no rational; then forced to distinguish equivocal ideas and expose fallacies, they reintroduced it as forensic and regulative, merely rational under another name, accessory to natural. Cyrenaics abolished natural and rational, content with moral alone, yet include under another title what they rejected, dividing moral philosophy into five parts: first, what to avoid and seek; second, passions; third, actions; fourth, causes; fifth, proofs, causes belong to natural, proofs to rational. Aristo of Chios: natural and rational not only superfluous but contradictory; limited moral to what was, Aristo cut off the advice part of moral philosophy, saying it belongs to the pedagogue, not the philosopher, as if the wise man were anything else than the pedagogue of the human race. Philosophy being threefold, first set in order the moral side: three parts, first, speculative, assigning each thing its function and weighing its worth, most useful; second, impulse; third, actions, first determine what things are worth, second conceive a regulated and ordered impulse, third make impulse and actions harmonize, so you are consistent with yourself; if any of the three is defective, confusion follows, knowing values is useless if impulses run to excess; curbing impulses useless if you do not know the proper times and seasons; knowing the moment is distinct from knowing values, and distinct from curbing impulses. Natural philosophy twofold, bodily and non-bodily, bodies divided into creative and created, created things are the elements; rational philosophy, speech continuous, rhetoric, or split between questioner and answerer, dialectic; dialectic divided into words and meanings; subdivisions vast, he stops here and follows the climax of the story, else his letter would become a debater's handbook; read on the subject provided you promptly relate all to conduct, hold your conduct in check, rouse what is languid, bind fast what has become relaxed, conquer the obstinate, persecute your appetites and the appetites of mankind; speak remedies all the more because objections are offered, medicine begins to do good at the time when a touch makes the diseased body tingle with pain, I shall utter words that will help men even against their will. At times hear hard truths collectively since individuals will not hear them singly. Then the diatribe on greed and luxury, estates that held a nation too narrow for a single lord, noble rivers flowing through private grounds, seas surrounded by estates, steward ruling beyond Adriatic, Ionian, Aegean, islands reckoned as paltry possessions; make whatever you can your own, provided only that it is more than your neighbour's, builders' luxury; gluttons, poor wretches, do you not know that your appetites are bigger than your bellies, talk this way to others provided while you talk you also listen; write in this way.

XC, On the Part Played by Philosophy in the Progress of Man, pp. 395-429: life is the gift of the gods, living well the gift of philosophy; the gods gave the faculty of acquiring wisdom to all, knowledge to none, wisdom is not one of Fortune's gifts; each man owes her to himself; her sole function is to discover the truth about things divine and human; religion, duty, justice, and the whole company of virtues cling to her. Philosophy taught men to worship the divine and love the human, dominion with the gods and fellowship among men; the fellowship lasted until avarice tore it asunder and caused poverty even among the richest, men cease to possess all the moment they desire all for their own; the first men followed nature, one leader and law, trusting a better man; nature subjects the weaker to the stronger; among beasts the biggest or fiercest lead, the tallest elephant, the strongest bull; among men the best is regarded the highest; so the ruler was chosen by mind, and happiness rested where no man could be more powerful unless better; Posidonius holds the golden age was ruled by the wise. Wise kings kept hands under control, protected the weak, gave advice, forethought provided, bravery warded off dangers, kindness enriched subjects, for them ruling was a service, not an exercise of royalty; the greatest threat was departure from the kingdom; when vice stole in and kingdoms became tyrannies, laws arose, framed by the wise, Solon, Lycurgus, Zaleucus, Charondas learned justice in Pythagoras's silent retreat; up to this point I agree with Posidonius; but that philosophy discovered the arts of which life makes use in its daily round, I refuse to admit, nor will I ascribe to it an artisan's glory. Philosophy did not invent the shrewdly-contrived multi-story dwellings, fish-preserves, keys and bolts, a hint to avarice; believe me, that was a happy age, before the days of architects, before the days of builders, primal man split wood with wedges, forked poles propping houses, branches and leaves heaped for the roof. The thatched roof covered free men; under marble and gold dwells slavery; Posidonius's claim that mechanical tools were invented by wise men is refuted, ingenuity, not wisdom, discovered traps, birdlime, hunting with dogs, mining, the hammer and tongs; the wise man was easy-going in his way of living; how can you consistently admire both Diogenes and Daedalus, the saw's inventor or the man who broke his cup on seeing a boy drink from his hand. Diogenes broke his wooden cup on seeing a boy drink from his hand, fool that I am, to have been carrying superfluous baggage all this time, curled up in his tub and slept; which is wiser, the inventor of saffron-spraying pipes, canals, revolving dining-room ceilings, or the man who shows nature has laid no stern law on us, we can live without marble-cutter and engineer, clothe ourselves without silk, have all necessaries content with what earth placed on its surface; if mankind listened, they would know the cook is as superfluous as the soldier; those wise men found care of the body easy, necessaries require no elaborate pains, only luxuries call for labour; follow nature, need no skilled craftsmen; nature equipped us for whatever she forced on us, skins of beasts protect from cold, many tribes cover bodies with bark of trees. Clothes, bird feathers sewn together; Scythians in fox and mouse skins; shelter, caves, wicker mats smeared with mud, stubble roofs, dug-out houses on the Syrtes; nature was not hostile, all things were ready at birth; we made everything difficult through disdain for what is easy. Nature suffices for what she demands; luxury turned her back on nature, first lusting for superfluities, then contraries to nature, finally making the soul a bondsman of the body; the crafts of weavers, carpenters, cooks, and teachers of wanton postures all serve the body as a master now; wanting only what is enough is now a sign of boorishness and destitution; Posidonius, great contributor to philosophy, is lured by eloquence into describing weaving in detail, the wise man supposedly invented weaving, Ovid's quote on warp and woof, and farming, ploughing, sowing, weeding, even the mill, the sage imitating nature's processes began to make bread, grain crushed by flinty teeth. The millstone imitating teeth, bread, ovens, Posidonius nearly declared even the cobbler's trade the wise man's discovery; Seneca's reply: reason devised all these, but not right reason; man, not the wise man, discovered them, as ships modeled on fish steering by their tails; Posidonius: the wise man discovered them but they were too petty to handle himself, so he entrusted them to others. Inventions, window-glass, vaulted baths, marble, rounded stone masses, shorthand, are the lowest grade of slaves' devisings; wisdom's seat is higher; she trains not the hands, but is mistress of our minds; wisdom brings not graceful poses or flute notes, nor arms, walls, war instruments, her voice is for peace, and she summons all mankind to concord; she is the skilled artisan of life, with the other arts under her dominion. Wisdom's course toward happiness, shows what things are evil and seemingly evil, strips minds of vain illusion, bestows substantial greatness, represses inflated showy greatness, delivers knowledge of the whole of nature and her own nature, discloses the gods, nether gods, household deities, protecting spirits, souls admitted to the second class of divinities; her initiation rites unlock the vast temple of all the gods, the universe itself; then back to beginnings of things, eternal Reason imparted to the whole, the force in all seeds; then inquiry about the soul, whence it comes, where it dwells, how long, into how many divisions; finally from corporeal to incorporeal, examining truth and its argument. The wise man never took up those arts at all, he would have judged nothing worth discovering that he would not afterwards judge worth using always; Anacharsis's potter's wheel, even if the wise man invented it, he did not invent it qua wise man, just as a wise man fleet of foot outstrips runners by virtue of being fleet, not by wisdom; these discoveries have been made since we men have ceased to discover wisdom. Democritus's arch claim false, bridges and gateways existed before him; Democritus also discovered softening ivory and turning pebbles to emeralds by boiling, same process used today; even if a wise man discovered such things, not qua wise man; he does many things as well or better done by men lacking sagacity. What did the wise man discover, truth and nature first, then the law of life, teaching us to follow the gods and welcome chance as divine commands, forbid false opinions, weigh values truly, condemn pleasures mixed with remorse, praise goods that always satisfy, publish that he is most happy who has no need of happiness, and that he is most powerful who has power over himself. The true philosophy counts nothing good except the honourable, cannot be cajoled by gifts of man or fortune, cannot be bought for any value; that such philosophy existed in that rude age when useful things were learned by use alone, Seneca refuses to believe; next came the fortunate period when nature's bounties lay open to all before avarice and luxury broke the bonds; the men of the second age were not wise men though they did what wise men should do; no condition of the human race anyone would regard more highly, no ploughman tilled the soil, men shared their gains, and earth more freely gave her riches to her sons who sought them not. The golden age, avarice broke in, made all things the property of others, introduced poverty by craving much and losing all; when there is no more that we can do, we shall possess much; but we once possessed the whole world, the soil more productive untilled, goods shared among unquarrelling friends, no stronger laying hands on the weaker, each caring for his neighbour as for himself. The golden age, men in dense groves, tranquil nights without a sigh under the open sky; care vexes us in our purple; their homes were in accordance with nature, free air, clear springs, meadows beautiful without art; therein it was a joy to live, fearing neither the dwelling itself nor for its safety. Golden-age men were not wise men, the title reserved for highest achievement; lofty in spirit, fresh from the gods; the world's progeny better before it was worn out; nature does not bestow virtue, it is an art to become good; they dug no gold, silver, or transparent stones, were merciful to dumb animals; innocence through ignorance is not virtue, knowing justice, prudence, self-control, bravery requires training; virtue not vouchsafed unless the soul has been trained and taught, by unremitting practice brought to perfection, for the attainment of this boon, but not in the possession of it, were we born.

XCII, On the Happy Life, pp. 447-471: outward things sought for the body's satisfaction, the body cherished for the soul's sake; in the soul are ministering parts and a primary part with irrational and rational elements; the happy life depends on perfect reason alone, the one good never impaired. What is the happy life, it is peace of mind, and lasting tranquillity, reached by a complete view of truth, order, measure, fitness, and a will inoffensive, kindly, intent upon reason; the wise man's soul ought to be such as would be proper for a god; nothing more can be desired by one possessing all honourable things; the absurdity of the Supreme Good admitting increase, like not being content with daylight unless increased by a tiny fire. What good is a spark in the midst of this clear sunlight, if not contented with the honourable alone, one must add either quiet, undisturbedness, attainable anyway, the mind free from disturbance when fully free to contemplate the universe, or pleasure, the good of cattle, adding the irrational to the rational; the man whose Supreme Good is flavours, colours, sounds should herd with dumb brutes; the irrational soul is twofold, spirited and ambitious, better, worthier of a man, yet neglected, and lowly and sluggish, devoted to pleasure, regarded as indispensable, they made the Supreme Good a monstrous hybrid of rational, spirited, and appetitive parts, like the Scylla monster. Virtue is man's primary art; the useless, fleeting flesh is joined to it; quiet removes hindrances, pleasure dissolves and softens the soul's vigour; what elements so inharmonious as these can be found united, the most vigorous joined to the most sluggish, the austere to the far from serious, the most holy to the unrestrained even to impurity. Objector: shall you not seek good health, rest, freedom from pain if they do not hinder virtue, of course I shall seek them, not because they are goods, I shall seek them because they are according to nature and because they will be acquired through the exercise of good judgment on my part. What, then, will be good in them, this alone, that it is a good thing to choose them. The good is in the deliberate choice that observes a mean conforming with reason, not the dinner, walk, or dress; man by nature a neat and well-groomed animal, choice of neat attire is a good in itself only as choice; the body is the soul's cloak, the scabbard does not make the sword good or bad; the objector's wise man happy but not perfectly happy without health concedes the harder point, the man in unremitting extreme pain is happy, while denying the lighter. Obstacles take nothing from virtue, only dim its brilliance; disasters, losses, wrongs have the same power over virtue as a cloud over the sun; the neither wretched nor happy objector is refuted, putting chance results on par with virtues, honourable things above contemptible, a sturdy leg, strong shoulders, good teeth, healthy muscles; the wise man's life cannot be neither desirable nor undesirable. The lukewarm-water refutation, adding cold makes cold, adding hot makes hot, but adding miseries to the neither happy nor wretched man never makes him wretched, so the figure fails; and blindness, crippling, unceasing severe pain render him wretched, so if these evils do not drag him from happiness, they cannot; if the wise man cannot fall from happiness to wretchedness, he cannot fall into non-happiness; the happy life cannot be destroyed or disjointed, virtue is itself sufficient for the happy life. The happy life does not grow with a longer life free of pain; the life lived longer and pain-free is not better or more honourable, therefore not happier, in order to live more happily, he must live more rightly; if he cannot do that, then he cannot live more happily either. Virtue cannot be strained tighter, therefore neither can the happy life, which depends on virtue; virtue is so great a good it is not affected by shortness of life, pain, bodily vexations; pleasure does not deserve even a glance from virtue; virtue's chief quality is needing no day beyond the present, not reckoning up days, in the slightest possible moment of time virtue completes an eternity of good; Epicurus again, in extreme pain on his happiest day, why should virtue's goods seem incredible when even pleasure's votaries declare the wise man happy amid excessive pain and misfortune. Reason is common to gods and men, perfected in them, perfectible in us; our vices bring us to despair; man is second-class rational being, a guardian too unstable to hold fast to the best, judgment still wavering; he may require sight, hearing, health, unloathsome bodily exterior, longer life with unimpaired constitution; though reason can lead a life without regrets, there resides in imperfect man a power making for badness, a mind easily moved to perversity; suppose the open badness is removed, he is still not a good man, but being moulded to goodness; one in whom any quality making for goodness is lacking is bad. The man in whom virtue dwells is equal to the gods, striving to return to his origin; all this universe which encompasses us is one, and it is God; we are associates of God; we are his members. Our soul has capabilities, and is carried thither, if vices do not hold it down. It would be a great task to journey heavenwards; the soul but returns thither; gold and silver are worthy of the gloom they once lay in, valued by the mire of ancient days, not by sheen; riches are stored elsewhere than men's treasure-houses, the soul, not the strong-box, should be filled; the soul may be set in dominion over all things, owner of the universe, limiting its riches only by the boundaries of the sky; the soul, freed from the body, regards the body's destination, fire, stone, earth, wild beasts, as no more its concern than the afterbirth is to a child just born; it fears no post-mortem threats, I am not frightened by the executioner's hook, nor by the revolting mutilation of the corpse which is exposed to the scorn of those who would witness the spectacle. I ask no man to perform the last rites for me; I entrust my remains to none. Nature has made provision that none shall go unburied. Time will lay away one whom cruelty has cast forth. Thesis for Volume III: the only good is the honourable, virtue, perfect reason, a free mind, an upright mind, subjecting other things to itself and itself to nothing. Everything else, length of life, wealth, renown, pleasure, health, place, is indifferent, and Fortune's gifts are only raw material that the soul turns to good or ill.

XCIII, On the Quality, as Contrasted with the Length, of Life, pp. 3-11: on the death of the philosopher Metronax, Seneca reproves Lucilius for railing at Fate, we should strive, not to live long, but to live rightly. A life is long if it is full; a person like him has not lived, he has merely tarried awhile in life. Measure lives not because of their width but because of their weight.

XCIV, On the Value of Advice, pp. 11-59: against Aristo's claim that precepts are old wives' precepts, Seneca defends the preceptorial department: doctrines remove error, but precepts refresh memory, rouse attention, and guide conduct in particular cases, we are indeed uplifted merely by meeting wise men. Alexander, Pompey, and Caesar show how conquerors are conquered by their own greed.

XCV, On the Usefulness of Basic Principles, pp. 59-105: precepts alone cannot make a wise man; doctrines are needed as the roots to the branches, the star by which sailors steer. Luxury has multiplied diseases as it multiplied dishes, count the cooks, and philosophy must likewise take on strength against the age's madness.

XCVI, On Facing Hardships, pp. 105-107: the only real evil is chafing and complaining. I have trained myself not merely to obey God, but to agree with His decisions. Life is a battle; a long life includes all its troubles, as a long journey includes dust and mud and rain.

XCVII, On the Degeneracy of the Age, pp. 109-117: vice is the vice of mankind, not of the times, Clodius bribed his jury with money and with adultery in Cato's own presence. The first and worst penalty for sin is to have committed sin; conscience lashes the guilty even when Fortune protects them.

XCVIII, On the Fickleness of Fortune, pp. 119-129: you need never believe that anyone who depends upon happiness is happy. The soul is more powerful than Fortune; meet every mishap with Heaven decreed better. Keep in memory what was lost, to have had, never can be taken away. Mucius, Regulus, Socrates, Rutilius, Cato, Fabricius, and Tubero show what can be overcome and scorned.

XCIX, On Consolation to the Bereaved, pp. 129-149: Seneca encloses his scolding letter to Marullus on the death of his little son: rejoice that you possessed him rather than mourn that you lost him; tears may fall by Nature's force but must not be commanded or feigned; he whom you count as passed away has simply posted on ahead. He rejects Metrodorus's pleasure blended with grief.

C, On the Writings of Fabianus, pp. 149-157: Lucilius criticizes Fabianus's style; Seneca defends it, a copious flow, not a tumble of words, not careless, it was assured, building character rather than words. Behind only Cicero, Pollio, and Livy he is third in so great a field, and his fluent, noble manner inspires young men to imitate him without despair.

CI, On the Futility of Planning Ahead, pp. 159-167: Cornelius Senecio, a knight rising to wealth, died of quinsy within hours of a busy day. Count each separate day as a separate life. Maecenas's debased prayer for life at any price, even on the cross, is condemned: the point is, not how long you live, but how nobly you live.

CII, On the Intimations of Our Immortality, pp. 169-185: Lucilius's letter wakes Seneca from a dream of immortality. He answers the dialectical objections to posthumous renown as a good, distinct things, praise as mere utterance, whose good it is: one good man's judgment is universal, praise is judgment not speech, and the good belongs to both praised and praiser. The soul's homeland is the whole firmament; death is the birthday of your eternity.

CIII, On the Dangers of Association with Our Fellow-Men, pp. 187-189: from fellow-men comes man's everyday danger, instantaneous and concealed, unlike storm or fire which give warning. Take refuge in philosophy, but do not vaunt it; be wise without parade.

CIV, On Care of Health and Peace of Mind, pp. 191-211: fleeing a fever to Nomentum, Seneca learns to care for his health for Paulina's sake. Place avails nothing, you travelled in your own company. Travel cannot cure what you carry with you; live instead with Catos, Laelius, Tubero, Socrates, Zeno, Chrysippus, and Posidonius. Socrates unchanged by poverty, Xanthippe, war, and the hemlock; Cato unchanged by defeat and death. Liberty cannot be gained for nothing.

CV, On Facing the World with Confidence, pp. 213-217: hope, envy, hatred, fear, and contempt goad men to destroy men; contempt is least harmful and can be worn as a cure. Keep still, talk little with others and much with yourself. The most important contribution to peace of mind is never to do wrong, the evil conscience never has assurance, even when it escapes notice.

CVI, On the Corporeality of Virtue, pp. 217-223: the Good is corporeal, because it acts, virtues and emotions move the body, change the face, and touch. Wisdom is a plainer thing than that; use literature to improve the mind, not for the lecture-room.

CVII, On Obedience to the Universal Will, pp. 223-229: runaway slaves are no surprise, life is a long, rugged journey. Expect everything, and pay without complaint the tax of mortality. Cleanthes: the willing soul Fate leads, but the unwilling drags along.

CVIII, On the Approaches to Philosophy, pp. 229-255: study by parts, a little each day; distinguish true hearers from theatre-goers and note-takers. Attalus's teaching moved the young Seneca to lasting resolutions, no oysters, mushrooms, perfumes, wine, or baths, and to a year without animal food under Sotion and Pythagoras, abandoned at his father's request under Tiberius. The scholar notes Virgil's word flies; the philosopher hears Time flies as an oracle: catch what flees. Prove words by deeds, a sea-sick pilot in a storm.

CIX, On the Fellowship of Wise Men, pp. 255-265: the wise help the wise, quickening impulses, sharing discoveries, keeping virtues in action, like wrestlers and musicians. Renown rests on good men's judgment, glory on the many's. Always desiring and always refusing the same things.

CX, On True and False Riches, pp. 265-277: whether or not a Genius attends each man, no heavier curse exists than to be at enmity with oneself. Distinguish the necessary from the superfluous; Attalus at a banquet of gold plate left despising riches, we have water, we have porridge; let us compete in happiness with Jupiter himself. Crave nothing, and you vie with Jupiter.

CXI, On the Vanity of Mental Gymnastics, pp. 277-281: sophismata are Cicero's cavillationes, tricky subtleties that make men clever, not good. The true philosopher stands high like a mountain, greater as you draw near. Learn first to despise life; controlling it comes second.

CXII, On Reforming Hardened Sinners, pp. 281-283: the hardened friend is like an old vine that will not take the graft, flabby and hardened at once. Luxury has merely upset his stomach; judge him only when luxury is hateful to him.

CXIII, On the Vitality of the Soul and Its Attributes, pp. 283-299: are virtues living things. The school says yes; Seneca dissents with reductios, round Justice, hungry Bravery, a six-footed living verse. Teach instead what bravery and justice are: let ill repute, well won, be a delight.

CXIV, On Style as a Mirror of Character, pp. 301-319: man's speech is just like his life. Maecenas's loose, effeminate style matches his ungirt life; Sallust's vices were copied wholesale by Arruntius. The soul is king or tyrant; guard it, and reflect that you are mortal, whatever you are doing, have regard to death.

CXV, On the Superficial Blessings, pp. 319-331: seek what to write, not how; the face of virtue, if seen, would be worshipped. Men are children over pebbles, we over marble and gold; money has made us alternately merchants and merchandise. Euripides's Bellerophon was hissed for praising gold. Philosophy's boon is absence of regret for one's conduct.

CXVI, On Self-Control, pp. 333-337: moderate emotions or none. No halfway disease is wholesome; resist faults at the entrance, for they grow. Panaetius on love: the wise man is another matter, we who are far from wisdom should not trust ourselves on slippery ground. We are in love with our vices.

CXVII, On Real Ethics as Superior to Syllogistic Subtleties, pp. 337-359: is wisdom a Good but being wise not a Good. Seneca dissents from his school: both are Goods, being wise is the employment of wisdom. Such hair-splitting wastes time owed to real cure: make me braver, make me calmer, make me the equal of Fortune.

CXVIII, On the Vanity of Place-Seeking, pp. 361-371: canvass for nothing and reduce Fortune to the ranks. The Good attracts the soul in accordance with nature and is inseparable from the honourable; like the keystone, its final addition transforms the merely natural into the Good.

CXIX, Nature as Our Best Provider, pp. 371-381: borrow from yourself. Nature's wants are few, hunger is satisfied by the nearest bread, thirst by water from the nearest spring. Alexander was poor after conquering the world; money never made a man rich. Riches grip their owners; enough is never too little.

CXX, More About Virtue, pp. 381-395: we learn the Good and the honourable by analogy, from great deeds, Fabricius scorning Pyrrhus's gold and poison, Horatius Cocles on the bridge. The perfect man is consistent in all actions; the fool shifts masks, never the same, never even like himself. Play the role of one man to the end of life's drama.

CXXI, On Instinct in Animals, pp. 397-411: animals have an inborn sense of their constitution, the hen fears the hawk without experience, the tortoise rights itself, the spider weaves without teaching. This art is born, not taught. Nature's first equipment is self-love and adaptability.

CXXII, On Darkness as a Veil for Wickedness, pp. 411-423: the day-reversers, Acilius Buta, Sextus Papinius, live backwards, sleeping by day and banqueting by night, because light is the enemy of an evil conscience and notoriety is their reward. Follow Nature, all is easy and unobstructed.

CXXIII, On the Conflict Between Pleasure and Virtue, pp. 425-435: at the Alban villa, with no bread ready, hunger makes bad bread good, a good-humoured stomach is a step toward independence. Shun the pleasure-preachers' voices as Ulysses did the Sirens; make pleasant and honourable mean the same.

CXXIV, On the True Good as Attained by Reason, pp. 435-449: the Good is grasped by the understanding, not the senses; it exists in neither beasts nor children, for they lack reason. The Good is a free, upright mind; perfect reason. You will come to your own when you shall understand that those whom the world calls fortunate are really the most unfortunate of all.
