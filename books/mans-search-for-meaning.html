Man's Search for Meaning

Date: Nov 6, 2026

Author: Viktor E. Frankl

Thesis in short

A man can be stripped of everything except one freedom, the freedom to choose his attitude in any given set of circumstances. Life does not ask us what its meaning is; life questions us, and we answer by taking responsibility for the unique task set before us, in work, in love, and in suffering. Meaning is found, not invented, and it is specific to each person, day, and hour.

Overall summary

Part One is Frankl's inside account of everyday life in the camps, in three phases: shock on admission, relative apathy once entrenched, and release. Part Two is logotherapy, the Third Viennese School after Freud and Adler: the will to meaning as the primary human motive, existential frustration and noogenic neurosis, the existential vacuum of modern life, and techniques including paradoxical intention and de-reflection. The book ends with a psychiatric credo: man remains self-determining, even in psychosis, and the camps showed both potentials, some behaving like swine, others like saints.

Part One: Experiences in a Concentration Camp, pp. 3-148

Frankl says this is not a factual account of the camps, but an inside story of everyday life as reflected in the mind of the average prisoner, in mostly small camps. He warns against sentimental outsider views: prisoners were not united in brotherhood, Capos were not all monsters, life was an unrelenting struggle for bread and for life, in which saving oneself or a friend could mean another victim was found. Prisoners were reduced to numbers, his was 119104, and tattooed. Survivors often had lost all scruples, so the best of us did not return. He sets out three phases: following admission, being well entrenched in camp routine, and following release and liberation. A premium coupon was worth twelve cigarettes, or twelve soups, and a prisoner who smoked his own cigarettes had given up the will to live.

Phase 1, shock, pp. 11-30: transport to Auschwitz, 1500 people, 80 to a coach, the cry of Auschwitz, gallows and low huts, the mind becoming accustomed to horror step by step. Prisoners clung to the delusion of reprieve. At the first selection an SS officer pointed right for work or left for the gas chamber, about 90 percent sent left. His friend pointed to the chimney and said he was floating up to Heaven. Stripped in the cleansing station, Frankl lost the manuscript hidden in his haversack, and that moment struck out his whole former life. Shaving, showers, and ill-fitting clothes left only naked existence. Curiosity and detachment acted as defence, and the body proved unexpectedly endurable. The thought of suicide was brief; on his first evening he promised himself not to run into the wire. A veteran colleague advised him not to fear selections, to shave daily and look fit for work, and explained the Moslem, the emaciated prisoner who could no longer do hard labour and would soon be gassed.

Phase 2, relative apathy, pp. 31-132: apathy, or emotional death, was the necessary protective shell of the mind. Longing for home continued, but feeling was blunted: he watched unmoved as a comrade was beaten, and saw a twelve-year-old's frostbitten toes picked off without horror or pity. Beatings hurt less from physical pain than from mental agony at their injustice, and the most painful part was the insult they implied. Indignation could still rouse a prisoner, not cruelty or pain. A foreman at 2 degrees Fahrenheit ordered Frankl to dig frozen topsoil with his teeth; Frankl answered he was a doctor who had worked for no money in clinics for the poor, and was knocked down. A Capo who owed Frankl for listening to his love and marriage troubles saved his life by reserving him a place in the first rows. The ordinary laborer lived on 10.5 ounces of bread and 1 and 1/3 pints of soup a day, threatened with death continuously. Inner life regressed: dreams were wish-fulfilment of bread, cake, cigarettes, and warm baths, and waking was the terrible contrast. Food dominated talk, rations were watery soup once daily and a small piece of bread, bodies devoured themselves while hut members died one after another. Prisoners touched bread in their pockets tenderly, breaking off a crumb and pocketing it again. On transfer from Auschwitz to a Dachau-affiliated camp, the train passed through Vienna at midnight, past the street and house where Frankl was born, seen with the eyes of a dead man who had come back from another world. Cultural life hibernated except for politics and religion: politics was rumor-based, optimistic rumors repeatedly disappointed, and incorrigible optimists were the most irritating; religion was sincere and deep, with improvised prayers in a hut corner or a dark cattle truck. In winter and spring 1945 typhus infected nearly all, with great mortality among the weak who kept working, no medicines and few attendants; Frankl stayed awake composing speeches and reconstructing his lost manuscript in shorthand on tiny scraps. Sensitive prisoners who retreated to inner riches and spiritual freedom often survived better than the robust. Love is the ultimate and highest goal: a man with nothing left can know bliss in contemplation of the beloved, and love goes beyond the physical person to the beloved's spiritual being, whether alive or not. Beauty also intensified inner life: a sunset over Salzburg mountains, prisoners watching in silence, a bird that seemed his wife's presence. Art in camp was improvised cabaret, songs, poems, and jokes; applause for a Capo's love poems saved Frankl's life, and a violin tango played on his wife's 24th birthday. Humor was a weapon of the soul: Frankl trained a surgeon friend to invent one amusing story a day about post-liberation life. Suffering is like gas, it fills the chamber completely whatever its size, so it is absolutely relative; prisoners danced with joy when their train did not cross the Danube bridge to Mauthausen but headed only for Dachau. The only two pleasurable moments in many weeks were relief from suffering, negative happiness in Schopenhauer's sense. No man should judge, Frankl says, unless he asks himself in absolute honesty whether in a similar situation he might not have done the same. In the sick quarters, 70 men were content and happy in spite of everything, with no parade or work; Frankl volunteered for the typhus camp, calling it simple mathematics, not sacrifice. A man's character was in turmoil, values thrown into doubt, and he became a number; the law was do not be conspicuous. He craved five minutes of solitude by the corpses' tent, looking at Bavarian hills through barbed wire. He twice prepared to escape, then decided to take fate into his own hands and stay with his patients, gaining an inward peace he had never experienced before. The turning point is the argument against determinism: a man can preserve a vestige of spiritual freedom and independence of mind; men walked through huts comforting others and giving away their last piece of bread. What sort of person a prisoner became was the result of an inner decision, not of influences alone. Meaning can be found in creative work, in experiencing beauty, art, and nature, and in the attitude taken to a life restricted by external forces: if there is meaning at all, there is meaning in suffering, for suffering is an ineradicable part of life, as fate and death are. A dying young woman, cheerful and grateful that fate had hit her hard, talked to the chestnut tree outside her window, which seemed to answer that it was life, eternal life. The most depressing influence was not knowing how long the term would be: a provisional existence of unknown limit, in which a day lasted longer than a week, a man could not see an end and so could not aim at a goal, and ceased living for the future. Loss of faith in the future was fatal. The senior block warden, a composer, dreamed in February 1945 that a voice told him the war would end for him on March 30; on March 29 he fell ill, on March 30 he was delirious, on March 31 he was dead. The death rate rose at Christmas 1944 to New Year 1945, when hope of being home by Christmas disappointed. Frankl's remedy was to give a man a why, and woe to him who saw no more sense in his life, no aim, no purpose, and therefore no point in carrying on. Life must be reversed: stop asking the meaning of life and realize life questions us daily and hourly; our answer must consist in right action and conduct, in taking responsibility for fulfilling the tasks life sets, each unique and unrepeatable. When destiny is to suffer, suffering itself is the task, and no one can relieve the sufferer of it. In camp psychotherapy he got two would-be suicides to see that life still expected something from them, one his adored child waiting abroad, the other his unfinished scientific books, for a man conscious of responsibility to a person or an unfinished work will never be able to throw away his life. Example is more effective than words. In a hut speech on a fasting evening, after a potato theft had the whole camp starved for a day, he told the men that life never ceases to have meaning, even in suffering, dying, and privation; someone, a friend, a wife, somebody alive or dead, or God, looks down and would not expect us to disappoint him.

Phase 3, following release and liberation, pp. 133-148: Frankl answers the question about the guards: some were sadists in the purest clinical sense; most were dulled by years of witnessing brutality, hardened, refusing an active part but not preventing others; and even among guards some took pity, the commander of the camp Frankl was liberated from paid for medicines from his own pocket. A foreman secretly gave Frankl a piece of bread saved from his breakfast ration, moving him to tears. There are only two races of men, the decent and the indecent, both found everywhere, and the rift dividing good from evil goes through all human beings, reaching into the lowest depths. On liberation morning, when the white flag was hoisted, suspense was followed by total relaxation, but not joy: men dragged tired steps to the gates, repeated Freedom yet could not grasp it, the word had lost its meaning. Meadows full of flowers produced no feeling; the first spark of joy was a rooster's multicolored tail, and it remained only a spark. They had literally lost the ability to feel pleased, depersonalization, and had to relearn feeling slowly; the body had fewer inhibitions than the mind, eating ravenously and talking irresistibly for hours, until feeling broke through days later. Kneeling in the meadows, hearing larks, Frankl recalled calling to the Lord from his narrow prison and being answered in the freedom of space. Sudden release is the psychological counterpart of the bends in a caisson worker and can damage moral and spiritual health: primitive natures became oppressors instead of the oppressed, justifying themselves by their terrible experiences, but no one has the right to do wrong, not even if wrong has been done to him. Bitterness followed on return home, and disillusionment was different: no one awaited some, the loved person was dead, home was different, and no earthly happiness could compensate for suffering; yet courage and meaning came not from hoping for happiness but from suffering, sacrifices, and dying. Part One ends with the crowning experience for the homecoming man, that after all he has suffered, there is nothing he need fear any more, except his God.

Part Two: Basic Concepts of Logotherapy, pp. 149-214

Introduction, pp. 151-153: logotherapy is less retrospective and less introspective than psychoanalysis; it focuses on the future, on assignments and meanings to be fulfilled, defocuses vicious circles, and breaks neurotic self-centeredness by confronting the patient with the meaning of his life. Logos is Greek for meaning; logotherapy is the Third Viennese School, after Freud's psychoanalysis and Adler's individual psychology.

The will to meaning, pp. 154-157: striving to find a meaning is the primary motivational force in man, not a secondary rationalization; it is distinct from Freud's pleasure principle and Adler's will to power. Meaning is unique and must be fulfilled by the person alone; a man would not live and die merely for defense mechanisms, he can live and die for his ideals and values. A French poll found 89 percent felt they needed something to live for and 61 percent something or someone worth dying for. Meaning is detected rather than invented, and values pull rather than push. Unmasking pseudo-values should stop at the authentic desire for a meaningful life.

Existential frustration and noogenic neuroses, pp. 159-163: the will to meaning can be frustrated; this frustration is existential. Noogenic neuroses do not come from conflicts between drives but from conflicts between values, moral and spiritual conflicts, and logos means both meaning and spirit. The diplomat case: a man discontented with his career was told in five years of analysis to reconcile with his father, since government and superiors were nothing but father images; he changed profession instead and was contented for five-plus years. Not every conflict is neurotic, and suffering is not always pathological; it may be a human achievement, especially from existential frustration. The doctor should pilot the patient through existential crises rather than bury despair under tranquilizers.

Noo-dynamics, pp. 164-167: the search for meaning arouses inner tension rather than equilibrium, and that tension is an indispensable prerequisite of mental health: nothing helps a man survive the worst conditions like knowing there is a meaning in his life. Mental health is based on a degree of tension between what one has achieved and what one still ought to accomplish, the gap between what one is and what one should become. Man needs striving for a worthy goal, not discharge of tension or homeostasis.

The existential vacuum, pp. 167-170: a widespread twentieth-century phenomenon, a feeling of total and ultimate meaninglessness, an inner emptiness. Man has lost the instincts that tell an animal what to do and the traditions that tell what he ought to do, and soon does not know what he wants, so he does what others do, conformism, or what others wish him to do. A Vienna Poliklinik survey found 55 percent showed the existential vacuum. It manifests mainly in boredom; boredom now causes more problems than distress. Automation will increase leisure, and many will not know what to do with free time; Sunday neurosis is the depression that appears when the busy week ends and the void shows itself. Suicides, alcoholism, and juvenile delinquency are traced to the vacuum. The frustrated will to meaning is compensated by the will to power, including the will to money, or by the will to pleasure.

The meaning of life and the essence of existence, pp. 171-176: a doctor cannot answer the meaning of life in general terms: meaning differs from man to man, day to day, and hour to hour, and is a specific meaning at a given moment. Like a chess champion, there is no best move apart from the situation and the opponent; everyone has a specific vocation or mission, a task that is unique and cannot be replaced or repeated, and life questions man, who answers by being responsible. Responsibleness is the essence of existence, expressed in a categorical imperative to live as if living already for the second time, and as if you had acted the first time as wrongly as you are about to act now. The logotherapist should not impose value judgments, but act like an eye specialist widening the visual field so the spectrum of meaning becomes conscious. Meaning is found in the world, not within the psyche; human existence is self-transcendence, not self-actualization, which comes only as a side effect of self-transcendence and cannot be attained if made an end in itself.

The meaning of love and the meaning of suffering, pp. 176-183: meaning is discovered in three ways, by doing a deed, by experiencing a value, and by suffering. The second way is experiencing something, nature or culture, or someone, in love. Love is the only way to grasp another human being in his innermost core, seeing his essential traits and potential, and enabling the beloved to actualize his potentialities. Love is not an epiphenomenon of sexual drives; it is as primary as sex, and sex is a mode of expressing love, justified only as its vehicle. The third way is suffering: when a man is confronted with an unavoidable fate, his attitude toward his suffering matters above all. An elderly doctor grieving his wife for two years was asked what would have happened if he had died first, his wife would have suffered terribly, and saw that he had spared her that suffering by surviving and mourning. Suffering ceases to be suffering when it finds a meaning, for example sacrifice; man's main concern is not pleasure or pain avoidance but seeing a meaning in his life, and he is ready to suffer if the suffering has meaning. Necessary suffering is not masochism: a curable cancer must be operated on, not shouldered as a cross. Logotherapy restores the capacity to work, to enjoy life, and to suffer, finding meaning even in suffering. Life's meaning is unconditional and includes the potential meaning of suffering, up to the last moment. Frankl found in an inherited coat pocket a single page with the Shema Yisrael, and read it as a challenge to live his thoughts instead of putting them on paper. To comrades asking will we survive, for if not, all suffering has no meaning, he replied, has all this suffering, this dying around us, a meaning? For if not, then ultimately there is no meaning to survival.

Meta-clinical problems, a logodrama, and the supra-meaning, pp. 183-190: patients bring human problems rather than neurotic symptoms, what is life, what is suffering, and the doctor cannot evade them. In a logodrama, a mother who had lost one son at eleven and tried suicide with her crippled older son was asked, with another woman, to imagine herself at eighty on her deathbed looking back: the childless, financially successful woman said her life was a failure; the mother said she wished to have children, one boy died, the other, the crippled one, she made a fuller life possible for him, and her life was no failure. Viewing life from the deathbed, she saw meaning including her sufferings; a short life can contain more meaning than eighty years. An ape used for poliomyelitis serum cannot grasp the meaning of its suffering, so man too may live in a dimension beyond which a supra-meaning exists that finite intellect cannot grasp; logotherapy speaks of a supra-meaning, and Logos is deeper than logic. A psychiatrist should not be embarrassed to use a patient's firm religious convictions therapeutically. A rabbi who had lost his first wife and six children, gassed at Auschwitz, and whose second wife was sterile, so no son could say Kaddish for him, hoped to see his children in Heaven, but as a sinful old man could not expect the highest place they held as innocent martyrs; Frankl suggested his suffering had purified him to become worthy of joining them, and perhaps none of his sufferings were in vain, bringing the first relief in many years.

Life's transitoriness, pp. 190-193: only potentialities are transitory; once actualized, they are saved into the past, and nothing is irrecoverably lost, everything is irrevocably stored. Transitoriness makes existence responsible: man chooses which potentialities to actualize, leaving an immortal footprint in the sands of time, and the past is full granaries; having been is the surest kind of being. The pessimist sees a wall calendar thinning, the active man files each leaf with diary notes, proud of the richness lived.

Logotherapy as a technique, pp. 193-204: realistic fear cannot be tranquilized away, and neurotic fear cannot be cured by philosophical understanding alone. Anticipatory anxiety produces precisely what the patient fears, the fear is mother of the event, and hyper-intention and hyper-reflection make impossible what one forcibly wishes, since pleasure must remain a side effect. Paradoxical intention invites the phobic patient to intend precisely what he fears: a young physician who feared perspiring was told to show how much he could sweat, and was freed permanently within a week after one session, after four years of phobia. A writer's cramp patient was told to write the worst scrawl, and was freed within 48 hours; a stuttering boy of twelve, trying to stutter to elicit sympathy, found he could not. A woman of 65 with a washing compulsion for 60 years, for whom lobotomy had been expected, was normal within two months and could joke about the obsession. Paradoxical intention also treats sleep disturbance by advising the patient to try to stay awake. The technique reverses the patient's attitude, fear is replaced by paradoxical wish, and the wind is taken out of anxiety's sails, using the capacity for self-detachment in humor. A symptom leads to a phobia of the symptom, a vicious circle that paradoxical intention cuts, so the symptom diminishes and atrophies.

The collective neurosis and critique of pan-determinism, pp. 204-211: every age has its own collective neurosis and needs its own psychotherapy; the existential vacuum is the mass neurosis of the present, a private and personal form of nihilism, the contention that being has no meaning. The danger is nothingbutness: man as nothing but the result of biological, psychological, and sociological conditions, a product of heredity and environment, a robot, fostering neurotic fatalism. Freedom is finite, not freedom from conditions but freedom to take a stand toward them. Pan-determinism, the view disregarding this capacity, is more erroneous and dangerous than pan-sexualism: man is not fully conditioned or determined, he determines himself whether to give in or stand up, and ultimately decides what he will become in the next moment. Dr. J, the mass murderer of Steinhof who ran the euthanasia program, was imprisoned by the Russians, died at about forty of bladder cancer, and before death became the best comrade and consolation to everybody, living up to the highest moral standard. Pan-determinism also undermines religion and education: freedom of decision for or against God and man must be recognized, or religion is a delusion and education an illusion.

The psychiatric credo and psychiatry rehumanized, pp. 211-214: nothing conceivable can condition a man so as to leave him without the slightest freedom; a residue of freedom is left even in neurotic and psychotic cases, and the innermost core is not touched by psychosis. A man of about sixty with auditory hallucinations for decades, regarded as an idiot, wanted to be a priest, sang in the church choir on Sundays, and always regained self-control, revealing an authentic religious life. The incurably psychotic may lose usefulness but retain dignity. For half a century psychiatry interpreted mind merely as a mechanism; now a humanized psychiatry looms, for a doctor who sees the patient only as a machine sees nothing more than a machine instead of the human being behind the disease. Man is ultimately self-determining; the camps were a living laboratory, some behaved like swine, others like saints, and both potentialities are in man, which is actualized depending on decisions, not conditions.

Bibliography, pp. 215-222: an English bibliography of logotherapy and Existenzanalyse, in three parts, books, chapters in books, and articles, including Frankl, The Doctor and the Soul, and articles on the will to meaning, paradoxical intention, and the challenge of suffering.

Central thesis

Frankl's thesis, in his own terms, is that the will to meaning is the primary human motive, and that meaning is found in three ways: in a deed, in an experience, above all in love, and in suffering when suffering is unavoidable. The last human freedom cannot be taken away, even in a camp, and it is the freedom to choose one's attitude. A man who knows the why for his existence will be able to bear almost any how. Life asks the question, and we answer with responsibility, in the specific task of this day and this hour.

Key terms

Logotherapy: therapy through meaning, logos as meaning and spirit, the Third Viennese School after Freud and Adler. Will to meaning: the primary motivational force, distinct from the pleasure principle and the will to power. Existential frustration: frustration of the will to meaning. Noogenic neurosis: a neurosis arising from conflicts between values, not from conflicts between drives. Existential vacuum: a widespread feeling of total meaninglessness, an inner emptiness, manifesting mainly in boredom, conformism, and doing what others wish. Noo-dynamics: the tension between what one has achieved and what one still ought to accomplish, an indispensable prerequisite of mental health. Paradoxical intention: inviting the patient to intend precisely what he fears, so fear is replaced by paradoxical wish. De-reflection: refocusing attention away from the self and the symptom, toward a meaning or a person. Supra-meaning: a meaning beyond the grasp of finite intellect, Logos deeper than logic. Self-transcendence: human existence directed toward the world and toward others, not toward self-actualization as an end in itself.

What the book does NOT prove

It does not prove that every prisoner who survived did so because of meaning, or that those who died lacked it; Frankl himself says the best of us did not return, and that survival depended on many factors beyond attitude. It does not prove logotherapy cures all neuroses, or that its case reports, a single session for a four-year phobia, a 48-hour cure for writer's cramp, a two-month cure for a 60-year compulsion, are typical or replicable. It does not prove the supra-meaning, or that suffering always has a discoverable meaning in this life; Frankl presents it as a therapeutic and existential stance, not as a measured result. The polls cited, 89 percent in France, 55 percent in a Vienna clinic, are reported as Frankl reports them, not independently verified here.

Questions to carry forward

How do you tell, in your own life, between a meaning that is detected and a meaning you invented to comfort yourself? When is suffering unavoidable, and therefore a task, and when is it curable, and therefore to be operated on, not shouldered? What is the specific task life is setting you today, not in general, but this day and this hour? How do you keep the tension between what you are and what you should become, without collapsing into either complacency or despair?
