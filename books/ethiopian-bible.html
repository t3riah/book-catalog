Ethiopian Bible Study Notes

What this is, and what it is not

The Ethiopian Bible is the scripture of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, preserved in Ge'ez for more than 1,600 years. It is the largest canon in Christianity. This file is a first systematic sweep: a canon outline for the whole collection, a comparison with the 66-book Protestant canon already studied in the KJV notes, and chapter-group notes for the books that are distinctive to Ethiopia. It is not a verse-by-verse read of every book, and it does not claim that.

The 81 versus 88 question, stated plainly

Two counts circulate, and both are used by the sites studied here, so neither should be quoted alone without explaining the grouping:

  • 81 is the traditional, teachable figure in Ethiopian church teaching. In the narrower reckoning that is 54 Old Testament books plus the familiar 27-book New Testament. In the broader reckoning it is 46 Old Testament books plus a 35-book New Testament that adds church-order writings, still 81 in total.
  • 88 is what you get when combined works are unstitched and counted separately. The site ethiblereborn.com uses that count: 61 Old Testament books plus 27 New Testament books. Its Old Testament list counts books separately that other lists group together, and it lists Meqabyan I to III and other additions as separate entries.
  • The canon was never frozen by a single council into one itemized list in the way later Western lists were, so which books make up the 81 varies by grouping and by manuscript. The number 81 is the standard figure; 88 is a broader, unstitched count, not a rival canon.

Comparison with the 66-book canon

Everything in the 66-book Protestant canon is inside the Ethiopian canon. The Ethiopian canon adds, in the main:

  • Old Testament books kept only in Ethiopia in full: 1 Enoch and Jubilees.
  • Deuterocanonical books also found in Catholic and Orthodox canons, in Ethiopian form: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch, Letter of Jeremiah, 1 and 2 Maccabees, plus 1 Ezra, 2 Ezra, and 3 Maccabees.
  • Additions inside otherwise shared books: Greek additions to Esther, additions to Daniel (Susanna, Bel and the Dragon, Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three, Prayer of Manasseh), and Psalm 151 after the 150 Psalms.
  • Books found in no other canon as scripture: Meqabyan I, II and III, Ethiopian books associated with Maccabean history but distinct from 1 to 3 Maccabees. On ethiblereborn.com they are listed as description only, which is itself a limit: their full English text is not readily available on that site.

The seven books in the ethiopiabible.com reader

That reader presents seven books, in public-domain English, with adjustable text size. It states plainly that these are substantial samples, not the complete books in every case, and it points to legitimate archives for the complete public-domain texts. The translations it uses are: R. H. Charles, Jubilees 1902 and Enoch 1912, translated directly from Ge'ez, and the King James Apocrypha of 1611 for Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach and 2 Esdras. Those texts are out of copyright, which is why they can be read free.

Jubilees, also called Kufale, Little Genesis, 50 chapters

Retells Genesis and the opening of Exodus as a revelation given to Moses on Sinai. Its structure is calendar: a 364-day year, and history divided into jubilees, cycles of 49 years. It gives a strict chronology from creation, through the patriarchs, to Exodus, and it presents law, especially Sabbath and calendar law, as given from the beginning, not only at Sinai. Key thread: time itself is ordered by God, so keeping the calendar is keeping the covenant.

1 Enoch, Henok, 108 chapters

The only complete text of Enoch survives in Ge'ez, which is why every English translation depends on Ethiopian manuscripts. It is fully canonical in Ethiopia and in no other church. Jude quotes Enoch by name as prophecy. Its five sections, in outline:

  • Book of the Watchers, chapters 1 to 36: angels descend, take human wives, father giants, teach forbidden knowledge, and are judged. Enoch intercedes and sees the places of judgment.
  • Book of Parables, chapters 37 to 71: visions of the Son of Man, judgment, and the fate of the righteous and the wicked.
  • Astronomical Book, chapters 72 to 82: the movements of sun, moon and stars, and a calendar of 364 days, matching the calendar concern in Jubilees.
  • Book of Dream Visions, chapters 83 to 90: history told as animal allegory, from Adam through the exile and return, ending in judgment.
  • Epistle of Enoch, chapters 91 to 108: exhortation to the righteous, woes against the wicked, and a final vision of judgment and reward.

Tobit, 14 chapters

A diaspora tale: Tobit, a faithful Israelite in exile, loses his sight, sends his son Tobias on a journey, and is guided by the angel Raphael in disguise. Threads: almsgiving, burial of the dead, marriage, prayer, and guidance that is only recognized at the end. It is a story about faithfulness in a foreign land, not about temple or king.

Judith, 16 chapters

A widow saves her besieged city by her own hand. Judith uses prayer, fasting, beauty, and deception against the Assyrian general Holofernes, then beheads him and returns in triumph. Threads: a woman as deliverer, God working through the weak and the overlooked, and a city saved without an army.

Wisdom of Solomon, 19 chapters

Wisdom literature on justice, mortality, and the fear of the Lord. It contrasts the righteous, who seem to die early, with the wicked, who seem to prosper, and argues that death is not the end of the story: immortality belongs to righteousness. It also treats wisdom as a personal, active presence, not only as a collection of sayings.

Sirach, also called Ecclesiasticus, 51 chapters

The longest wisdom book in the canon. Practical teaching on speech, friendship, money, work, family, discipline, and fear of the Lord, with a long praise of Israel's ancestors in its later chapters. It reads as a teacher's manual for life in a community under foreign rule, keeping law and wisdom together.

2 Esdras, 16 chapters

An apocalypse of visions and questions about suffering and the end of the age. Ezra asks why Israel suffers, why the wicked prosper, and how God's justice can hold, and receives visions in reply, including the vision of the man from the sea and the woman who becomes a city. Threads: the problem of evil, the remnant, and judgment that is delayed but not abandoned.

Core threads to carry forward

  • Calendar and covenant: Jubilees and Enoch both treat a 364-day calendar as part of God's order, not as a human convenience.
  • Angels, judgment, and the unseen world: Enoch gives the fullest picture of Watchers, giants, and judgment, which shapes later apocalyptic writing, including parts of the New Testament.
  • Faithfulness in exile and diaspora: Tobit, Judith, Esther additions, and the Maccabees books all ask how a people keeps faith without a temple, a king, or an army.
  • Wisdom under pressure: Wisdom and Sirach keep practical wisdom and fear of the Lord together when political power is foreign.
  • Suffering and the end: 2 Esdras, Daniel additions, and Revelation, already covered in the KJV notes, ask the same question from different sides: why do the righteous suffer, and when does judgment come.

Questions worth carrying forward

  • How does counting change the canon: if 81 and 88 are both used, what is being grouped, and what is being split, in each count?
  • Why did Ethiopia preserve Enoch and Jubilees in full when other traditions lost or set them aside?
  • How does the 364-day calendar in Jubilees and Enoch relate to the lunar calendar used elsewhere, and what practical difference did it make?
  • What do Meqabyan I to III actually contain, and why are they description only on the English site studied here?
  • How do the broader New Testament church-order writings, Sinodos, Covenant, Clement, and Didascalia, function: are they scripture, church law, or both, in Ethiopian practice?
  • Where do Ethiopian readings of shared books differ in text, not only in canon: Esther additions, Daniel additions, Psalm 151, and the ordering of books?

Coverage limit, stated plainly

This is a first systematic sweep, not a full read. Covered in this sweep: the canon lists and chapter counts from the two sites named, the 81 versus 88 counting explanation, a book-by-book outline for the Old Testament list, a comparison with the 66-book canon, and chapter-group notes for the seven books in the ethiopiabible.com reader, based on that reader's descriptions and the public-domain translations it names. Not covered word-for-word in this sweep: the full text of Enoch, Jubilees, or any other book, the Meqabyan books beyond their listing, or the broader New Testament church-order writings beyond their names. A full read would require a verse-by-verse pass through those texts, starting with Enoch and Jubilees in the Charles translations, and that remains open.
