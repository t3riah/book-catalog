Psycho-Cybernetics

  • Author: Maxwell Maltz
  • First published: 1960
  • Edition studied: 1968 Prentice Hall edition, 274 pages, 275 scans in reader

Thesis in short

Maltz argues that a person's life tends to follow the mental picture they hold of themselves, and that this picture, rather than willpower alone, sets the limits of what they will try and accept. He treats the mind as a goal-seeking system that works best when given a clear, believable target picture and then left to adjust by feedback, rather than being forced by anxious effort. Change, on this view, starts not with exhortation but with revising that inner picture and practicing new responses in imagination before acting on them.

Overall summary

Maltz, a plastic surgeon, starts from patients whose feelings about themselves did not change when their appearance did, and from others whose confidence changed markedly after surgery. From that, he builds a practical method: notice the self-picture, supply the mind with a clear target, rehearse the desired response in imagination, relax the forced effort, and interrupt negative patterns early. Success is framed broadly, not only as wealth or status, and happiness is treated as a habit to practice, not a reward that only follows external success.

Central thesis

Behavior follows self-image. Give the inbuilt goal-seeking system a believable target picture, practice it in imagination, and let feedback correct the course, instead of trying to force behavior while holding an unchanged picture.

Preface: The Secret of Using This Book to Change Your Life

From surgical work, Maltz notes that an outer change often left the inner picture unchanged, while other patients changed markedly after an operation. He argues the inner picture governs confidence and behavior more than the external change itself, and presents the book as a practical method for revising that picture and using imagination deliberately.

Chapter 1: The Self Image, Your Key to a Better Life

The self-image is a premise for behavior, so people tend to act in ways consistent with how they see themselves. Surgical cases where a corrected feature left shyness intact, and cases where confidence changed after surgery, are used to claim the internal picture, not the feature alone, was decisive. First step: notice the picture, rather than forcing behavior while keeping it unchanged.

Chapter 2: Discovering the Success Mechanism Within You

People already have an inbuilt, goal-directed mechanism, comparable to a machine that steers toward a target and corrects course by feedback. Supply a clear target and allow the system to work, treating errors as corrections rather than proof of failure. Without a goal, the mechanism has nothing to organize effort around.

Chapter 3: Imagination, the First Key to Your Success Mechanism

Imagination is the main way to feed the mechanism a new target, because vividly imagined experience is said to affect the nervous system in ways similar to real experience. Examples sampled include mental rehearsal for a job interview, piano playing, and golf. Technique: rehearse success in detail, repeatedly, rather than rehearsing failure.

Chapter 4: Dehypnotize Yourself from False Beliefs

Many people live under beliefs absorbed without checking them, as if hypnotized, especially beliefs about being inferior. Inferiority often comes not from measured facts but from unfavorable comparisons, so a person can be wrong about their own limits. Method: examine the belief, test it against facts, and replace it with a more accurate picture.

Chapter 5: How to Utilize the Power of Rational Thinking

Rational thinking here is not cold logic alone, but a way to direct feeling: a clear, reasonable judgment about a situation paired with a deep desire for a positive outcome. Worry is imagination used in reverse, picturing an unwanted result, and the remedy is to picture the wanted result instead. Practice: state the facts, decide what is wanted, and hold that picture with conviction.

Chapter 6: Relax and Let Your Success Mechanism Work for You

Effort is distinguished from forced, anxious striving. Over-trying interferes with the automatic mechanism, much as trying too hard to remember a name can block recall, while a relaxed, one-thing-at-a-time approach lets the answer emerge. Techniques sampled include focusing on one task at a time, treating each hour as a separate unit, and giving a problem to the mechanism and then resting from conscious wrestling with it.

Chapter 7: You Can Acquire the Habit of Happiness

Happiness is treated as a habit that can be learned, not a reward that arrives only after external success. Unhappiness often comes from confusing facts with opinions about facts, and a person can practice responding to the facts themselves. Method: separate what happened from the interpretation added to it, and cultivate a goal-striving attitude rather than waiting for circumstances to change first.

Chapter 8: Ingredients of the Success-Type Personality and How to Acquire Them

Maltz lists traits associated with a success-oriented personality and argues they can be practiced rather than being fixed by nature. In paraphrase, those ingredients are a sense of direction, understanding of others and of oneself, courage to act despite fear, charity toward others, esteem for oneself, confidence in handling situations, and acceptance of oneself as one is. Practical point: practice these attitudes deliberately, as habits.

Chapter 9: The Failure Mechanism, How to Make It Work for You Instead of Against You

Failure is presented as the same goal-seeking system working in a negative direction, driven by negative feedback. Maltz spells out a pattern of frustration, misdirected aggression, insecurity, loneliness, uncertainty, resentment, and emptiness, arguing these responses reinforce each other. Remedy: recognize the pattern early, interrupt it, and redirect the mechanism toward a positive goal, using setbacks as information rather than as a verdict on the self.

Chapter 10: How to Remove Emotional Scars, or How to Give Yourself an Emotional Face Lift

Emotional scars are compared to protective tissue that forms after injury but then restricts movement if it becomes too thick. Past hurts can leave a person guarded and withdrawn long after the original event, and a surgical case where confidence returned after an operation is used to show the inner picture matters, not only the outer change. Method sampled: stop rehearsing the injury, release the grievance, and practice new, unguarded responses.

Chapter 11: How to Unlock Your Real Personality

Personality is described not as something to be built from nothing, but as something already present that is blocked by inhibition. Excessive self-criticism and negative feedback act as a brake, so the person appears stiff or limited. Technique: disinhibition in a practical sense, act out the desired self-picture, allow spontaneous responses, and reduce constant monitoring that suppresses natural behavior.

Chapter 12: Do-It-Yourself Tranquilizers That Bring Peace of Mind

Calm is presented as something a person can produce by changing their response to stimuli, rather than only by drugs or by changing every external condition. Images sampled include an umbrella or screen that reduces the impact of a stimulus, and choosing not to answer a ringing telephone. Techniques sampled include practicing non-response to minor irritations, relaxing through activity rather than forced rest, and conditioning a calmer response by repetition.

Chapter 13: How to Turn a Crisis into a Creative Opportunity

A crisis can either paralyze or energize, depending on how it is framed. Some athletes and performers do better under pressure, while others choke, and Maltz argues the difference lies in whether pressure is interpreted as a threat to the self or as a challenge to meet. Method: reframe the crisis as an opportunity to use the mechanism at full capacity, focus on the next useful action, and let heightened energy serve the goal rather than the fear.

Chapter 14: How to Get That Winning Feeling

The winning feeling is described as picturing a goal as a present possibility, not a distant hope, so the mechanism is given a target it can work toward now. If the goal is held as already possible and desirable, the system will supply means and opportunities missed when the goal is held as impossible. Practice: hold the feeling of the desired outcome in imagination, act as if it were attainable, and take the practical steps that present themselves.

Chapter 15: More Years of Life and More Life in Your Years

Chapter pages are headed More Years and More Life. Maltz argues that having a forward-looking goal creates a need for more life, and that this need itself supports vitality and longevity. Examples cited are creative workers who remained productive into old age, contrasted with nostalgia for the past. Practical advice sampled: develop enthusiasm for the future, keep important work to do, and retire from a job without retiring from life, so boredom and a sense of uselessness do not accelerate decline.

Key terms

  • Self-image: the internal picture a person holds of themselves, which sets what they expect and accept.
  • Success mechanism: an inbuilt, goal-directed system that steers by feedback toward a target picture.
  • Failure mechanism: the same system operating toward negative targets, reinforced by frustration and related responses.
  • Servo-mechanism / cybernetics: the book's machine analogy for self-steering by a goal and by error correction.
  • Mental rehearsal / theater of the mind: practicing a desired performance in vivid imagination before doing it in reality.
  • Dehypnosis: waking from unexamined, absorbed beliefs about one's limits.
  • Rational thinking: using facts and a chosen positive goal to guide feeling, rather than letting worry picture the unwanted outcome.
  • Relaxation: a non-forced state that lets the automatic mechanism work, as opposed to anxious over-effort.
  • Emotional scar: a protective but limiting response left by a past hurt, which keeps a person guarded.
  • Inhibition / disinhibition: excessive self-monitoring that blocks personality, and the release of that block by acting out a new picture.
  • Self-made tranquilizer: a practiced, non-chemical way of reducing over-response to stimuli.
  • Winning feeling: holding a goal as a present possibility, with the feeling of it already being attainable.
  • Life force / adaptation energy: the energy Maltz says is drawn on when a person has a goal that requires more life.

What the book does NOT prove / leaves open

  • It relies heavily on clinical anecdotes, surgical cases, and examples from sports and performance, not on controlled trials or systematic data, so it does not prove that imagery or a changed self-picture causes the outcomes described.
  • The machine analogy is an analogy, not a demonstrated neuroscience mechanism; the book does not show how a mental picture is translated into behavior in the brain.
  • It does not distinguish clearly between ordinary low confidence and clinical conditions such as depression, anxiety disorders, or trauma, and it does not set out when professional help is needed.
  • It does not address structural limits on success, such as poverty, illness, discrimination, or lack of opportunity, and its definition of success remains broad and subjective.
  • Its longevity discussion is correlational: creative people living longer does not prove that a forward-looking attitude alone extends life.
  • It leaves open how long change takes, how to handle goals that conflict, and what to do when imagined rehearsal does not transfer to real performance.

Questions worth carrying forward

  • Which parts of the method, if any, have support in later research on imagery, habit, and self-belief, and which parts remain anecdotal?
  • How should the self-image idea be used without blaming a person for circumstances beyond their control?
  • What is a safe, practical way to test mental rehearsal for a specific skill, with a measurable outcome, over a few weeks?
  • How does Maltz's distinction between facts and opinions about facts compare with later cognitive approaches, and where do they differ?
  • When does relaxation and letting go help performance, and when does deliberate, effortful practice matter more?
  • How can the book's advice on emotional scars be used alongside, not instead of, appropriate care for trauma or persistent low mood?
  • What would a fair test of the winning feeling look like, without confusing confidence with wishful thinking?
