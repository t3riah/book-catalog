Study notes: Think and Grow Rich

Date: 2026-11-04

Author: Napoleon Hill (1937)

These are original study notes, paraphrased throughout. Quotations are kept to a phrase or one short sentence at most. No book text is pasted here. This Scribd edition states it is a reproduction of the complete 1937 version, now in the public domain.

Thesis in short

Hill argues that riches begin as a definite thought, held with desire and faith, and turned into a plan and persistent action. The book presents thirteen steps, from desire through faith, auto-suggestion, knowledge, imagination, planning, decision, persistence, and the master mind, as a practical philosophy of individual achievement. Its central claim is that controlling thought, and directing it toward a clear goal, is the starting point of material and personal success.

Overall summary

Hill says he studied hundreds of successful men, prompted by Andrew Carnegie, and distilled a philosophy he presents as the secret behind their achievement. The book is written as instruction, not entertainment: each chapter gives a step, stories of people who applied it, and exercises for the reader to apply it. Wealth is defined broadly at times, but the focus is money, earned by converting a definite desire into organized effort, useful service, and sustained persistence, while guarding the mind against fear and negative suggestion.

Central thesis

Hill's fuller argument is that thought is a force that can be organized. A burning desire, made definite in amount, time, and plan, and repeated to the subconscious mind through faith and auto-suggestion, changes what a person notices, attempts, and endures. Specialized knowledge, imagination, organized planning, prompt decision, persistence, and cooperation through a master mind group then convert that inner state into outward results. Fear, indecision, procrastination, and negative influences are presented as the main enemies, because they break the chain between thought and action. The book treats this as a law-like philosophy, illustrated by biography and anecdote, rather than as a tested scientific theory.

Chapter 1: Introduction

Argument: thoughts, mixed with definiteness of purpose, persistence, and burning desire, can be turned into riches. Hill opens with Edwin Barnes, who arrived with little money or influence, held a definite desire to become Edison's business associate rather than just an employee, took a low-paid job in Edison's office to stay near the opportunity, and later won the chance to sell the Edison Dictating Machine nationally.

Key ideas and examples: the Darby gold story, where quitting three feet from a vein is used as a warning against quitting at temporary defeat; a child's persistence over fifty cents, used to show that refusing to accept no can change an outcome; Henry Ford's V-8 engine, presented as determination overcoming engineers who called the design impossible. Hill also frames the reader as master of fate through control of thought, and says dominating thoughts attract matching people, circumstances, and plans.

Practical steps as presented: fix a definite major purpose, refuse to treat temporary defeat as final, and replace a failure-conscious outlook with a success-conscious one. Note on language and evidence: the chapter uses dated racial language in the Darby stories, and its stories are anecdotes selected to fit the thesis, not controlled evidence that desire alone causes wealth.

Chapter 2: Desire (First Step)

Argument: desire is the starting point of all achievement, but only when it is definite, intense, and backed by persistence. Hill returns to Barnes and then gives a six-step method for turning desire into money: fix the exact amount wanted, decide what will be given in return, set a definite date, form a definite plan and start at once whether ready or not, write a clear statement of amount, date, and plan, and read it aloud twice daily, morning and night, while imagining already possessing the money.

Key examples: Hill's son Blair, born without normal hearing, whose parents are said to have used desire and faith to help him learn to hear and speak rather than accept deafness as a limit; a story of a man who wanted a specific house and, after fixing desire, found a way to buy it; and the general claim that desire outwits nature when it becomes an obsession. Dated note: the chapter's discussion of disability reflects 1937 attitudes and should not be read as medical advice. The claim that desire alone reshapes hearing is Hill's claim, not verified evidence.

Chapter 3: Faith (Second Step)

Argument: faith is a state of mind that can be created by repeated affirmation to the subconscious mind, through auto-suggestion. Hill calls faith the head chemist of the mind, and says that when faith, love, and sex emotions color a thought, it reaches the subconscious and is translated into action and plans. Negative beliefs work the same way in reverse, which is why he warns against a mind dominated by fear and doubt.

Key ideas and examples: a five-part self-confidence formula to be signed, memorized, and repeated daily, promising persistent action toward a definite chief aim, daily visualization of the person one intends to become, and business conducted to benefit all affected; Abraham Lincoln and Gandhi used as examples of faith moving people, with Gandhi presented as power without money or armies; and the long Charles Schwab story, where an idea for combining steel companies, presented persuasively to J. P. Morgan, is said to have created United States Steel and a large paper profit. Hill presents that story as proof that an idea plus faith can be marketed for hundreds of millions, which is his interpretation, not a controlled test.

Practical steps as presented: repeat the definite chief aim aloud with feeling, not as empty words; concentrate daily on the person you intend to become; and guard dominating thoughts, because the subconscious is said to act on whatever is emotionalized, positive or negative.

Chapter 4: Auto-Suggestion (Third Step)

Argument: auto-suggestion is the channel between conscious thought and the subconscious mind. The conscious mind acts as a guard, but dominating thoughts, repeated with emotion, pass through and become habits, plans, and actions. Mere reading without feeling does nothing, in Hill's account; the subconscious responds only to thoughts mixed with belief and emotion.

Key ideas: the subconscious is compared to fertile garden soil where weeds grow if desirable seeds are not planted; the six steps from Desire are the practical use of auto-suggestion, reading the written statement aloud twice daily while seeing and feeling oneself already in possession of the goal; and concentration until desire becomes a burning obsession is presented as the price of influence over the subconscious.

Practical steps as presented: follow the Desire statement routine exactly, mix emotion and faith into the words, persist when results do not come at once, and take responsibility for failure as failure of application rather than of method. That last claim is noted here as Hill's framing, not as evidence, since it makes the method unfalsifiable.

Chapter 5: Specialized Knowledge (Fourth Step)

Argument: general knowledge alone does not attract money. Knowledge becomes power only when organized into definite plans and directed to a definite end. Hill distinguishes general knowledge from specialized knowledge, and argues that the latter can be acquired personally or borrowed through a master mind group.

Key ideas and examples: Henry Ford in court, presented as educated because he knew where to get answers and how to organize them, rather than because he memorized facts; Andrew Carnegie, said to know little technical steelmaking but to command that knowledge through his group; Stuart Austin Wier, who retrained as a lawyer after forty and built a practice during the Depression; and a grocery salesman who learned bookkeeping, built a mobile bookkeeping service for small merchants, and turned an idea into a business. The chapter also praises home study and night school for employed people, and presents a woman's prepared plan book for marketing her son's personal services as a model for selling oneself at a higher level rather than starting at the bottom.

Practical steps as presented: decide what specialized knowledge your major purpose requires, find dependable sources including experience, cooperation, colleges, libraries, and special courses, organize it into a plan, and keep studying after school ends.

Chapter 6: Imagination (Fifth Step)

Argument: imagination is the workshop where desire is given shape and a plan. Hill divides it into synthetic imagination, rearranging old ideas into new combinations, and creative imagination, receiving hunches and new ideas through contact with Infinite Intelligence when the mind is highly stimulated by desire. Synthetic imagination is the one most used in converting desire into money, while creative imagination is called on when synthetic methods fail.

Key ideas and examples: the chapter opens with the claim that humanity can create anything it can imagine, then gives idea-based fortunes as illustrations, including the Enchanted Kettle story that follows in the text, where an old kettle and a secret formula become a large business. The practical point is repeated: reduce desire and plan to writing, because writing gives concrete form to an intangible thought.

Practical steps as presented: use synthetic imagination first to build a plan from experience and observation, stay alert for creative hunches, write the plan down, and revisit the chapter after reading the whole book to put imagination to work on a specific plan.

Chapter 7: Organized Planning (Sixth Step)

Argument: desire becomes action only through a definite, practical plan, built and checked with a master mind group, and replaced when it fails. Temporary defeat means the plan was unsound, not that the goal is impossible, in Hill's framing. The chapter then turns into a long practical section on selling personal services, because most readers begin with services rather than capital.

Key ideas: four master mind instructions, ally with needed people, decide what benefits to offer them, meet at least twice a week until the plan is perfected, and maintain perfect harmony; eleven attributes of leadership, including unwavering courage, self-control, justice, definiteness of decision and plans, doing more than paid for, pleasing personality, sympathy, mastery of detail, responsibility, and cooperation; ten causes of failure in leadership, including inability to organize details, unwillingness to render humble service, fear of competition from followers, lack of imagination, selfishness, intemperance, disloyalty, and emphasis on authority or title; leadership by consent versus leadership by force, with force presented as unable to endure.

Practical steps as presented: write a brief for marketing personal services, study the prospective employer and what you can offer, present a plan for the exact position desired rather than asking if a job exists, and apply the QQS formula, quality plus quantity plus spirit of cooperation, to keep a market for services. The chapter also argues the employer-employee relation should become a partnership serving the public, a 1937 view shaped by the Depression.

Chapter 8: Decision (Seventh Step)

Argument: procrastination is the opposite of decision, and lack of decision is a major cause of failure. Hill says successful people decide promptly and change slowly, while failures decide slowly and change often. Decisions should be based on facts and counsel from one's master mind group, not on the opinions of the crowd, because opinions are cheap and often wrong.

Key examples: the story of a young woman who decided to become a writer despite discouragement, and the general warning against telling the world your plans before acting, since discouragement from others can kill a plan. The chapter also links indecision to fear and to the habit of drifting.

Practical steps as presented: decide promptly, write the decision down, seek facts rather than opinions, keep your own counsel until the plan is formed, and change a decision only on new facts, not on pressure or doubt.

Chapter 9: Persistence (Eighth Step)

Argument: persistence is sustained effort, based on power of will, and is presented as the quality that turns desire and faith into results. Weak desires bring weak results, in Hill's metaphor, while a strong fire under desire makes persistence easier. Spasmodic effort is said to be worthless; the principles must become fixed habit.

Key examples: Fannie Hurst, said to have received thirty-six rejection slips before breaking through, then earning large sums; Kate Smith, said to have sung for years without pay before Broadway relented; and the nightmare analogy, where regaining control muscle by muscle illustrates snapping out of mental inertia step by step.

Practical steps as presented: strengthen desire if persistence is weak, surround yourself with a master mind group that supports persistence, follow the auto-suggestion and subconscious chapters until the picture of desire is handed to the subconscious, and treat every failure as carrying the seed of an equivalent advantage. Note: the chapter's claim that thousands of better singers failed only for lack of persistence is Hill's assertion, not a measured comparison.

Chapter 10: Power of the Master Mind (Ninth Step)

Argument: power is organized knowledge, directed intelligently, and no individual has enough experience, education, and ability alone to accumulate a great fortune. A master mind is the coordination of knowledge and effort, in harmony, between two or more people, for a definite purpose. Hill claims that when minds are coordinated, a third, intangible mind emerges that is more than the sum of its parts.

Key ideas and examples: Andrew Carnegie is presented as the model, commanding steel knowledge through his group rather than possessing it himself; Henry Ford is similarly presented as commanding specialized knowledge through his master mind; and the chapter gives instructions for forming a group, choosing members in complete harmony, meeting regularly, and deciding what each member offers and receives.

Practical steps as presented: form a master mind alliance for a definite purpose, select only members in sympathy and harmony with the purpose, meet at least twice a week until plans are perfected, and use the group to check plans, supply knowledge, and sustain persistence and faith.

Chapter 11: The Mystery of Sex Transmutation (Tenth Step)

Argument: sex desire is presented as the most powerful human desire, and transmutation means switching the mind from physical expression to creative work, so that the same energy fuels imagination, courage, persistence, and creative ability. Hill says the desire should not be submerged or eliminated, but given an outlet in work that enriches body, mind, and spirit.

Key ideas: three constructive potentialities are listed, perpetuation of mankind, maintenance of health, and transformation of mediocrity into genius; ten mind stimuli are listed, with sex expression first, followed by love, desire for fame, power, or money, music, friendship, master mind alliance, mutual suffering, auto-suggestion, fear, and narcotics and alcohol, the last two called destructive; personal magnetism is presented as sex energy communicated through handshake, voice, posture, thought, and appearance; and Hill claims men seldom succeed before forty because love and sex harmonize only around thirty to forty, making forty to sixty the most productive years. Those age and sex claims are Hill's generalizations from biography, not verified findings, and the chapter's language about men, women, and sex reflects 1937 norms.

Practical steps as presented: use willpower to redirect sexual energy into creative effort, cultivate enthusiasm in sales and speaking by transmuting that energy into sales enthusiasm, and avoid intemperance in sex, drink, or drugs, which Hill says dissipates creative force.

Chapter 12: The Subconscious Mind (Eleventh Step)

Argument: the subconscious mind is the connecting link between the finite mind and Infinite Intelligence, receiving sense impressions and thought impulses, storing them, and acting on them whether positive or negative. It works continuously, day and night, and is reached only through auto-suggestion, emotion, and the principles already described.

Key ideas: the subconscious does not distinguish constructive from destructive impulses, so fear, poverty, and failure thoughts are said to be translated just as readily as faith and prosperity thoughts; positive and negative emotions are listed, with faith, love, sex, enthusiasm, romance, hope, and prosperity on one side, and fear, jealousy, hatred, revenge, greed, superstition, and anger on the other; and Hill instructs the reader to keep the mind occupied with positive emotions and to use the subconscious as a file for plans, ideas, and inspiration.

Practical steps as presented: feed the subconscious only thoughts you want translated, use auto-suggestion daily, review plans before sleep so the subconscious can work on them, and watch for ideas, hunches, and plans that surface afterward.

Chapter 13: The Brain (Twelfth Step)

Argument: every brain is both a broadcasting and a receiving station for thought vibrations, operating through the ether like radio. The subconscious is the sending station, creative imagination is the receiving set, and auto-suggestion is the medium for operating the station. When stimulated by strong emotion, especially sex, the mind is said to vibrate faster and become more receptive to ideas from other brains and from Infinite Intelligence.

Key ideas: Hill cites the brain's vast network of cells and connections as evidence that it is more than a physical organ, and cites Duke University researcher Dr. Rhine's telepathy and clairvoyance experiments as parallel support. Those Rhine results are presented in the book as impressive, but they are not accepted as established science today, and the brain-as-radio model is Hill's metaphor, not a demonstrated mechanism.

Practical steps as presented: apply the three principles together, subconscious mind, creative imagination, and auto-suggestion, beginning with desire, and use a close working alliance to stimulate the mind for solving problems.

Chapter 14: The Sixth Sense (Thirteenth Step)

Argument: the sixth sense is the apex of the philosophy, the portion of the subconscious mind called creative imagination, through which Infinite Intelligence communicates voluntarily. It can only be understood and applied after mastering the other twelve principles, Hill says, and it warns of dangers and notifies of opportunities in time to act.

Key ideas and examples: Hill's nightly imaginary council meetings with nine invisible counselors, Emerson, Paine, Edison, Darwin, Lincoln, Burbank, Napoleon, Ford, and Carnegie, whom he addressed aloud to acquire their traits and rebuild his character through auto-suggestion. He reports that the figures became apparently real, with distinct habits and dialogue, which he treats as evidence of the method working. A modern reader should distinguish the practical exercise, imagining admired traits and rehearsing them, from Hill's claim that the figures became real presences.

Practical steps as presented: master the earlier principles first, practice meditation and mind development from within, hold imaginary council meetings to clarify character traits to develop, and act promptly on hunches and inspirations that arrive through the sixth sense.

Chapter 15: How to Outwit the Six Ghosts of Fear

Argument: before the philosophy can work, the mind must be cleared of indecision, doubt, and fear, which Hill calls an unholy trio. Indecision is presented as the seedling of fear, crystallizing into doubt and then fear, often without being noticed. The chapter then inventories six basic fears, gives symptoms for each, and ends with a self-analysis test, a list of fifty-seven alibis, and a closing appeal to control thought.

Key ideas: the six fears, in Hill's order, are poverty, criticism, ill health, loss of love, old age, and death. Fear of poverty is called the most destructive, with symptoms including indifference, indecision, doubt, worry, over-caution, and procrastination. Fear of criticism is linked to loss of initiative and self-reliance. Fear of ill health is linked to hypochondria, self-coddling, intemperance, and worry about disease. Fear of loss of love is linked to jealousy, fault-finding, and gambling to buy love. Fear of old age is linked to slowing down at forty, apologizing for age, and fear of poverty in later years. Fear of death is linked to religious fanaticism in Hill's account, and is answered by accepting death as transition or sleep, a philosophical claim, not a proven fact.

Practical steps as presented: take inventory of yourself against each symptom list, answer the self-analysis questions truthfully, preferably over at least a day and revisited weekly, ask someone who knows you well to judge your answers, replace worry with prompt and firm decision, and protect the mind from negative suggestion by keeping it busy with a definite purpose and plan. The chapter also includes a seventh basic evil, susceptibility to negative influences, in the Devil's Workshop section, and warns against association with negative people.

Key terms

  • Definite Chief Aim: the clear, written goal, with amount, date, and plan, that organizes all other steps.
  • Burning Desire: desire so intense and definite that it becomes an obsession and drives persistent action.
  • Money consciousness: a mind saturated with desire for money, so that one sees oneself already in possession of it.
  • Faith: a state of mind that can be induced by repeated affirmation, presented as the chemist that gives life to thought.
  • Auto-suggestion: self-administered suggestion through the senses, the channel between conscious thought and the subconscious mind.
  • Transmutation: changing one form of energy or thought into another, as desire into money, or sex energy into creative work.
  • Synthetic imagination: rearranging old concepts, ideas, or plans into new combinations.
  • Creative imagination: receiving new ideas and hunches, presented as direct communication with Infinite Intelligence.
  • Master Mind: coordination of knowledge and effort, in harmony, between two or more people for a definite purpose.
  • Infinite Intelligence: Hill's term for a universal intelligence or first cause that converts thought into form and answers prayer.
  • Sixth Sense: creative imagination as a receiving set, said to warn of dangers and notify of opportunities after the other principles are mastered.
  • QQS: quality plus quantity plus spirit of cooperation, Hill's formula for marketing personal services.
  • The six ghosts of fear: poverty, criticism, ill health, loss of love, old age, and death.
  • Old Man Worry: worry as sustained fear caused by indecision.
  • Fifty-seven famous alibis: excuses beginning with if, presented as defenses for failure that the philosophy makes obsolete.

What the book does NOT prove, and what it leaves open

  • Causal proof: Hill presents biography and anecdote as proof, but does not provide controlled comparisons, failure rates, or a test that separates desire, faith, and persistence from talent, capital, connections, timing, and luck. The method is also framed so that failure is blamed on incomplete application, which makes it hard to falsify.
  • The Carnegie secret and the 500 men: Hill claims Carnegie disclosed a secret and that he analyzed more than 500 successful men over twenty-five years, but the book does not name a verifiable sample, method, or dataset, and later researchers have questioned the extent of Hill's Carnegie connection. Treat those origin claims as Hill's account, not as independently verified history.
  • Mind, ether, and brain broadcasting: claims about thought vibrations, the ether, telepathy, and the brain as a radio station are presented as science in 1937 language, but are not established by modern neuroscience or physics. The practical parts, setting clear goals, planning, persisting, and seeking counsel, can be separated from those metaphysical claims.
  • Health claims: stories about desire and faith overcoming deafness, illness, or death risk should not be read as medical advice. The Blair story involves real hearing technology and parental advocacy as well as Hill's interpretation, and the book's claims about physicians not fearing disease, or fear causing disease, are not evidence-based medicine.
  • Dated attitudes: the book uses 1937 language about race, gender, disability, and sex, assumes a male reader and male leaders in most examples, and presents generalizations about women, highly sexed men, and productive age that reflect its time, not current evidence. The Darby stories in Chapter 1 use racial language that is dated and offensive today.
  • Who is excluded: the book focuses on individual thought and effort, and gives little sustained attention to structural barriers, discrimination, capital access, market conditions, or the role of luck and inheritance in the fortunes it cites.
  • Later history: the book ends in 1937, before later research on goal setting, habit, social networks, and persuasion, and before later scrutiny of Hill's biography. Its practical advice should be judged alongside that later evidence, not taken as a complete theory of wealth.

Questions worth carrying forward

  • Which parts of Hill's system still work when the metaphysics is removed: clear goals, written plans, daily review, counsel from others, persistence, and prompt decision?
  • When does a burning desire help, and when does it become rigidity, sunk cost, or refusal to change a bad plan?
  • How would Hill's advice change if it started from a person's actual capital, network, and constraints, rather than from thought alone?
  • What would count as good evidence that auto-suggestion or visualization helps: a specific outcome, a comparison group, or a change in behavior that can be observed?
  • How can a master mind group be built today without the book's demand for perfect harmony, which can silence useful disagreement?
  • What is a fair way to use the fear inventory and self-analysis questions without turning normal worry, illness, or grief into a personal failure?
