Thesis in short

Television did not just report the Cold War. It projected, shaped, and amplified Cold War ideas, but what it could do depended on who owned and controlled production, on technology and staging, and on the audience in front of the set. This is an edited volume, not a single-author argument, so its chapters test that shared question in different places and periods rather than proving one uniform effect.

Overall summary

The book began as a Virginia Tech senior capstone project, HIST 4914 in spring 2023, and its twelve student essays cover British, Italian, Soviet, and American television from the early 1950s into the 1980s. The cases range from a royal coronation and a live nuclear test to spy shows, Vietnam news, chess, children's cartoons, professional football, and primetime soap opera. Together they argue that programming mattered, and that the structures behind programming mattered at least as much: monopolies, state control, military and satellite infrastructure, sponsors, toy companies, leagues, and deregulation. A recurring surprise is that television did not always work the way its makers intended. It could domesticate a bomb, undermine a war, or turn a quiet board game into a proxy contest.

Central thesis

The volume's fuller argument is that Cold War television cultures were made, not simply broadcast. A program's meaning came from a chain: who paid for it, who was allowed to make it, what technology could show live or clearly, what rules governed its time slot, and what viewers already believed. Governments used television for statebuilding, diplomacy, and persuasion, sometimes by running stations and sometimes by staging events for cameras. Commercial firms used it to sell products, teams, and stars, and in doing so carried political assumptions with them. Because the chapters are separate studies, the strength of the claim varies. Some show direct state or industry control. Others show a dominant frame in popular programs, with audience effect inferred rather than measured. The volume is therefore strongest as a map of mechanisms, weaker as proof of persuasion.

Part I: Politics

The editors frame this part as television's role in building and maintaining nations and ideologies: offering unifying images, projecting the ideals of those in charge of production, and letting governments either run stations or televise major events to create a connection with audiences.

Chapter 1: Crowning a Commonwealth, by Stratis Bohle

Argument: the televised coronation of Elizabeth II on June 2, 1953 helped both television and the monarchy. Television moved from a low-status entertainment device to a serious medium for national ceremony, and the monarchy moved from a distant, sacred institution to a visible, shared presence across a changing empire. Survival came at a price: the palace gave up some secrecy and tradition in exchange for visibility, reframing empire as a Commonwealth held together by a common sovereign and a common viewing experience.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • The BBC held a monopoly over British radio and television. Its television service, begun in the 1930s and suspended during World War II, was still limited in reach and expensive in 1953, with a set costing about eight weeks' wages for an average British man.
  • George VI's 1937 coronation had been covered outside Westminster Abbey, not inside. Prime Minister Winston Churchill, the Earl Marshal, and the Archbishop of Canterbury opposed televising the 1953 service on grounds of sacredness and propriety.
  • After public backlash against a decision to film rather than televise, the ban was lifted on December 8, 1952, with limits: no televising of the Act of Consecration or communion, no close-ups, and fixed camera positions.
  • Scale cited in the chapter: 930 international reporters from over 50 countries, 8 BBC commentators, 20 cameramen, and 72 other BBC staff. UK television licenses rose from 1.9 million in December 1952 to 2.7 million by Coronation Day, and 20.4 million adult Britons watched on television, for the first time more than BBC radio listeners.
  • The relay reached Belgium, France, the Netherlands, and West Germany live. North America could not receive a live signal, so NBC, CBS, and the CBC flew filmed versions across the Atlantic for same-evening broadcast.
  • Canada gets special weight. The chapter reports that NBC and ABC ended up rebroadcasting the CBC version, while CBS alone aired its own original broadcast in North America, and treats Canada being first in North America as strengthening the Canada-Britain bond.

What it leaves open: it shows a large audience and a surge in set ownership, not proof that television caused the monarchy or Commonwealth to survive. Reception outside Britain, beyond Canada, is asserted more than demonstrated, and the most colorful claim about BBC motives in the North American jet race rests on a secondhand private recollection, not a contemporaneous document quoted in the chapter.

Chapter 2: For War and Peace: Television in the Cold War, by Tyler Ripley

Argument: television was born from wartime military technology and then became a flexible Cold War instrument. It projected prestige, let leaders speak directly to foreign publics and rivals, staged peaceful cooperation, and turned technical achievements into political victories. The same visual power could also hurt leaders, because appearance and live failure were now visible. It did not unify the world as early optimists hoped, but it became indispensable to how superpower rivalry was performed and watched.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Prewar television research fed wartime radar and sonar displays. Civilian sets lost out to military uses of cathode ray tubes during World War II.
  • Our World, planned as East-West cooperation, went ahead after Eastern Bloc participants withdrew in protest at the Western response to the Six-Day War. It is described as, at the time, the most watched television event ever, and as proof that cooperation was possible but short-lived.
  • The 1960 Kennedy-Nixon debate is used for image politics: radio listeners were said to favor Nixon, television viewers Kennedy, who looked youthful and photogenic against a tired, makeup-free Nixon.
  • Presidential satellite speeches let leaders address allies and opponents at once: Truman's first televised White House speech in March 1947, Eisenhower broadcast internationally by satellite in December 1958, and Kennedy's Telstar speech in July 1962 combining blunt talk about Berlin and nuclear testing with an appeal for diplomacy.
  • Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev used international television in April 1964 to propose a more equal relationship among Warsaw Pact states, a point used to challenge the idea of a monolithic Communist bloc.
  • The 1964 Tokyo Olympics, relayed by the Syncom 3 satellite from Japan's national broadcaster to NBC in the United States, showed live global satellite coverage working, with military as well as civilian interest in the same capacity.
  • Apollo 11 is presented as a political victory proving American administrative and industrial capacity, watched by hundreds of millions. Apollo 13, ignored at launch by some stations, became a global spectacle after an oxygen-tank explosion, with even foreign offers of help cited as rare cooperation. Apollo-Soyuz later docked an American Apollo capsule with a Soviet Soyuz capsule on camera, before politics resumed.

What it leaves open: this is a broad synthesis of secondary and newspaper sources, not archival or audience research. Claims about what audiences believed or leaders intended are often illustrative, and topics such as television inside the Soviet bloc and the commercial structures shaping coverage are named but not developed.

Chapter 3: Lascia o Coppia: The Legacy of Fascism for Postwar Italian Television, by Anthony Salisbury

Note on title: the chapter title uses a variant spelling. The program discussed in the text is Lascia o Raddoppia, in English Leave it or Double it.

Argument: fascist Italy created a state broadcasting monopoly and a model of television as a controllable, expensive, preplanned medium ideal for state messaging. After fascism fell, Italy's new republican rulers, above all the Christian Democrats, did not dismantle that structure. They inherited it, staffed it with loyal managers, wrapped it in Catholic moral rules, and used it to build a liberal, Catholic, Western-aligned Italy while marginalizing the Communist Party. Antifascist statebuilding, in this account, reused fascist instruments.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Benito Mussolini's first Italian television experiment was a broadcast speech meant to inspire nationalism and project strength. It went out through URI, Unione Radiofonica Italiana, which later became RAI, Radio Audizioni Italiane, without, Salisbury argues, a fundamental change to its founding legislation.
  • Television suited state control because production required costly equipment, technical preparation, and scripting in advance, making dissenting production hard. Early viewing was often communal in bars, so speeches and ceremonies could reach crowds even when sets were scarce.
  • After the war, the Christian Democrats, aligned with Western liberalism and backed by the Vatican, faced the PCI, Partita Comunista Italiana, strong among workers because of its wartime role. With Vatican and US-British support, Christian Democrats filled senior RAI management with party loyalists and edged Communists out of content decisions.
  • Pope Pius XII welcomed television as a way to spread the Church's message. RAI operated under rules banning references to sexuality and censoring material insulting Catholic family norms. RAI executive Filiberto Guala, a loyal Christian Democrat who later became a Catholic monk, personifies the church-party overlap for Salisbury.
  • Radiotelevisione Italiana began television transmissions on January 3, 1954, offering news, sport, documentaries, and cultural programs mostly produced in Italy, aiming to be the single source Italians needed.
  • The US Information Service's Voice of America found RAI audiences far larger than its own. USIS offered RAI American films, programs, and news items made for RAI anchors to read without acknowledging their origin. RAI agreed because both sides wanted to weaken Communist influence.
  • Lascia o Raddoppia, modeled on US quiz shows such as The $64,000 Question, is the central popular example. Quiz questions emphasized soccer history or American literature rather than Marx or Gramsci, which Communists noticed as a liberal worldview inside entertainment. Host Mike Bongiorno, an Italian-American former antifascist partisan, became a cultural phenomenon and a role model for white-collar aspirational Italy.
  • Tribuna Politica, Political Gallery, launched in part after the televised 1960 Nixon-Kennedy debates and hailed as the start of telepolitics in republican Italy. Salisbury argues it mainly let Christian Democrats deflect bias charges while keeping control and giving themselves first and foremost airtime.
  • RAI documentaries followed Italian migrants abroad during the mid-1950s economic miracle and recast them not as colonizers but as working-class collaborators, modernizers, and managers. Interviews were rerecorded and read by a narrator rather than heard directly.

What it leaves open: the claim that postwar law was nearly identical to fascist law is carried largely by secondary citations, not by a side-by-side legal comparison in the chapter. It does not quantify television's effect on Communist decline, since prosperity, Vatican pressure, US aid, and PCI dependence on Moscow are not disentangled, and audience evidence rests mainly on letters and complaints rather than systematic research.

Chapter 4: Nuclear Screening: A Look Into the Operation Tumbler-Snapper Television Broadcast, by Jeremiah Albert

Argument: the first live televised American nuclear explosion had the opposite effect from what its terrifying subject might suggest. People at the Nevada test site experienced awe, heat, shock, and a sense of godlike destructive power. People at home, watching on small, low-resolution early television sets, mostly saw a dull, brief, confusing flash. Combined with news that troops could march through the blast zone soon after, the broadcast helped strip away mystery and fear, recast the bomb as a manageable tactical weapon, even a mercy weapon that could spare infantry casualties, and turned a weapon of mass destruction into something that looked like just another television show. Technical limits and framing domesticated it.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Operation Tumbler-Snapper at Yucca Flat, Nevada, tested different bomb types, compositions, and delivery methods to diversify the US arsenal after the Soviet Union's first nuclear test in 1949 and during the Korean War. The desert offered seclusion, sparse population, and room to build mock towns.
  • Mock towns modeled Anglo-American and European structures, furnished with mannequin families, cars, tanks, carriers, and artillery, so blast recovery crews could measure effects on civilians and equipment.
  • Only Shot Charlie allowed camera crews among the first shots. Able on April 1 and Baker on April 15, 1952 were each about one kiloton, with no follow-up military exercises. Shot Charlie, April 22, 1952, at 9:30 a.m., was 31 kilotons, larger than the 15-kiloton Hiroshima and 21-kiloton Nagasaki bombs cited in the chapter.
  • About 1,400 personnel, mostly troops for post-blast drills, waited in a five-foot trench 3.5 miles from ground zero. The concurrent Desert Rock IV exercise involved about 7,350 Defense Department personnel. Troops walked within 200 meters of ground zero, and an hour after detonation radiological checks cleared drills to proceed.
  • On-site witnesses described shock as a shove and slap and heat like opening a furnace door. At home, newspaper headlines the next day framed the telecast as a dud or dull, including the Chicago Daily Tribune on transmission difficulty and the Washington Post on disappointment.
  • Why home viewing disappointed: only 9 percent of US homes had television in 1950, rising to 90 percent by 1960, but early sets had poor resolution and outside broadcasts were much worse than studio programs. The first live outside-studio broadcast had occurred less than a year earlier, in September 1951. In KTLA live stills, the visually interesting part lasted under eight seconds.
  • Mercy weapon framing: United Press president Hugh Baillie later wrote that one bomb does it all and spares slow, costly infantry advances. New York Times writer Gladwin Hill called tactical nuclear weapons essentially another weapon backing the foot soldier. General Joseph Swing wanted a smaller tactical bomb that devastated an enemy stronghold and let infantry move in fast. Albert connects this acceptance to Korea, where napalm was being dropped in large quantities and denounced in the press.
  • Poll context: 37 percent of Americans in 1945 thought they were in real danger of nuclear attack; by 1950, 91 percent thought Russia would use the atomic bomb against American cities.

What it leaves open: it does not prove a national change in public opinion from this one broadcast. Home-audience evidence is a handful of newspaper headlines and opinion pieces, not polls, ratings, letters, or regional comparisons. The chapter also does not assess actual exposure, fallout, or later health effects, and downwind civilian populations are largely absent. Animal testing is introduced, with anesthetized animals outside the mock house, then dropped.

Part II: Propaganda

The section introduction presents television as a new channel for framing global conflicts for national and regional audiences. In both entertainment and news, producers drew ideological battle lines and marked enemies, especially the Soviet Union, in ways that made Western liberalism look heroic.

Chapter 5: Cold War Spy Television: I Led 3 Lives and Communism, by Chase Frazier

Argument: early Cold War spy television drew on an existing public fear of communism, used that fear to attract viewers, and then helped spread and harden the same fear. The shows did not merely reflect anxiety. By staging communism as a hidden domestic danger and anticommunist vigilance as ordinary civic duty, they gave audiences a vivid, repeatable script for suspicion and reporting.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • The central case is I Led 3 Lives (1953-1956), produced by Ziv Television Programs and starring Richard Carlson as Herbert Philbrick, presented as one of the first American television spy shows and a template for later spy drama.
  • Philbrick joined the Communist Party in the United States in the early 1940s, then worked as an FBI informant and counterspy. He broke cover to testify against Communist Party leaders in 1949. His 1952 book, I Led Three Lives: Citizen, Communist, Counterspy, supplied the first two seasons. The third season drew on his files and other agents' experiences.
  • Political context includes the second Red Scare, Senator Joseph McCarthy, FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover, background checks, the Marshall Plan, the Soviet atomic test in 1949, and the 1953 Rosenberg trial and executions.
  • Unlike shows centered on foreign agents, I Led 3 Lives stressed a homegrown threat. Communists appear as secretive, ruthless, and hostile to American freedoms, in the pilot even discussing arming citizens for a takeover. Philbrick appears as the model citizen: ordinary American, party member by cover, and FBI counterspy.
  • In the Commie Son episode, a mother suspects her son has joined the Communist Party, asks Philbrick for advice, and is encouraged to report him to the FBI. The son ultimately leaves the party. Frazier reads this as a moral lesson that normalized reporting family and friends.
  • Success evidence: 117 episodes over three seasons, a top-ten showing in most major markets according to Broadcasting Magazine in 1955, and Primetime Emmy nominations in 1954 and 1955.
  • The genre then moved into the 1960s with The Man From U.N.C.L.E., I Spy, and Get Smart, alongside Ian Fleming's James Bond novels beginning with Casino Royale in 1953 and the film Dr. No in 1962.

What it leaves open: it does not show, with audience research, polling, or FBI reporting records, that viewers became more fearful or more likely to report others after watching. Ratings establish popularity, not persuasion. It explicitly says there is no evidence the FBI funded the show or shaped production beyond Philbrick's involvement, so the propaganda effect is presented as indirect and cultural, not as a documented state production operation.

Chapter 6: Broadcasting War: Vietnam in America, by Danielle Schumacher

Argument: television moved American opinion from the relatively uncritical patriotic support associated with World War II toward opposition to the Vietnam War. Battlefield footage contradicted official claims, antiwar advertising gave the movement a televised language and a call to action, and the combined effect eroded trust in presidents and policymakers. The war, in this account, was not only reported by television but reshaped at home by it.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Televised coverage began in the early 1960s and became prominent in 1965. By 1968, the chapter says, more than half of Americans relied on television as their main news source.
  • Escalation under Lyndon B. Johnson, rising casualties, Richard Nixon's Vietnamization policy of withdrawing US combat troops while aiding South Vietnam, and Nixon's 1970 expansion into Cambodia, linked to a new wave of campus protest.
  • The Tet Offensive in January 1968 attacked more than 100 cities and outposts. The chapter contrasts administration claims of military success with images that looked like defeat, and argues that this gap damaged remaining support. It cites 179 accredited American journalists in Saigon on the eve of Tet and more than 600 reporters in Vietnam at the war's height.
  • Earlier breaking point: coverage of the Buddhist crisis in South Vietnam in 1963, including self-immolation protests, is treated as an early moment when images undercut US support for the Diem government.
  • The My Lai killing of civilians by US troops is presented as another televised and reported shock that contradicted claims about the mission.
  • Antiwar advertising: one ad warned that American boys would grow up still facing Vietnam unless viewers wrote to their senators. Another, tied to the war's tenth anniversary, featured former Navy Lieutenant John F. Kerry, cited a figure of 73 percent of Americans wanting troops removed, attacked war spending, and also urged letters to senators. Schumacher reads both as emotional and practical appeals that turned private unease into political pressure.
  • The movement originated among students and civil rights activists in 1965, then spread nationally, including to military bases. Tactics discussed include draft-card burning and marches on the Capitol, alongside the hawk versus dove split in public debate.

What it leaves open: the causal claim is stronger than the evidence presented. The chapter offers no systematic polling series, content analysis by network, or comparison that separates television's effect from casualties, the draft, civil rights politics, economic pressure, or elite dissent. It does not show that the two antiwar commercials changed votes, policy, or enlistment, and it gives less attention to how editing, narration, placement, and government efforts to manage coverage shaped what counted as reality.

Chapter 7: Atomic Television in the 1950s, by Christopher Porter

Argument: 1950s American television allowed only a narrow, two-sided discussion of the atom. On one side stood peaceful, productive, American-controlled nuclear energy. On the other stood a destructive atom that was dangerous mainly when other nations, above all the Soviet Union, held it. American stockpiling could therefore be presented as defense, deterrence, and scientific progress, while foreign stockpiling appeared as threat. That framing appeared across political broadcasts, entertainment, and educational programs, and dissenting voices were sidelined rather than engaged on equal terms.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Political milestones: Truman's televised announcement of the detected Soviet test in 1949, Eisenhower's Atoms for Peace address to the United Nations on December 8, 1953, and Sputnik in 1957.
  • Eisenhower's speech proposed an international atomic agency, shared uranium, and peaceful development, while also stressing the size and growth of the US stockpile. Porter reads this as reassurance plus deterrence, and as a way to put pressure on Moscow: accept the peace proposal or look like the party rejecting it.
  • The Atomic Energy Commission and peaceful projects: radiation research in medicine and agriculture, including seed irradiation and radioisotope work, nuclear power for cities, and controlled detonations to shape land or expose natural gas. These projects coexisted with continued weapons stockpiling, and many peaceful applications proved fruitless or risky.
  • Meet the Press, May 11, 1958: chemist and Nobel laureate Linus Pauling appeared as a voice for ending nuclear testing. Porter argues that interviewers, including Lawrence Spivak, redirected him toward deterrence and questioned his credibility by raising alleged communist connections, blunting his warning about fallout.
  • Chronoscope, December 9, 1953: Representative Carl Hinshaw, appearing the day after Eisenhower's speech, praised sharing nuclear knowledge and described controlled fission as a slow explosion that could turn weapons material into energy.
  • Duck and Cover, from 1951: the Federal Civil Defense Administration campaign used Bert the Turtle to teach schoolchildren to duck and cover. Porter reads it as proof that the destructive atom could not be kept out of everyday life, even while peaceful uses were being promoted.
  • Space Patrol (1950-1955), February 1954 two-part story The Deadly Radiation Chamber and The Plot in the Atomic Plant: a trusted ally spreads rumors of reactor failure and tries to kill the heroes with radiation. They escape and are treated for radiation poisoning, with no explanation of the cure. Porter reads the message as: nuclear power is controllable, medicine can fix exposure, and danger comes from bad actors, not from the technology itself.
  • Tales of Tomorrow, Sneak Attack (December 7, 1951): on the tenth anniversary of Pearl Harbor, unmanned Soviet planes land in American cities. One destroys Denver, killing tens of thousands in the episode's account. The Soviets threaten further blasts unless the United States surrenders. An American spy in the Soviet Union stops the remaining bombs.
  • The Twilight Zone, Time Enough at Last (November 20, 1959): bank teller Henry Bemis survives an H-bomb attack because he is in a bank vault reading, then finds a ruined city and a library, only to break his glasses. Porter reads the unnamed attacker and total destruction as a generalized fear that any nuclear-armed adversary could annihilate an ordinary American city.
  • Watch Mr. Wizard, Atomic Energy (1953): Mr. Wizard explains chain reactions with a branching gunpowder demonstration and tells a boy, Willie, that the energy in a pound of butter, converted to electricity, would be worth an enormous sum and could run US trains for years. The bomb is mentioned briefly but not lingered on.
  • Walt Disney's Our Friend the Atom (January 23, 1957): made at the Eisenhower administration's request to support Atoms for Peace, the special used animation, Walt Disney as host, and scientist Heinz Haber. Haber compared the atom to a genie from a fisherman's vessel: it could destroy or grant wishes. Porter calls this the clearest dramatization of the central split, and notes its unusual regret over Hiroshima and Nagasaki alongside optimism about reactors, medicine, agriculture, submarines, and planes.

What it leaves open: Porter himself states the limit plainly. More research is needed on whether these shows swayed public opinion, and it is unlikely they radically changed many minds. The chapter establishes a recurring frame, not a measured change in attitudes. The sample is selective rather than exhaustive, it does not examine Soviet television or American viewers who rejected the frame, and it does not fully separate government intent from commercial and creative motives, except for Disney's special as the strongest case of direct administration involvement.

Chapter 8: Bombs Over the Chess Board, by Jason Toy

Argument: television turned the 1972 World Chess Championship into a Cold War proxy contest. A quiet, long, technically opaque board game was refitted for mass television through personality, national symbolism, and crisis narrative. Bobby Fischer was cast as impulsive, individualistic, and American; Boris Spassky as disciplined, stoic, and Soviet. The match therefore became a contest between political systems, even though the players themselves, and some moments in the match, broke that script.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Fischer, born in Chicago in 1943, child prodigy, youngest grandmaster at fifteen at the time, meticulous and aggressive on the board but erratic about money and playing conditions. Spassky, born in Leningrad in 1937, also a child prodigy, grandmaster in 1956, world champion after beating Tigran Petrosian in 1969, and a product of Soviet coaching, training, and state support.
  • Soviet chess enjoyed government funding, coaching programs, and cultural prestige, producing a long run of champions. The United States gave chess little central support and did not treat it as a Cold War arena worth funding before 1972.
  • Fischer's 1962 charge, reported in Sports Illustrated, that Soviet players at the Stockholm Interzonal had prearranged draws to block him. FIDE investigated and did not find enough evidence to punish the Soviet players. Fischer had lost his three prior games against Spassky before Reykjavik.
  • Road to the match: after an 18-month break, Fischer skipped the zonal stage. The US Chess Federation persuaded Paul Benko and William Lombardy to yield places so he could enter the 1970 Palma de Mallorca Interzonal. He then swept Mark Taimanov and Bent Larsen 6-0 each in the 1971 Candidates matches and beat Petrosian 6.5-2.5.
  • Television introductions: Dick Cavett interviewed Fischer on The Dick Cavett Show in 1971, shifting his image from reclusive eccentric to determined patriot. On 60 Minutes on April 9, 1972, Mike Wallace showed Fischer training physically and mentally, and Fischer framed the coming match as a struggle against the whole Soviet chess establishment, not only Spassky. President Richard Nixon also sent Fischer a congratulatory letter before the Petrosian final.
  • Where's Bobby?: Fischer demanded a larger prize fund, a share of gate receipts, and better conditions, then fled New York's JFK Airport on June 28 and hid at former US champion Anthony Saidy's home in Queens while media camped outside. National security adviser Henry Kissinger called him on July 3, warned that absence would hand Moscow a propaganda victory, and urged him to play. British businessman James Slater then doubled the prize fund to $250,000, and Fischer flew to Reykjavik that evening.
  • Broadcasting the match: filmmaker Chester Fox bought film and photography rights through the Icelandic Chess Federation. NBC, ABC, and CBS sent crews to Reykjavik. Special telegraph links carried moves to US analysts. Larry Evans hosted for ABC, while Shelby Lyman's PBS play-by-play drew millions and, according to the chapter, achieved the highest rating in public television history to that point.
  • Games as television drama: in Game 1 on July 11, Fischer blundered with his bishop into a trap and lost to Spassky in 56 moves. He forfeited Game 2 over noisy cameras. Game 3 moved to a small back room, the ping pong room, without audience or cameras. Fischer won with the aggressive Benoni Defense, his first win over Spassky. Spassky then complained about lighting and a possibly bugged chair; inspections found nothing. Fischer won Game 5 before a crowd chanting his name, and won the celebrated Game 6 on July 23 after ABC brokered quieter filming. When Spassky stood and applauded Fischer's Game 6 play, Toy reads it as a crack in the simple rivalry script.
  • Ending: Fischer won 12.5-8.5 in Game 21 on August 31, then fled into the hills outside Reykjavik. Spassky returned to what the chapter, citing the Associated Press, calls anti-VIP treatment: lines for bags and customs, a shabby bus instead of an official limousine, and public criticism from former champions Mikhail Botvinnik and Vasili Smyslov. Fischer returned as a hero and appeared on The Tonight Show Starring Johnny Carson on November 8, 1972, defending Spassky as a scapegoat and attacking the Soviet chess system, complicating the story that had made him its champion.
  • Why chess was hard to televise: classical games could last over three hours, matches were long, silence was normal, Elo ratings were opaque to casual viewers, venues were small, and prizes were low. Spassky won only $1,400 for the 1969 world title, by the chapter's account. Only in the Soviet Union were professional games regularly televised before 1972.

What it leaves open: the chapter relies heavily on Edmonds and Eidinow, Brady, and later documentary retellings, and offers less direct analysis of the actual broadcasts themselves, such as transcripts, ratings over time, or a comparison of ABC, PBS, and network news framing. Its claim that the match made chess mainstream is asserted in the conclusion without a detailed post-1972 account of membership, funding, or participation. Soviet television and Soviet domestic reception are largely absent, and Icelandic coverage is mostly background despite Reykjavik bearing the financial and logistical strain.

Part III: Pop Culture

The editors frame these chapters as studies of changing production and representation in American television, and of how those changes both reflected and shaped wider culture. Their emphasis is not only on programs, but on industrial conditions that set what reached screens. They note a longstanding dismissal of television as low art, and a hierarchy in which children's programs and soap operas were treated as beneath programming aimed at middle-class adult men.

Chapter 9: America Reconciles with Vietnam Veterans, by Joshua Buckingham

Argument: American television moved through three broad veteran images, and the 1980s image helped repair the public standing of Vietnam veterans. After World War II, veterans appeared as steady fathers and community authorities. During and just before Vietnam, that image loosened into comic irreverence and then into antiwar criticism. Under Reagan, the dominant primetime veteran became a capable, wounded but functioning hero seeking to help others and rejoin civilian life. Television reflected a cultural reconciliation and also helped make it happen, as part of an escape from reluctance to use force abroad after Vietnam.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Early documentary-style programs about World War II, built around archival combat footage, presented American soldiers as determined defenders of democracy, with suffering shown but victory as the frame.
  • Leave It to Beaver and The Andy Griffith Show: Ward Cleaver and Sheriff Andy Taylor are presented as modest veteran-fathers whose service quietly supports their household and community authority, and who teach honesty, hard work, and service.
  • The Phil Silvers Show (Sergeant Bilko) and McHale's Navy: a second strand in which servicemen are schemers and comic rule-benders rather than model citizens. The chapter reads this as growing irreverence that later made harsher portrayals possible.
  • Gilligan's Island: two Navy veterans used mainly for physical comedy. It began just after the Tonkin Gulf Resolution but ended before Tet changed the war's public meaning.
  • M\*A\*S\*H: set in Korea but read as commentary on Vietnam. A documentary-style interview episode, with much improvised response, is singled out as evidence of closeness to frontline experience and alignment with antiwar feeling.
  • Historical bridge: World War II as a widely supported war, with about sixteen million in service; Vietnam as a decolonization war absorbed into Cold War containment; televised casualty lists, the Tet Offensive, Walter Cronkite's stalemate judgment, Kent State, withdrawal in 1973, and the fall of Saigon in 1975. Returning Vietnam veterans, many of them draftees, are described as facing indifference or hostility rather than parades, with trauma later understood as PTSD.
  • Reagan-era turn: Reagan is presented as calling Vietnam a noble cause, expanding veterans' services, and supporting completion of the Vietnam War Memorial, although planning began under Carter. Production of Magnum P.I. began in December 1980, just before Reagan's inauguration.
  • Magnum P.I.: Thomas Magnum, a former Navy SEAL and intelligence officer, is haunted by Vietnam flashbacks and lost comrades but remains a reliable friend, neighbor, and investigator. The chapter contrasts him with the film character John Rambo, who cannot live normally in civilian society, and cites letters from veterans thanking creator Donald Bellisario for a non-stereotyped portrayal.
  • The A-Team: four former Army Special Forces soldiers, on the run after a mission gone wrong and a false accusation, use their distinct skills together to help civilians. The chapter acknowledges cartoonish action and stereotyping, but stresses camaraderie, loyalty, and a search for redemption.
  • Miami Vice is named in the introduction as part of the 1980s veteran-centered action boom, but the detailed analysis centers on Magnum P.I. and The A-Team.

What it leaves open: it does not show, with ratings, surveys, or audience research, that these shows caused the public shift rather than riding a shift already underway. The Reagan framing is complicated by the chapter's own dating, since Magnum P.I. production began before Reagan took office. The sample is narrow: it does not systematically examine women veterans, Black or Latino veterans, veterans in news or documentary after Vietnam, or less heroic 1980s portrayals. Its Vietnam history section should be checked against other sources before reuse, since its dating and description of the peace accords sit uneasily with its own statement that the last troops left in March 1973.

Chapter 10: Who Has The Power?: The Unique Landscape of Children's Television in the 1980s, by Aaidin Finefield

Argument: the central force in 1980s children's television was not education or child development, but corporate risk management. When Reagan-era deregulation removed restraints, advertisers and toy companies stopped merely buying commercial breaks and began shaping, financing, or co-owning the programs themselves. The result was a flood of product-based shows, lower and more formulaic quality, and representations chosen because they seemed safe and profitable for a presumed white middle-class audience. Sesame Street is presented as the important exception that proves how different the field could have been, not as the model the industry followed.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Sesame Street (Children's Television Workshop, premiered 1969) and Mister Rogers' Neighborhood: built with educators, child-development experts, and social-issue specialists sharing decision power; diverse casting; willingness to address difficult topics such as death and disability; later responsiveness to criticism about bilingual representation. The chapter cites about nine million daily child viewers by 1976 and a small later school-grade advantage for preschool viewers.
  • Earlier educational television, including TV Kindergarten and Merlin the Magician, and growth to 140 noncommercial educational stations by 1967, many serving schools and workplaces rather than homes.
  • Regulation and its reversal: the Johnson-era National Commission on the Causes and Prevention of Violence, Federal Communications Commission pressure for self-regulation, and Action for Children's Television lobbying in 1971 for closer scrutiny of commercials in children's programs. Under Reagan, the FCC lifted limits, including by 1984 the restriction on unrestricted commercial time, opening the way for program-length commercials.
  • The advertisers' problem: remote controls, mute buttons, VCRs (in half of US households by 1987), and cable channels such as MTV, the Weather Channel, and C-SPAN weakened the old commercial break. Market research also found that older children increasingly ignored or resented ads, while younger children tended to accept ad claims uncritically. Advertisers therefore moved into the programs that attracted children.
  • The toy industry's seasonal risk: toys traditionally sold mainly at holidays and birthdays, so a failed season could be costly. Sesame Street merchandise, sold year-round, showed an alternative. Toy companies sought the same stability through television.
  • Character licensing and consolidation, in stages: Strawberry Shortcake (1980), developed by Those Characters From Cleveland, a collaboration involving American Greetings and General Mills' toy design group, growing from a card image to a doll, then a show and wider merchandise, into a billion-dollar enterprise. The Smurfs: rights held by SEPP International, animation by Hanna-Barbera, broadcast by NBC, products by Wallace Berrie, showing a split but coordinated franchise. He-Man and the Masters of the Universe (1983): a closer Mattel and Filmation arrangement, co-financed by Group W, treated as a turning point for syndicated children's production. ThunderCats: distribution, animation, licensing, and toy firms sharing profits and creative say, presented as the fullest merger of advertiser and producer interests.
  • Growth figures given: product-based shows from zero in 1980 to fourteen in 1982 and forty in 1984; licensed product revenue from 26.7 billion dollars in 1983 to 64.6 billion in 1988. Older non-product shows such as Fat Albert and Captain Kangaroo ended around the start of the decade.
  • Quality: many shows are described as formulaic, with repeated backgrounds, limited animation, and near-identical hero plots in He-Man and ThunderCats. NBC's Adventures of the Gummi Bears is offered as a show created to sell a candy of the same name. A 1994 survey is cited in which most surveyed parents found Saturday morning cartoons felt like one long commercial and worried about materialism.
  • Race: earlier progress associated with Sesame Street, Fat Albert and the Cosby Kids, and The New Mickey Mouse Club is contrasted with 1980s patterns. Black characters in live action were often shown as hostile within their own group but friendly to white characters; animation allowed producers to avoid assigning real-world race to characters; Native American representation remained marginal and prejudiced; Asian American characters were confined to narrow supporting types such as sidekick, newscaster, or mystic; Latino and Hispanic audiences were largely served by imported Spanish-language programming or dubbing rather than original US production. Public-service spots, by contrast, tended to show diverse urban children, while product ads tended to show white children in rural settings.
  • Gender: progress in the 1960s and 1970s was limited, then stalled. A 1974 study found fewer than half of the ten most popular children's programs had even one female character; a 1982 study found three times as many male as female characters. Advertisers are said to have assumed girls would watch boys' shows but boys would not watch girls' shows, so fewer girls' programs were made. The Smurfs is used as an example of extreme imbalance, with one female character among many male characters. She-Ra, added after girls watched He-Man, is read as a secondary female version of the male hero rather than an equal lead.
  • Present-day coda: later rules ended the most blatant program-length commercials, but product placement and revivals continue, including a 2021 Strawberry Shortcake series and a 2022 Transformers series. The Owl House is cited as a recent case in which broad representation still required a fight with the studio.

What it leaves open: risk management is a persuasive organizing idea, but the chapter infers motive mainly from business structures and outcomes, not from a systematic set of internal company documents. It does not demonstrate direct effects on children from the race and gender patterns it describes, as opposed to correlation with wider social attitudes. Much of that section rests on secondary studies rather than new coding of a defined sample of 1980s episodes and ads. It does not fully separate deregulation from cable, syndication, VCRs, and changes in toy retail, all of which it also names.

Chapter 11: Civil Rights, NFL Television Culture and Integration, by Daniel Smith

Argument: the NFL mattered to integration and to the wider civil rights story not only because it signed Black players, but because it did so while becoming a national television pastime. Cold War pressure made American racism a foreign-policy liability, and federal action, municipal leverage, competitive self-interest, and publicity together broke the league's color line. The final holdout, Washington, was forced into line when the Kennedy administration used control of a new federally controlled stadium. Television then carried the integrated league to a mass audience, helping football overtake baseball as the dominant American sport.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Opening contrast: Black World War II veterans returned to segregation and violence, illustrated by the 1946 murder of George Dorsey and three companions. The Soviet Union used American racism in propaganda, and Truman responded with a pro-civil-rights posture, including desegregation of the armed forces by Executive Order 9981 in 1948, implemented unevenly through the Korean War.
  • Brown v. Board of Education (1954), Southern massive resistance, and Kennedy's 1963 civil rights address are used as the federal timeline, followed by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965.
  • Early NFL history: Fritz Pollard played and coached in the 1920s; in the 1930s owners stopped signing Black players under an informal agreement the chapter attributes chiefly to Washington owner George Preston Marshall. The league remained effectively white-only until 1946.
  • First reintegration moves in 1946: Cleveland Browns owner Paul Brown signed Bill Willis and Marion Motley, both later Hall of Fame players. The Los Angeles Rams, after moving from Cleveland, signed Kenny Washington and Woody Strode after the LA Coliseum lease required Black players and after pressure from Los Angeles Sentinel sports reporter Halley Harding.
  • Television growth: the New York Giants experimented with NBC broadcasts in 1947; owners feared telecasts would hurt gate receipts, and the Philadelphia Eagles banned home telecasts after attendance concerns. In 1951 owners voted eleven to one to allow telecasts in home territory with both clubs' permission. CBS's 1956 contract used nearly 200 affiliates to carry 63 games for eleven of twelve teams. Television ownership grew from about 12,000 sets in 1946 to about 24 million homes by 1954.
  • The 1958 NFL Championship between the Giants and Baltimore Colts, broadcast by NBC, drew about 45 million viewers, went to overtime, and is presented as a turning point that helped create the rival American Football League in 1959.
  • Blackout policy: games were withheld locally to protect ticket sales, including the 1957 championship in Detroit and the 1962 Giants-Packers game in New York. The first seven Super Bowls were not televised in their host cities. Congress changed the rule in 1973 for games sold out 72 hours before kickoff.
  • Southern barriers: Black players on integrated teams could not always stay in the same hotels or eat with teammates in the segregated South.
  • By 1960, every team except Washington had integrated, including the new Dallas Cowboys.
  • Washington showdown: Marshall defended an all-white roster by claiming the team recruited from segregated Southern colleges and served a Southern radio and television audience, and at one point said the team would sign Black players when the Harlem Globetrotters signed white players. Kennedy's Executive Order 10925 in 1961 created a federal equal-employment committee. Interior Secretary Stewart Udall wrote to Marshall, then proposed writing integration into the lease for the new federally controlled stadium. Press coverage spread the story nationally. Udall also enlisted Commissioner Pete Rozelle. In August 1961 Marshall conceded and promised to draft a Black player. Washington drafted Ernie Davis, who refused to play for Marshall and was traded to Cleveland for Bobby Mitchell. Mitchell's Hall of Fame Washington career is presented as completing NFL integration.
  • Aftermath: AFL competition, the 1966 merger announcement and full merger in 1970, the first Super Bowl in January 1967, and ABC's Monday Night Football from September 19, 1970, produced by Roone Arledge. The chapter closes with modern markers: Super Bowl LVII in 2023 as the first Super Bowl with two Black starting quarterbacks, and large 2022 audiences for Monday Night Football and NBC's Sunday Night Football.

What it leaves open: the television argument is the weakest causal link. Integration began in 1946, before mass television, and the strongest mechanisms shown are a stadium lease, Black press pressure, talent competition, and federal lease power. The chapter shows television and integration growing in parallel, and shows that integration disputes received national publicity, but it does not show that broadcasts changed owners' decisions or white viewers' racial attitudes. It gives limited space to Black agency beyond Harding, boycott calls, and fan letters, and little attention to Black coaches, executives, quarterbacks, or later barriers after roster integration.

Chapter 12: The Best Trash of the Lot: Dallas, Melodrama, and 1980s America, by Cathryn Jones

Argument: Dallas succeeded by taking daytime soap opera tools into primetime and making them respectable for a mass audience. It kept soap melodrama and an open, cliffhanger-driven story, but moved the action to primetime, centered men and business conflict rather than women's relationships, and set the story in a wealthy Texas oil family on a working ranch. That combination let it offer both Reagan-era nostalgia for an imagined stable 1950s family order and the material glamour of the 1980s. Its pleasure, in this reading, came from emotional realism rather than literal realism: viewers could play with a fantasy world whose feelings and family dilemmas felt recognizable.

Key programs, people, institutions, and evidence:

  • Historical frame: the Great Society's expansion of civil liberties, followed by 1970s stagflation, the 1973 oil shock, and defeat in Vietnam, is presented as the background for a conservative turn. Reagan is presented as promising economic revival, intensifying Nixon's war on crime, pursuing deregulation, and appealing to a return to a simpler past. Scholar Michael Dwyer's term for that imagined past is the fantasy fifties.
  • Cowboy mythology: work by Christopher Le Coney and Zoe Trodd on Reagan-era cowboy dreams is used to link rugged masculinity, the nuclear family, and an attempt to rewrite Vietnam defeat. Contemporary examples include Back to the Future, Grease, and Madonna's Material Girl video referencing Marilyn Monroe.
  • Genre theory: serial melodrama is defined by seriality (open, continuing narrative with cliffhangers and no fixed endpoint) and melodrama (psychic realism plus moral legibility). Psychic realism means characters feel emotionally true even in exaggerated plots; moral legibility means a contemporary moral problem is dramatized and made understandable.
  • Daytime soap history: cheap daytime serials filled airtime, served as advertising vehicles for household products, and were built around housewives' domestic routines, with repetition before and after commercials so a viewer could follow while working. Scholar Robert C. Allen is cited on soap opera's dismissal as trash, its association with dirt and gossip, and its resistance to closure. Scholar Mary Cassata is cited as doubting whether Dallas counted as a soap opera because it was less predictable than daytime serials. Daytime shows are cited as having 49.2 percent women characters, compared with 29 percent in primetime.
  • Dallas itself: created by David Jacobs, aired on CBS from 1978 to 1991, revived in 2012. It follows the Ewing oil family at Southfork: patriarch Jock and wife Ellie, sons Bobby and J.R., their wives Pamela and Sue Ellen, estranged son Gary, and Gary's daughter Lucy. J.R.'s weekly scheme is a recurring beat. The show had no planned endpoint, and its instability is illustrated by killing Bobby in a season-eight cliffhanger and then revealing season nine as Pamela's dream.
  • Who Shot J.R.?: the shooting cliffhanger became a national guessing game, advertised so heavily that even non-viewers knew it. The shooter was J.R.'s sister-in-law Kristin Shepard, with whom he had an affair. The storyline also marks a shift from Pamela's entry into the family toward J.R. as the center.
  • Male centrality and the male gaze: unlike daytime soaps, primetime serials centered men. Laura Mulvey's film-theory idea of the male gaze is extended by the author to television: women are at times presented as visual objects for male characters and viewers, and women's stories are rerouted through men's reactions. In season one, episode two, Lessons, Pamela tries to guide Lucy back to school and secure her place in the family. The discovery that Lucy is involved with older ranch hand Ray Krebbs creates a triangle involving Ray, Pamela, and Bobby. The chapter argues that the Pamela-Lucy relationship is displaced by the Bobby-Ray confrontation, and that a disco scene films Pamela from Bobby's point of view in a way that makes her a visual object. Pamela's agency is largely framed as becoming a good wife and future mother. Ray is later revealed as Jock's illegitimate son, making Lucy his niece. Ray keeps the secret to protect the family, and Ellie forgives Jock within one episode, reinforcing Ellie as the forgiving Southern matriarch. In season one, episode four, Winds of Vengeance, relatives of women involved with J.R. and Ray take the family hostage. The chapter reads the episode as drawing sharp boundaries: outsiders are dangerous, working-class intruders are cruel unless they assimilate like Pamela, women are exposed by men's actions, and wealthy Ewing men restore order.
  • Class and setting: Dallas and Dynasty are presented as part of a primetime pattern of focusing on an upper-class family and business rivals while working-class characters are largely absent. Southfork combines oil wealth with ranch life, producing a nostalgic western setting updated with discos, fashion, cars, and technology. Pamela works, but in a clothing shop coded as temporary rather than in an executive role like Bobby or J.R.; Lucy has premarital relationships without permanent punishment. The chapter compares the show to Giant (1956) and to rival-family romance plots.
  • Audience pleasure: Ien Ang's study of 42 letters from Dutch Dallas fans is used for escapism, glamour, and emotional realness. One fan's emphasis on show, expensive clothes, and horses is used to support the idea of flight into fantasy without denying reality. Producer Leonard Katzman and actor Larry Hagman are cited for a recession-era explanation: during hard times, viewers enjoyed imagining Ewing wealth from home, since a night out was expensive.

What it leaves open: the author states the scope limit explicitly. Mainly early seasons are covered, with only a few later plot points, because the show's popularity took off early. Later Dallas, Dynasty, and the wider primetime-soap cycle are therefore not fully tested against the argument. The audience case rests heavily on Dutch fan letters for a claim about American fantasy-fifties nostalgia, without a comparable American audience study or ratings breakdown. The link to Reagan is congruence, not demonstrated causation. Extending Mulvey's film-based male gaze to television is the author's argued extension, not an established application, and it rests mainly on close reading of a few early episodes. Race, and the whiteness of the Ewing-centered fantasy, are less developed than gender and class.

Key terms

  • Cold War television cultures: the volume's term for how television, in different countries and industries, carried, staged, and reshaped Cold War politics, not just reflected them.
  • Ideological projection: using a broadcast, event, or program to present a political system, nation, or way of life as attractive, normal, or superior.
  • Industrial conditions: the ownership, funding, regulation, distribution, and advertising arrangements that decide what gets made and shown, as distinct from a program's script alone.
  • Soft diplomacy: using a televised ceremony, speech, or cultural event to build goodwill and influence abroad without military force, as in the coronation and satellite speeches.
  • Telepolitics: politics conducted through television formats, such as Italy's Tribuna Politica, where access and staging shape who is heard.
  • Liveness: the claim that an event is happening as viewers watch. The volume shows liveness did not guarantee impact: the coronation gained power from being seen at home despite poor picture quality, while the Tumbler-Snapper blast lost power at home for the same technical reason.
  • Domestication: in chapter 4, the process by which framing and technical limits made a terrifying weapon look manageable, brief, and ordinary on home screens.
  • Atoms for Peace framing: the two-sided 1950s split between peaceful, American-controlled nuclear energy and a destructive atom dangerous mainly in foreign, especially Soviet, hands.
  • Program-length commercial: a children's program effectively built to sell a toy or product line, rather than a show interrupted by separate ads.
  • Product-based show: a program whose characters, story, and merchandise were developed together by toy, licensing, and production firms to reduce seasonal sales risk.
  • Seriality: an open, continuing narrative with cliffhangers and no fixed endpoint, central to soap opera and to Dallas.
  • Psychic realism: characters feeling emotionally true even in exaggerated plots.
  • Moral legibility: a contemporary moral problem dramatized in a way that makes it understandable, even if the plot itself is implausible.
  • Fantasy fifties: Michael Dwyer's term, as used in chapter 12, for an imagined stable 1950s family order invoked in Reagan-era nostalgia.
  • Televised proxy contest: a non-military competition, such as the 1972 chess championship, refitted by television into a contest between political systems.

What the book does NOT prove, and what it leaves open

  • Audience effects: this is the volume's largest shared limit. Most chapters establish frames, popularity, business structures, or vivid examples, not measured changes in belief, voting, or behavior. Chapter 7 says this plainly for atomic television. Chapters 5, 6, 9, and 12 infer influence from timing, ratings, letters, or content, without systematic audience research.
  • Creation versus amplification: the chapters often cannot separate television creating a belief from television selecting, dramatizing, or undermining a belief already in circulation. Chapter 6, on Vietnam, is the clearest case where television worked against official hopes.
  • Single thesis: because this is an edited volume by different authors, there is no single argument proven across all twelve cases. Some chapters show direct state or industry control, such as RAI in Italy and Disney's Our Friend the Atom. Others show indirect cultural influence, such as I Led 3 Lives, with no evidence of state funding or production control.
  • Soviet and non-American reception: Soviet television, Soviet domestic reception, and audiences outside Britain and the United States are largely absent or thin, except where a chapter uses Soviet press criticism or secondary accounts. The chess chapter, for example, says little about Soviet broadcast evidence or Icelandic coverage.
  • Local and dissenting voices: the focus is national programs, networks, leagues, and Washington institutions. Local stations, Black press beyond the NFL chapter, labor press, non-English broadcasting, and organized dissent get less sustained treatment than the central programs.
  • Period limits: the book ends its cases in the 1980s, before later changes in cable, satellite, and streaming, and before later revelations about state-media relations. Its claims about routines should not be stretched forward without new evidence.
  • Student-project limits: as a senior capstone volume, most chapters synthesize secondary scholarship and close readings rather than presenting new archival research or new audience data. Some details flagged by the chapter readers should be checked before reuse, including chapter 9's Vietnam chronology, chapter 11's Cleveland-to-Miami travel example, and chapter 4's varying spelling of the KTLA reporter's name.
  • Coverage in these notes: all twelve chapters were read in full from the PDF, including their endnotes as summarized for scope. Endnotes are not reproduced line by line here. Non-book material was excluded. The DOI landing page itself failed to fetch and was not retried; the PDF was used instead, so any DOI-only front matter beyond the PDF is not covered.

Questions worth carrying forward

  • When a news or entertainment routine treats an official statement, a sponsor, or a league as the story, what context would a viewer need to judge it, and who supplies that context if not the newsroom or the show?
  • In which cases did television create a belief, in which did it select and dramatize a belief already in circulation, and in which did it undermine the belief its sponsors or officials hoped to spread?
  • Does voluntary or commercial cooperation with government become propaganda at the point of script approval, at the point of exclusive access, at the point of undisclosed sponsorship, or only when the audience is misled about authorship?
  • If anticommunism, consumer capitalism, or a presumed white middle-class audience counted as neutral in these decades, what present-day assumptions might be doing the same work inside today's neutrality, recommendation, and product-placement routines?
  • How would the argument change if local stations, non-English broadcasting, dissenting publications, or Soviet and other non-American audiences were centered instead of the three networks and the flagship programs?
  • What would count as good evidence of television persuasion here: ratings, letters, polls, policy change, or something else, and why do so many of these chapters have to settle for less?
