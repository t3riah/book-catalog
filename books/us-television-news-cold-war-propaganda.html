Study notes: U.S. Television News and Cold War Propaganda, 1947-1960

Date: 2026-11-04

Author: Nancy E. Bernhard (Cambridge University Press, 1999)

Thesis in short

From about 1948 to 1954, government information officers and young network news divisions jointly produced programs presented as news or public affairs, with government supplying scripts, film, and officials and networks supplying airtime and legitimacy. It worked because each side got what it needed, and it was shielded by free enterprise rhetoric and an objectivity routine that treated repeating official statements as neutral. Open co-production faded in the mid 1950s, but official source dependence and anticommunism built into the neutrality standard stayed.

These are original study notes, paraphrased throughout, saved together with the thesis above in this one file. Quotations are kept to a phrase or a short sentence. The Scribd page adds a FAQ block after the index; that block is not part of Bernhard's book and is not used here.

Overall summary

Television news and the Cold War grew up together. Bernhard argues that early network news did not simply report the Cold War from a distance. In the years roughly 1948 to 1954, government information officers and network news divisions worked together so closely that many programs presented as news or public affairs were scripted, produced, or approved by the White House, the State Department, or the Defense Department.

The deal made sense to both sides, for different reasons. Government officials wanted public support for a long, expensive policy of containment, but direct government propaganda at home was politically toxic. Networks were young, losing money on news, short of film and access, and afraid of sponsor boycotts and blacklisting. Free government material, official guests, and a reputation for public service solved several problems at once.

The result, in Bernhard's reading, was a picture of the Cold War built around consumer capitalism: freedom meant private ownership, abundant goods, and the absence of government control, even when the programs themselves were shaped by government. As the networks grew richer and more capable in the mid 1950s, the open co-production faded, but the habits stayed: dependence on official sources, a narrow range of acceptable debate, and a professional code in which strong anticommunism counted as neutral.

Central thesis

Bernhard's thesis is that the early Cold War consensus was not simply discovered by television news, it was manufactured through a joint public-private information operation. Institutions, not only individual beliefs, did the work. Government wanted control of the message, networks wanted cheap content, access, sponsor safety, and legitimacy, and the market rhetoric of free enterprise covered the partnership from scrutiny. Objectivity, as practiced then, did not prevent this partnership. It enabled it, because reporting official statements without interpretation looked objective while it passed official framing straight to viewers.

A second, related claim: this was not a single conspiracy with a central plot. It was a system of overlapping institutional interests and shared beliefs, limited mainly by competition between networks, friction between agencies, and the profit motive itself.

Introduction: The Marketplace of Ideas

Argument: the book studies how programs were produced, not whether they changed any individual viewer's mind. Bernhard sets out three institutional logics.

  • Government: officials did not trust an unmanaged marketplace of ideas to sustain a long arms buildup, so they built offices to steer information at home and abroad.
  • Networks: news divisions were small, unprofitable, and dependent on government film and newsmakers.
  • Culture: private, advertiser supported broadcasting was already equated with freedom, so a program that looked commercial and voluntary looked free, even when its content came from the state.

Key point: voluntarism was the system. Public service time, required in exchange for use of public airwaves, became a distribution channel for official material. Government avoided the look of direct control by not tying itself to one network or one broadcaster, and networks avoided the look of propaganda by keeping the programs competitive and ordinary.

What to notice: Bernhard is explicit that effects are outside the scope. She does not claim a measured effect on public opinion, and she warns that opinion research from the period was itself shaped by government and military programs.

Chapter 1: Market Failure

Title meaning: private enterprise, left alone, failed to deliver the kind of coordinated information the national security state wanted. So the state took over the job it had once hoped business would do.

Argument in steps:

  • World War II: the Office of War Information (OWI) claimed truth as its weapon, under Elmer Davis, with Byron Price running voluntary censorship. It was attacked from Congress as partisan and liberal, from other agencies as a bottleneck, and from its own writers when business executives in the domestic branch favored slick sales campaigns over plain facts.
  • Private triumph, briefly: the War Advertising Council, later the Advertising Council, ran more than 100 wartime campaigns at huge donated value. That success made private, voluntary information look superior to any government agency, and it set the pattern for postwar selling of America.
  • The revolving door: business leaders moved between corporate boards, advisory committees, and Washington. Paul Hoffman of Studebaker, for example, helped shape Marshall Plan legislation and then ran the agency that administered the aid. Hundreds of advisory committees linked business expertise to federal policy.
  • Overseas breakdown: the Associated Press withdrew its service from the State Department in 1946, arguing that government use tainted its reputation and competed with private expansion. The State Department replied that private news did not give foreign audiences a full picture of U.S. policy. A study by newspaper editors reluctantly backed continued overseas programs.
  • Voice of America (VOA): William Benton, advertising executive turned assistant secretary of state for public affairs, tried a public corporation and then a private foundation plan to keep VOA at arm's length from government. Neither survived congressional suspicion and the networks' own reluctance. By 1948, after a congressional investigation attacked NBC programs for VOA as vulgar or misleading, NBC and CBS canceled their VOA contracts and handed programming to the State Department.

People and institutions: Elmer Davis, Byron Price, Gardner Cowles Jr., William Benton, Paul Hoffman, the OWI, the War Advertising Council, the Associated Press, CBS, NBC, the Smith-Mundt Act of 1948, and the U.S. Advisory Commission on Information.

Evidence to remember: the argument rests on appropriations fights, internal memos, congressional hearings, and the sequence of VOA plans. The chapter's payoff is the reversal: in 1946 private control looked triumphant; by 1948 state management of overseas information was accepted, and domestic cooperation through private broadcasters became the next step.

Chapter 2: A Weapon for Truth

Argument: television news looked legitimate, and therefore its government collaborations went unnoticed, because three cultural beliefs lined up.

1. Technology: television was described as an X-ray that exposed character and a mirror that merely reflected reality. Both metaphors hid the people choosing, framing, and editing.

2. Capitalism: corporate ownership was equated with a free marketplace of ideas. Criticism of the oligopoly itself, including the FCC spectrum decisions that favored VHF and entrenched NBC and CBS, was pushed aside.

3. Professionalism: a narrow code of objectivity, reporting who, what, when, and where without interpretation, protected news divisions from charges of corporate bias. It also made simply repeating official statements look like the highest professional standard.

Details that matter:

  • Early TV news was thin: no network camera crews until the early 1950s, reliance on newsreel film flown to New York, 15 minute nightly newscasts, and panel and interview shows that mattered more because they were live and featured newsmakers. In the 1950s, about 85 percent of Meet the Press guests were government officials.
  • Growth was fast: TV households rose from about 9 percent in 1950 to about 86 percent in 1959 in the book's table, and stations rose from 69 to 609 over the same span.
  • Blacklisting shaped the range: Red Channels (1950) listed 151 people in radio and television, including only three network news figures at CBS by Bernhard's count, but the climate set hiring and content codes. Networks required loyalty oaths, CBS from December 1950, and presented themselves as reluctant victims of blacklisters even while they enforced the lists to protect sponsors.
  • The FCC freeze, September 1948 to April 1952, limited new licenses and, in Bernhard's account, helped NBC and CBS while weakening DuMont, ABC, and would-be competitors.

People and institutions: Edward R. Murrow, Walter Cronkite, Lawrence Spivak, Frank Stanton, William Paley, David Sarnoff, RCA, the FCC, the National Association of Broadcasters (NAB), American Business Consultants, and Counterattack.

Chapter 3: Clearer Than Truth

Argument: the State Department's domestic information staff sold containment policy more successfully than it sold itself. It wanted to be seen as a truthful service, but under pressure from McCarthy, Korea, and NSC-68, it moved toward scare campaigns and scripted television.

Key episodes:

  • The department's public affairs staff (PA), 215 to 264 people for domestic information between 1947 and 1952, was caught between Congress, which distrusted it, and the foreign service, which disdained publicity work. Director Francis Russell tried to keep domestic and overseas work separate, fearing that the public would learn the same propaganda machine was aimed at them.
  • Early television: Your Foreign Policy (1948), World Spotlight on Berlin and the North Atlantic Pact, and later World Briefing and Diplomatic Pouch. The department learned to hide government authorship, for example by billing a show as an interview of the department rather than a program made in cooperation with it, and by scripting questions in advance while preserving an appearance of spontaneity.
  • Films: government film units lent films free to networks. DuMont ran some as Our Secret Weapon: The Truth; ABC gathered others as Everybody's Business. Brief end-of-program disclaimers attracted little notice, and later network produced programs with government help carried no equivalent disclaimer.
  • McCarthy: after the Wheeling speech of February 9, 1950, the department tried point-by-point rebuttal, then personal access for friendly journalists, then a Public Relations Working Group. Senior officers avoided unscripted television, which left McCarthy's dynamic charges largely unanswered on the medium that could have humanized Secretary Dean Acheson.
  • NSC-68 and the scare campaign: Edward Barrett, assistant secretary for public affairs, welcomed the call for massive rearmament but warned that a democracy would tire of a long arms race without a public relations campaign. He proposed building public awareness of the threat first, then presenting government programs to meet it. Truman's Campaign of Truth speech and Acheson's companion speech to newspaper editors in 1950 fit that sequence.
  • Korea: after China's intervention in late 1950, support for the war fell, and Barrett urged a message that combined toughness with restraint: massive mobilization as the way to preserve peace. A 1951 opinion study found the paradox that Americans approved many individual foreign policies while distrusting overall foreign policy. Bernhard's verdict: PA sold the policies but failed to sell the department.
  • End of the chapter's arc: Barrett resigned in January 1952 after an appropriations fight. Overseas information moved out of the department in 1953 as the U.S. Information Agency (USIA). Domestic public affairs became more openly a public relations staff job under Eisenhower.

People: Francis Russell, Edward Barrett, Dean Acheson, William Benton, George Allen, Howland Sargeant, Carl McCardle, C. D. Jackson, Paul Nitze.

Programs: Your Foreign Policy, World Spotlight, World Briefing, Diplomatic Pouch, The Marshall Plan in Action (later Strength for a Free World, ABC, 1950 to 1953).

Chapter 4: Ready, Willing, Able

Argument: during the Korean War, the broadcasting industry volunteered so thoroughly that no new official wartime information agency was needed. Voluntarism protected corporate control while delivering state-formulated information.

Key episodes:

  • Panic, then profit: at the war's start, broadcasters feared plant conversion, parts shortages, sponsor flight to radio, and staff losses to the draft. Those fears eased. TV set sales continued, and network income moved from a $10 million deficit in 1950 to a $71 million profit in 1953 in Bernhard's figures.
  • The Broadcasters Advisory Council (BAC): the NAB, under president Justin Miller, maneuvered to be recognized by the White House as the industry's organizer. Assistant to the President John Steelman wrote the requested letter, and the NAB issued a resolution that broadcasting was ready, willing, and able to support national security. The council's membership favored NAB stations, which drew complaints from unaffiliated stations and news directors.
  • Civil defense and signals: broadcasters lobbied against broad seizure powers in civil defense and electromagnetic radiation control bills. The compromise was CONELRAD, a joint government-industry plan in which AM stations would stagger short bursts on shared frequencies during an air raid alert so enemy planes could not navigate by continuous signals.
  • Censorship, asked for by journalists: field censorship in Korea was chaotic at first, with correspondents left to judge classification themselves and forbidden to criticize command decisions. CBS killed a Murrow report from August 1950 that criticized a costly offensive, plus other critical cables that week. In a striking turn, the Overseas Press Club and senior correspondents asked for formal censorship to get clear rules. Formal censorship then removed not only military secrets but also material judged harmful to morale or embarrassing to the United States and its allies.
  • The Defense Bulletin: the NAB distributed White House gathered material to all stations, with scripts and public service suggestions on recruiting, savings bonds, the Crusade for Freedom, and United Nations Day. It never reached its planned frequency and faded by late 1951 as the war stalemated and association with the Truman administration lost public relations value.
  • Briefings as pressure: in December 1950, BAC members met Acheson, Truman, Defense Secretary George Marshall, and Commerce officials. The message was consistent: preserve morale, do not frighten the public, and do not undermine confidence in leaders by airing critics. In return, broadcasters received exclusive briefings and unreleased film.

People and institutions: Justin Miller, Ralph Hardy, John Steelman, David Sarnoff, Edward R. Murrow, Bill Downs, General Douglas MacArthur, George Marshall, the NAB, the Advertising Council, the Defense Bulletin.

Chapter 5: Closer to Your Government

Case study: NBC's Battle Report: Washington, 1950 to 1952, hosted and produced in the office of Truman aide John Steelman.

Argument: the program shows the partnership at its most open, and its limits. It gave the White House a weekly televised report on mobilization, gave NBC prestige and congressional praise, and still failed as a commercial product and as a bipartisan project.

Key episodes:

  • Format: Steelman opened and closed each episode, introduced officials from across government, and promised viewers a firsthand account of the fight against communism. Government information officers wrote scripts. NBC supplied technical support at $1,000 to $6,000 per episode. The show ran more than two years and reached 36 markets by mid 1951, with an estimated audience around 1.8 million by NBC's calculation.
  • Origins disputed: Steelman later claimed the idea; NBC internal memos show network executives proposed it and valued the press and congressional praise it earned, including nine mentions in the Congressional Record in its first year.
  • The Jones boys: NBC sent twin cameramen Gene and Charles Jones on a three month European tour in 1951 to give the show overseas film. The State Department steered their itinerary and story choices, but their behavior in Yugoslavia and Germany embarrassed U.S. missions, and planned head-of-state visits and an Iran trip were canceled.
  • Content pattern: scripts followed the NSC-68 formula, dire threat followed by reassuring American countermeasures. The one surviving film episode, the fiftieth of 84, contrasts a communist youth rally in Berlin with American youth programs, cutting communist rally film against Nazi rally film. Steelman's language for communist leaders was florid and demonizing.
  • Consumerism as argument: Steelman repeatedly linked ownership of cars, radios, television sets, and savings bonds to the stakes of the Cold War. A person with ordinary possessions, in his framing, would be a target if Soviet forces prevailed.
  • Sponsorship and partisanship: NBC tried to sell the show as institutional advertising, with the White House vetting acceptable sponsors and rejecting products it deemed undignified. No national sponsor materialized. Republicans charged that the show was Democratic propaganda and asked for equal time. NBC replied that guests appeared as officials, not partisans, and that the program fell outside political controversy. NBC canceled the show in spring 1952, before the election season, to save money and reduce vulnerability to partisan attack.

Related programs: CBS's The Facts We Face, with White House script approval; CBS radio's One Nation Indivisible; DuMont's Pentagon.

Chapter 6: A More Vivid Picture of War

Argument: the Defense Department adapted best to television because war, weapons, and military life dramatize more easily than diplomacy. Over the 1950s, its cooperation with networks moved from dull public affairs filler to popular documentaries and then to prime time drama, and the government hand became harder to see.

Stages:

  • Organization: the National Security Act of 1947 created the Defense Department, the National Security Council, and the CIA. Secretary James Forrestal wanted domestic information coordinated with foreign policy, but early unification was messy, with service rivalries and frequent changes at the top of public affairs. The Office of Public Information (OPI) radio-TV branch supplied film, clips, speakers, and access, and measured success partly by the commercial value of donated airtime, over $1.7 million for television in 1950 by its chief's estimate.
  • Early filler: The Armed Forces Hour (NBC, then DuMont, 1949 to 1951) mixed bands, training films, and discussion, but NBC found it lacked news value and dropped it. The Big Picture, an Army series begun on CBS in late 1951, ran far longer, eventually 823 episodes and syndication to 1957 and beyond in the book's account, with an explicit opening claim to be an official Army report.
  • Documentaries: March of Time's Crusade in Europe (ABC, 1949) and Crusade in the Pacific used Pentagon archives and script review, with the Pacific series more closely supervised. NBC's Victory at Sea (1952 to 1953) used Navy film and personnel, cost taxpayers $65,000 in the book's figure against NBC's $500,000 production allocation, won a special Peabody Award, and became a long running syndication property. CBS's Air Power (1956 to 1958) drew on Air Force archives at Wright-Patterson, opened with a simulated North American attack narrated by Walter Cronkite, and drew complaints both that it flattered the Air Force and that it was anti-American. CBS's The Twentieth Century (from 1957) used Pentagon support and moved from compilation history to contemporary biography and current affairs.
  • Drama and script approval: Pentagon policy for dramatic series required advance script submission and later review of the finished product. Navy Log (CBS, then ABC, 1955 to 1958) used Navy ships, planes, and personnel for fact based, embellished rescue and heroism stories. The West Point Story (1956 to 1958) used Military Academy files and locations. Steve Canyon (NBC, 1958 to 1959) used Air Force footage, including missile and hydrogen bomb test material, and was valued as a recruiting aid. Behind Closed Doors (NBC, 1958 to 1959) drew on counterintelligence files. Men in Space (CBS, 1959 to 1960) kept Defense script approval and aimed for scientific plausibility after Sputnik.
  • A late twist: in 1956, the Joint Chiefs' Subsidiary Activities Division worked to weave themes from the secret domestic psychological warfare program Militant Liberty into Hollywood and television productions, including work associated with John Ford and John Wayne.

People: James Forrestal, Charles Dillon, Andrew Berding, Henry Salomon, Robert Sarnoff, Perry Wolff, Sam Gallu, Walter Cronkite.

Chapter 7: The Most Vigorous Anticommunist Campaign

Argument: early Cold War objectivity was not neutral. It was historically specific, and anticommunism was built into it. Journalists could be combative and still supportive, because their combat policed the consensus rather than challenging it.

Steps:

  • Print literature is incomplete: earlier accounts of McCarthy and the press argued that detached, fact only reporting trapped journalists into amplifying McCarthy's charges. Bernhard notes that this describes mainly elite papers and wire services. Other papers were openly partisan, and television is missing from that literature altogether.
  • Television spectrum: at one end, NBC's America at Mid-Century (January 1951) warned in lurid terms about communism while claiming to present only compiled facts, drawing on sources including the Chamber of Commerce, Plain Talk, and a House Un-American Activities Committee pamphlet. At the other end, CBS in 1954, after Murrow's See It Now program on McCarthy, commissioned a Wisconsin journalism school content analysis of its newscasts. The newscasts scored well on avoiding advocacy, but Bernhard stresses that selection itself confers significance.
  • Paley's 1954 speech: CBS chairman William Paley called for fairness and individual responsibility, while also insisting that broadcasters must judge which ideas upheld democracy and which undermined it. In Bernhard's reading, communism was the one subject exempt from balance.
  • Meet the Press as case study: founder Lawrence Spivak presented himself as taking no left or right position, only right versus wrong, while boasting of a vigorous anticommunist campaign from the start. Bernhard uses sociologist Alvin Gouldner's term normative objectification: prevailing norms are absorbed into professional practice while the practice still claims objectivity.
  • McCarthy on the program: McCarthy appeared seven times between 1950 and 1954. Panelists pressed him on proof, and on December 13, 1953, they pressed him hard on his shifting dismissal numbers, but they did not press the civil liberties question to its conclusion. Seeking accurate numbers of supposed traitors still accepted the legitimacy of the hunt; defending the rights of the accused risked looking like defending communists.
  • Wechsler case: New York Post editor James Wechsler, a former Young Communist League member turned liberal anticommunist, was called by McCarthy's committee in spring 1953. On Meet the Press on May 17, 1953, Spivak, Frank Waldrop, and Bert Andrews treated Wechsler's complaint about press intimidation as illegitimate and tried to establish that he was still sympathetic to communism. Marquis Childs was more sympathetic but still framed McCarthy's abuses as secondary to domestic vigilance. Moderator Martha Rountree asked whether the communist danger mattered more than an individual.
  • Intellectual context: Bernhard links this to chastened liberalism, the postwar turn from optimism to hard realism after Munich, the Nazi-Soviet Pact, and Stalin's terror. In that frame, overestimating the threat looked prudent, underestimating it looked naive, and the standard for what counted as a fact shifted.

People: Lawrence Spivak, Martha Rountree, Joseph McCarthy, Roy Cohn, James Wechsler, Marquis Childs, Frank Waldrop, Bert Andrews, William Paley, Edward R. Murrow, Richard Rovere, Michael Schudson, Edwin Bayley, Hannah Arendt.

Conclusion: Selling America

Argument: Bernhard refuses both a simple conspiracy story and a simple innocence story. There was no central plot, but there was deliberate campaigning by professionals who knew they were bending press norms and justified it as patriotic necessity. The limits on cooperation came less from respect for truth than from competing institutional interests: networks competing with each other, agencies competing for control, and profit pulling against propaganda.

Two closing cases:

  • Greece and George Polk: CBS correspondent George Polk, who reported critically on the Greek Royalist government during the civil war, was murdered in Salonika in May 1948. Bernhard recounts how a committee of prominent journalists, chaired by Walter Lippmann, worked alongside official investigators, how the investigator who found a financial motive involving Greek foreign minister Constantine Tsaldaris was recalled, and how suspicion was steered toward communists and toward Polk's associates. No one was ever indicted for the murder in the account given. Only I. F. Stone, in Bernhard's telling, publicly attacked the whitewash.
  • The CIA and the press: Frank Wisner's phrase for the network of cooperative private groups was the mighty Wurlitzer. Bernhard summarizes later congressional findings: journalists and executives exchanged information with the CIA, were briefed and debriefed, provided notes and film, and in some cases acted as couriers, hosts, or recruiters. CBS figures including William Paley and Sig Mickelson appear in this section, with Paley admitting personal cooperation while denying organizational involvement. Bernhard stresses that the public record likely shows only part of the relationship.

Final costs, as Bernhard frames them:

  • Dissent was the first price: leftists were excluded, and airing dissent risked access, sponsor trouble, and charges of subversion.
  • Minority voices lost to majoritarian programming logic: giving the people what they want, measured by ratings, is not the same as protecting minority opinion in a democracy.
  • Confusion of capitalism with democracy: salesmanship replaced argument about the common good, and corporate control was treated as freedom itself, even when it narrowed debate.
  • The lasting institutional legacy is in news routines: proximity to federal power became a source of legitimacy, and context for official claims became optional.

Bernhard closes with a question she leaves open: when does defending freedom by controlling information become sabotage of the freedom being defended?

Key terms

  • Marketplace of ideas: the ideal that truth wins in open competition. In the book, a metaphor that equated commercial competition with intellectual debate and hid coordinated messaging.
  • Voluntarism: private, nominally voluntary cooperation with government, used to avoid the political cost of a state information agency.
  • National security state: the postwar pattern of institutions, attitudes, and policies built around anticommunism and containment.
  • Strategy of truth: the OWI claim that accurate information was itself the best propaganda. Bernhard shows it giving way to selection, emphasis, and scare campaigns.
  • NSC-68: the 1950 policy paper calling for massive rearmament. In the book, the key test of whether public information could sell a long arms buildup.
  • Smith-Mundt Act (1948): the law giving permanent authorization to overseas information programs and requiring maximum practicable use of private media.
  • Objectivity, early Cold War version: strict separation of fact from interpretation, reliance on official sources, and avoidance of context. In Bernhard's argument, a historically changing practice, not a timeless rule.
  • Normative objectification: Gouldner's term, as Bernhard uses it, for norms absorbed into objective routines so thoroughly that they no longer look like values.
  • Chastened liberalism: the postwar intellectual turn toward tough realism, in which alarm about totalitarianism made skepticism toward optimism look like maturity.
  • Hegemony, as Bernhard uses it: not mind control, but the power of dominant institutions to set legitimate lines of debate and exclude others, while audiences can still resist, ignore, or find other sources of meaning.
  • CONELRAD: Control of Electromagnetic Radiation, the joint plan to deny enemy navigational use of broadcast signals during an attack.
  • Militant Liberty: a secret 1955 domestic psychological warfare program whose themes defense officials tried to place in entertainment.

What the book does NOT prove, and what it leaves open

  • Audience effects: Bernhard explicitly leaves aside whether these programs changed minds or votes. She argues from production, scripts, memos, and institutional records, not from a controlled measure of persuasion.
  • Preexisting anticommunism: anticommunism predated 1947 and had genuine popular roots. The book shows amplification, selection, and framing, not creation from nothing.
  • Conspiracy versus culture: Bernhard argues the line is blurred, but she also says the campaigns do not amount to one thoroughgoing conspiracy. Where evidence is thin, especially on CIA-media ties, she flags that the public record is partial and secrecy limits what can be shown.
  • Who killed George Polk: the conclusion presents a whitewash argument and notes that how deliberate the concealment was remains unknown in key places.
  • The Soviet threat itself: Bernhard does not settle whether containment, rearmament, or any particular policy was right. She judges methods and costs, and she says the overall balance of Cold War costs and gains remains a matter of judgment.
  • Later television: the book ends in 1960, before Vietnam era changes in objectivity routines, before the 1971 Selling of the Pentagon documentary, and before later CIA-media revelations. Its claims about routines should not be stretched forward without new evidence.
  • Local stations and audiences: the focus is network news divisions, national programs, and Washington institutions. Local variation, Black press, labor press, and audience resistance get less sustained treatment than the central partnership.

Questions worth carrying forward

  • When a news routine treats an official statement as the story, what context would a viewer need to judge it, and who supplies that context if not the newsroom?
  • Does voluntary cooperation with government become propaganda at the point of script approval, at the point of exclusive access, or only when the audience is misled about authorship?
  • If anticommunism counted as neutral in the 1950s, what present day assumptions might be doing the same work inside today's neutrality routines?
  • How would the argument change if local stations, non-English broadcasting, or dissenting publications were centered instead of the three networks?
