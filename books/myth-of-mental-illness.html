The Myth of Mental Illness: Foundations of a Theory of Personal Conduct

  • Author: Thomas Szasz
  • First published: 1961, after Szasz's 1960 American Psychologist essay of the same name
  • Edition studied: 1961 Hoeber-Harper edition, 337 pages, Internet Archive mythofmentalilln00szas, 355 scans in reader
  • Structure correction: earlier notes in this thread assumed 15 chapters. The checked Contents in this edition show 16 chapters, in two books and five parts, plus an Introduction, a Summary, and an Epilogue. This file follows the edition's own structure.

Thesis in short

Szasz argues that mental illness is not a disease of the mind in the same sense as a disease of the body, because the mind is not a physical organ that can be diseased in that way. What psychiatry labels mental illness is better understood as problems in living, in communication, and in following or breaking social rules. Psychiatry should therefore be rethought as a study of personal conduct and social interaction, not as a medical science of a diseased mind.

Overall summary

Book One is largely critical and historical: it traces how hysteria and related labels grew out of neurology and medicine, and argues that the medical model was stretched beyond what it can explain. Book Two is constructive: it replaces the disease model with a model of signs, language, games, and rules, in which so-called symptoms are forms of communication and conduct within relationships.

Central thesis

Many psychiatric problems are moral, social, and communicative problems, not medical diseases, and treating them as diseases obscures the choices, conflicts, and social arrangements involved.

Preface and Introduction

Preface: Szasz presents the first part as a critical analysis of the idea of mental illness and the later part as an attempt to build a theory of personal conduct in its place. He warns that he is not simply denying that people suffer or act in troubled ways, but questioning the category used to explain that suffering.

Introduction: He contrasts thinking in terms of things or substances with thinking in terms of processes and relationships. Psychiatry, he argues, goes wrong when it treats mind as if it were a thing that could be ill, and he proposes to examine conduct, communication, and social rules instead.

Book One: The Myth of Mental Illness

Part I: Growth and Structure

#### Chapter 1: Charcot's Contribution to the Problem of Mental Illness

Charcot's work on hysteria was important less as a neurological discovery than as a social and professional turning point. Charcot, a neurologist, gave hysterical complaints a place in medicine by treating them seriously, yet the distinction between hysteria and deliberate deception remained unresolved. Hysteria raised a classification problem from the start, not simply a medical one.

#### Chapter 2: The Origins of the Problem of Mental Illness

Mental illness arose when problems of behavior and communication were fitted into a medical classification system designed for bodily disease. Szasz discusses classification, logic, and malingering to show how a label can create a category that then seems to explain the behavior it names. A classification is not itself a discovery of a disease.

#### Chapter 3: Sociology of the Therapeutic Situation

A therapeutic encounter is not a neutral medical event, but a social situation shaped by who is involved, who employs or directs the therapist, and what the surrounding society expects. Bodily illness can be described relatively independently of social setting, whereas psychosocial difficulties vary much more with social context. The therapist's role and loyalties change with the setting.

Part II: Hysteria as an Example

#### Chapter 4: Breuer and Freud's Studies in Hysteria

Early psychoanalytic work on hysteria moved the problem from neurology toward meaning, memory, and communication, without settling whether hysteria was a disease. Breuer and Freud showed hysterical complaints could be understood as meaningful, rather than as simple bodily defects. That shift opened the way to a communicative account, even if psychoanalysis kept medical language.

#### Chapter 5: Hysteria as an Example of the Medical Model

Psychiatry's competing theories, and its inability to settle basic questions by the methods of natural science, make it resemble a field of belief or politics more than a unified science. Hysteria is used as a test case: the same phenomena are explained in incompatible ways depending on the school. The medical model, applied to hysteria, produces disagreement and authority rather than settled knowledge.

#### Chapter 6: The Ethics of Hysteria, Conversion Hysteria and Psychosomatic Medicine

Szasz distinguishes between a bodily sign that can be observed and a symptom as reported and interpreted, and argues that calling a bodily complaint psychogenic does not by itself explain it. He questions psychosomatic framing, which asks whether a physical disorder is caused by the mind, suggesting that question already assumes the mind-body split it claims to investigate. Labeling a complaint as mental or psychogenic changes how the person is treated and judged.

Book Two: Foundations of a Theory of Personal Conduct

Part III: A Semiotical Analysis of Hysteria

#### Chapter 7: Hysteria, A Language

Szasz introduces a theory of signs, in which a sign is a physical thing standing between an object and a person who uses it. He distinguishes early, nonverbal signaling, called protolanguage, from later verbal language. Hysteria is reframed as nonverbal, bodily communication, rather than as a disease process in the nervous system.

#### Chapter 8: Hysteria, A Nondiscursive Language

Hysterical complaints communicate in a picture-like, bodily way, rather than in the discursive, sentence-like way of ordinary speech. Such complaints can function as appeals for attention and care, as expressions of distress, or as ways of avoiding direct statements. Understanding hysteria requires reading it as communication, not only as pathology.

#### Chapter 9: Hysteria, A Metaphorical Language

Hysterical communication is often indirect and metaphorical, using hints and bodily imagery instead of direct statements. Indirectness protects the speaker from the social risks of saying something plainly, especially when a direct statement might offend or provoke conflict. Hysteria is understood as a strategy of communication under social constraint, not merely as a failure of the nervous system.

Part IV: A Theory of Personal Conduct

#### Chapter 10: Rule-Following, Role-Taking, and the Social Organization of Personal Conduct

Conduct is analyzed as following social rules and taking social roles, with habits and learned expectations organizing behavior. Szasz contrasts a rule-based account with a purely motivational or drive-based account, arguing that much of what is called personality is better described as a pattern of learned roles. Social life is organized by shared rules, which sets up the game model.

#### Chapter 11: The Ethics of Helplessness

Modern societies teach that the weak should be helped, through family life and religious traditions, and that ethic shapes how helplessness is displayed and rewarded. A child who is helped, and a believer taught to care for the suffering, learn a social game in which helplessness can elicit care. This explains why claims of illness and disability can become socially intelligible and effective.

#### Chapter 12: The Ethics of Hysteria

Szasz draws a parallel between earlier theological explanations of witchcraft and modern psychiatric explanations of hysteria, arguing that both can obscure social and moral problems by giving them a specialized, expert explanation. He distinguishes closed, group-bound games from more universal games, and argues that mental-illness language can function like an earlier religious language: it names a problem, assigns experts, and directs moral judgment. Suffering is not denied, but the explanation system matters.

Part V: The Mental Patient as a Player in a Game

#### Chapter 13: Mead's Game Model of Human Conduct

Szasz adopts a model, associated with George Herbert Mead, in which mind and self develop through social interaction and role-taking. A person learns to see themselves from the standpoint of others, and to play social roles, through participation in shared games. This is presented as a more suitable framework than the medical model for understanding conduct, because it treats meaning and rules as central.

#### Chapter 14: Codification, Impersonation, and Cheating

Social games require codified rules, and problems arise when a person appears to play one game while following the rules of another. Szasz distinguishes mistakes, deliberate deception, and the subtler case of playing a game in the wrong code, and outlines three broad responses: submitting to the dominant game, withdrawing into private games, or compromising between them. This framework reinterprets so-called symptoms as mismatches between games.

#### Chapter 15: The Hysterical Game

Hysteria is presented as a mixed game, combining elements of coercion, self-help, and cooperation, in which bodily complaints and indirect communication are used to influence others. Lying, in this context, can function as a way of controlling what others can predict and therefore how they will act. Hysterical behavior can be understood as a coherent, if costly, social strategy, rather than as random or purely diseased behavior.

#### Chapter 16: Object Relationships

The final chapter reworks the psychoanalytic idea of object relationships in terms of internalizing, assimilating, and relinquishing objects, including rules and games as objects. A hysterical person suffers, in part, from difficulty in letting go of old objects and old games, and from continuing to play them when they no longer fit. Forgetting and relinquishing are active parts of learning new conduct, not mere loss.

Summary and Epilogue

Summary: Szasz restates hysteria as the central example through which the medical model of mental illness was built, and argues that reducing social and communicative problems to a single neurological or medical cause is a category error. A term that explains everything ends up explaining nothing.

Epilogue: There is no single game of life to master; there are many games, and modern life requires repeatedly learning and relearning them. Game-conflict arises when old rules are kept for new games, when old and new games are mixed, or when a person concludes that no game is worth playing. The closing challenge is to learn how to learn new social rules, rather than despairing over the loss of old ones.

Key terms

  • Mental illness: a metaphorical label, in Szasz's argument, not a literal disease of a physical mind.
  • Myth: not a falsehood in the sense of a lie, but a widely shared explanatory story that organizes perception and action.
  • Hysteria: the book's central example, reframed as bodily, nonverbal, and metaphorical communication.
  • Malingering: deliberate deception about illness, used as a contrast case that exposes classification problems.
  • Medical model: the framework that treats troubled conduct as if it were a bodily disease with a cause, diagnosis, and cure.
  • Sign and protolanguage: a sign as a physical intermediary between object and user, and early nonverbal signaling before full verbal language.
  • Discursive and nondiscursive language: sentence-like, explicit communication versus picture-like, bodily communication.
  • Game, rule, and role: social life as organized play, with shared rules and roles that a person learns and performs.
  • Rule-following and role-taking: conduct explained by learned rules and by taking the standpoint of others, rather than by inner drives alone.
  • Game-conflict: trouble arising from keeping old rules, mixing incompatible games, or rejecting all games.
  • Object relationship: an internalized pattern, including rules and games, that a person may need to relinquish to learn new conduct.
  • Panchreston: a term Szasz uses for an explanation applied to everything and therefore explaining nothing in particular.

What the book does NOT prove / leaves open

  • It is a conceptual and historical argument, not an empirical study; it does not prove, by data, that brain processes play no role in troubled behavior, nor does it test its game and sign models against alternatives.
  • It does not address, in the sampled passages, later neuroscience, genetics, or pharmacology, and it does not show that those fields have no explanatory value for any condition.
  • Its historical readings of Charcot, Breuer, and Freud are interpretations, not settled historical proof, and other historians could weigh the same material differently.
  • It does not resolve the ethical and practical questions it raises: when, if ever, involuntary care is justified, how to help a person in severe distress who cannot communicate clearly, or how to distinguish a communicative strategy from a condition that impairs a person's ability to choose.
  • It leaves open how a game-based account would handle severe psychosis, profound cognitive impairment, or conditions with clear bodily correlates, and it does not provide a tested therapeutic method in place of psychiatry.
  • The game and sign models themselves are analogies and frameworks; the book does not demonstrate, by measurement, that they predict behavior better than competing models.

Questions worth carrying forward

  • If mental illness is a metaphor, what language should be used for severe suffering that does not minimize it, and what practical difference would that language make?
  • How can Szasz's game model be tested, rather than only illustrated, for a specific pattern of conduct?
  • Where does Szasz's account succeed or fail for conditions with strong bodily or cognitive components, such as dementia or severe psychosis?
  • How should the ethics of helplessness be balanced with a duty to help people who cannot currently help themselves?
  • What would a non-medical, conduct-based practice actually do in a crisis, and who would decide when it is enough?
  • How do later findings in neuroscience and psychology change, qualify, or leave intact Szasz's conceptual critique?
  • Is the distinction between a disease and a problem in living as sharp as Szasz argues, or are there cases that fall between the two?
